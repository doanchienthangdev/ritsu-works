# Writing craft distilled — They Say / I Say (Gerald Graff & Cathy Birkenstein)

## Core thesis on writing
Good persuasive and explanatory writing is not the assertion of true, smart, or logical statements in a vacuum — it is *entering a conversation*. Every strong claim ("I say") must be framed as a response to something others say or might say ("they say"); without that motivating view, the reader sees *what* you claim but never *why* it matters. The book's gift is making the invisible "moves that matter" explicit as reusable templates — formulas that, far from stifling originality, free the writer to put original content into a proven shape, then outgrow it.

## Frameworks it gives /write
- **They Say / I Say** — open with the existing view, state your claim as a response to it, and keep returning to it. The master move; a reason-for-being for any piece.
- **Quotation Sandwich** — frame every quote/stat: who's speaking → the quote → what it means and why it matters here. Kills "hit-and-run" quotations.
- **Plant a Naysayer** — voice the strongest objection *before* the reader does, give it a fair hearing, then answer or concede. A preemptive strike that builds credibility.
- **So What? / Who Cares?** — name a group with a stake (who cares) and tie the claim to a consequence readers already value (so what). The stakes layer.
- **Yes / No / Okay-But** — declare your stance plainly and early so readers can place you, then unfold the nuance; "agree with a difference," disagree *with reasons*, or do both.
- **Return-Sentence Threading** — re-anchor the motivating "they say" at intervals through long pieces so the throughline never goes slack.
- **Translate the Jargon (High-Then-Low)** — say it once technically, then again in plain words; the everyday restatement drives the point home. The core EdTech explanation move.

## Craft principles
- **Write the voices of others into your text** — an argument that names no "they say" literally won't make sense.
- **Play the believing game** — summarize the other side so fairly a reader can't tell you disagree; biased summaries destroy credibility.
- **Avoid the closest-cliché syndrome** — summarize what the source *actually* said, not the nearest familiar slogan.
- **Pick the accurate signal verb** — "complains," "urges," "concedes," not flat "says"; the verb carries the stance.
- **Do the connecting work yourself** — every sentence reaches back and forward via transitions, pointing words, and repeated key terms; never make readers guess the link.
- **Use "I" when it clarifies** — evidence makes an argument strong, not pronoun-dodging; the first person is how you mark the shift from their view to yours.
- **Add a metatext** — stand beside your main text and tell readers how (and how not) to read you; even clear prose invites misreading.
- **Make the title do interpretive work** — a vague title signals the writer never figured out the point.
- **Let the form free the content** — templates shape *how* you say it; the *what* stays as original as you can make it.

## Signature lines
- "If your own argument doesn't identify the 'they say' that you're responding to, it probably won't make sense." — Graff & Birkenstein
- "The main text says something, the metatext tells readers how — and how not — to think about it." — Graff & Birkenstein
- "Creativity and originality lie not in the avoidance of established forms but in the imaginative use of them." — Graff & Birkenstein
- "Quotations are orphans: words that have been taken from their original contexts and that need to be integrated into their new textual surroundings." — Graff & Birkenstein

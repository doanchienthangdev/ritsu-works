# Communication Plan Matrix

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A structured planning tool that organises all internal and external communications across five dimensions: Message, Audience, Channel, Sender/Owner, and Timing — ensuring every stakeholder receives the right message through the right channel at the right time.

**Origin:** Project management and corporate communications practice; structured matrix format widely used in McKinsey change management engagements since the 1980s

## Why it works
Communication failures in strategy execution are almost always failures of planning, not intention. The matrix forces pre-commitment to who says what to whom, when, and how — eliminating the 'someone else will handle it' assumption.

## When to use
For any external marketing campaign, product launch communication, or internal change programme requiring coordinated multi-channel messaging.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the 3–5 overarching messages (using the Pyramid Principle: one headline claim + three supporting messages each) that form the narrative backbone of the communication programme.
2. List all audiences: segment by role (executive, manager, frontline), geography, and engagement level needed.
3. For each audience × message combination, select the optimal channel: Town Hall (high-impact, low-frequency), Team Briefing (medium-impact, medium-frequency), Intranet/Email (low-impact, high-frequency), Video (emotional, asynchronous), 1:1 Meeting (highest-impact, select audiences).
4. Assign a sender: match the sender's organisational authority and personal credibility to the audience's expectations. 'Why are we changing?' messages are most credible from the CEO; 'What does this mean for your day-to-day?' is most credible from the direct manager.
5. Build the timeline: map all communications onto a calendar, ensuring messages reinforce rather than contradict each other.
6. Track execution: update the Status column weekly. Flag any overdue communications as red; escalate to the Change Lead.

## Real-life example — General Electric (GE Work-Out programme, 1989)
Jack Welch's GE Work-Out programme — a cross-functional process improvement initiative rolled out across 250,000 employees — used a structured communication matrix to sequence the rollout. Key communications: CEO announcement letter and video (Inform, all employees, Month 1); Business Unit head town halls explaining local relevance (Engage, all BU employees, Month 2); Manager briefing kits with Q&A guides (Equip managers, Month 3); Employee participation invitations with WIIFM messaging (Engage frontline, Month 4). The matrix ensured that managers were briefed before employees, preventing the credibility gap of employees knowing more than their managers — a common failure in top-down communications.

**So what:** The matrix's sequencing value is as important as the content: managers must be informed before their teams — this is the most commonly violated rule in change communication.

## Pitfalls
- Over-communicating on some messages (saturation fatigue) while under-communicating on others (ambiguity): the matrix reveals imbalances when reviewed as a whole.
- Using only broadcast channels (email, intranet) without two-way engagement mechanisms: people need to ask questions and feel heard.
- Not assigning clear ownership per row: if everyone owns it, no one does — every communication event needs a named owner.

# Product Life Cycle (PLC)

*Category: growth · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A model describing the four stages a product passes through — Introduction, Growth, Maturity, and Decline — each with distinct marketing, pricing, and distribution strategies.

**Origin:** Theodore Levitt, Harvard Business Review, 1965 ('Exploit the Product Life Cycle'); rooted in biological lifecycle analogies applied to product markets

## Why it works
The competitive dynamics, customer base, and profit potential change dramatically across the four stages. Applying a Growth-stage strategy to a Mature product (e.g., heavy advertising spend) wastes capital; applying a Maturity strategy to a Growth-stage product (e.g., cost-cutting) leaves market share on the table.

## When to use
Portfolio strategy, pricing review, marketing investment allocation, and deciding when to launch next-generation products.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Identify which PLC stage each of your products/services is currently in: Introduction (low sales, negative profit, early adopters), Growth (rapidly rising sales, rising margins, mass market emerging), Maturity (peak sales, competitive pressure, margins stabilising), Decline (falling sales, shrinking market, margin erosion).
2. For each stage, define the appropriate commercial strategy:
3. Introduction: invest in awareness (PR, targeted digital), set skimming or penetration price, build selective distribution.
4. Growth: invest heavily in share capture (scaling advertising, expanding distribution), maintain or slightly raise prices.
5. Maturity: shift from share growth to margin defence (reduce advertising ROI threshold, optimise pricing, cut costs, extend product variants), consider market segmentation.
6. Decline: harvest (cut costs, maintain price, reduce distribution) or exit. Evaluate whether a 'rising phoenix' repositioning is viable.
7. Validate the stage classification with at least 3 years of sales, margin, and market growth data before making investment decisions.

## Real-life example — Kodak (film, 1990–2012) vs. Fujifilm (2000–2015)
Both Kodak and Fujifilm faced the same PLC inflection: photographic film entering terminal Decline from 2000 as digital photography grew. Kodak misread its film product as Mature rather than Declining, continuing to invest in film marketing and cut prices to defend share. It filed for bankruptcy in 2012. Fujifilm correctly diagnosed Decline in 2003 and applied a harvest-and-pivot strategy: it harvested film margins aggressively (minimal marketing investment) while simultaneously reinvesting cash into growth-stage adjacent technologies (cosmetics using film chemistry, medical imaging, optical systems). By 2015, Fujifilm's revenue was $22bn with film representing <1% — the same starting point as Kodak, but a different PLC stage decision.

**So what:** The PLC framework's value is forcing an honest stage classification — calling Decline 'Maturity' is the most expensive misclassification in commercial strategy.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the PLC as deterministic: companies can extend the Maturity stage through innovation and repositioning (e.g., Harley-Davidson in the 1990s).
- Applying the PLC at the wrong level of aggregation: the market may be in Decline while a niche sub-segment is in Growth.
- Using PLC as the sole basis for investment decisions without considering competitive dynamics and capabilities.

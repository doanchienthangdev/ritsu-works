# GE-McKinsey Matrix

*Category: portfolio · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A nine-cell portfolio matrix that plots business units or product lines on two dimensions: Industry Attractiveness (vertical) and Competitive Strength (horizontal), to guide investment decisions (invest/grow, hold/selectively invest, harvest/divest).

**Origin:** Developed jointly by General Electric and McKinsey & Company in the early 1970s to manage GE's portfolio of 150+ business units

## Why it works
Unlike the BCG 2×2 (which uses only two proxies — market growth and relative share), the GE-McKinsey matrix allows multiple weighted factors per axis, giving a more nuanced picture of where to allocate capital.

## When to use
Annual portfolio review, M&A target screening, business unit capital allocation, or when deciding which product lines to discontinue.

## Visual
`matrix-2x2`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List the business units or product lines to be assessed.
2. Define 5–7 Industry Attractiveness factors (e.g., market size, growth rate, profitability, competitive intensity, technological requirements, environmental impact) and assign weights summing to 100%.
3. Define 5–7 Competitive Strength factors (e.g., market share, brand strength, production capacity, profit margins, technological capability, quality) and assign weights.
4. Score each business unit 1–5 on each factor, then compute the weighted score for each axis.
5. Plot each unit as a circle on the 3×3 grid, with circle size proportional to the unit's revenue or market size.
6. Assign strategic recommendations: Invest/Grow (top-left zone), Selectively Invest/Hold (diagonal), Harvest/Divest (bottom-right zone).
7. Validate with the portfolio owners and finance team before making capital allocation decisions.

## Real-life example — Nestlé (2018 portfolio review)
Nestlé used portfolio analysis analogous to the GE-McKinsey framework to assess its 2,000+ brands. Units in high-attractiveness, high-strength zones (e.g., Nespresso, Purina, infant nutrition) received growth investment. Units in low-attractiveness zones (e.g., confectionery in mature markets, US ice cream) were divested — Nestlé sold its US candy business to Ferrero in 2018 and its ice cream unit for $4bn in 2019. This freed capital for high-growth segments, and Nestlé's operating margin improved from 15% to 17% by 2020.

**So what:** The matrix gave Nestlé a defensible, multi-factor rationale for divesting iconic brands — 'not strategic' beats 'we like it' in a board conversation.

## Pitfalls
- Subjective factor scoring without data: anchor scores to quantitative benchmarks wherever possible.
- Assigning equal weights to all factors: weight by strategic relevance — growth rate matters more in early-stage markets.
- Ignoring interdependencies: some 'harvest' units supply critical inputs to 'invest' units.

# Sales Distribution Channel Selection Matrix

*Category: growth · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A weighted scoring matrix that evaluates eight distribution models (Retail, Wholesale, Online, Own Physical Stores, Franchising, Strategic Partnerships, Multi-channel, Direct Sales Team) against five strategic criteria to select the optimal channel mix.

**Origin:** Synthesised from distribution strategy practice in McKinsey, Bain, and BCG; channel economics analysis formalised through Philip Kotler's distribution channel theory in Marketing Management (1967–2022 editions)

## Why it works
Channel selection is one of the highest-leverage commercial decisions: the right channel reaches the target customer at an acceptable cost; the wrong channel destroys margin and creates misaligned incentives. The matrix forces an explicit, weighted multi-criteria evaluation rather than defaulting to the channel the founding team knows best.

## When to use
When designing or redesigning the sales distribution model, entering a new geography, launching a new product category, or optimising channel mix.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the five evaluation criteria and their weights based on your strategic objectives: Margin Impact (20%), Customer Reach (25%), Control Level (20%), Investment Required (15%, inverse — lower investment = higher score), Strategic Fit with ICP (20%).
2. Score each of the eight channel models on each criterion (1–5 scale) using your market and channel economics data.
3. Calculate the weighted score for each channel model.
4. Identify the top 2–3 channel models by weighted score. Evaluate whether a multi-channel combination (not a single channel) is optimal.
5. Build a channel economics model: for each selected channel, model the unit economics (revenue per unit sold, channel margin/commission, logistics cost, net margin contribution).
6. Design the sales team structure aligned with the selected channels.
7. Document the channel selection rationale for the CCO review.

## Real-life example — Tesla (2008–2023 direct sales model)
Tesla scored the direct-to-consumer online channel highest on its distribution matrix: Margin Impact (4/5 — eliminates dealer margin of 5–10%), Customer Reach (3/5 — limited showroom network), Control Level (5/5 — full control of customer experience, pricing, and data), Investment Required (2/5 — high upfront in showrooms), Strategic Fit (5/5 — tech-savvy early adopters prefer digital purchase). Weighted score significantly outperformed franchise dealer model. Tesla's decision to refuse dealers was structurally driven by this matrix: dealer margin compression and loss of customer data were existential threats to a premium technology narrative. The direct model enabled $5,000–$8,000 lower ASP (Average Selling Price) without margin erosion.

**So what:** Tesla's channel decision was economics-first: eliminating dealer margin enabled either better pricing to customers or higher margins — the matrix made this visible.

## Pitfalls
- Selecting a channel because it is familiar rather than because it has the best economics: the matrix must be data-driven.
- Ignoring channel conflicts: if you sell through both retail and online direct at different price points, channel partners will object — manage this explicitly.
- Under-investing in channel management: selecting a channel without funding the partner management, training, and compliance infrastructure required to execute it well.

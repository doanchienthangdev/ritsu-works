# Pricing Strategy Selection Ladder

*Category: financial · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A structured decision framework for selecting the optimal pricing strategy from six canonical options — Penetration, Competitive, Premium/Skimming, Freemium, Value-Based, and Dynamic — based on company objectives, market context, and product lifecycle stage.

**Origin:** Synthesised from Hermann Simon's pricing strategy framework (Simon-Kucher & Partners) and Thomas T. Nagle's The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing (1987, now 6th edition)

## Why it works
Each pricing strategy implies a fundamentally different relationship between price, volume, and value delivery. Choosing the wrong strategy is irreversible in many markets: price anchoring means that customers remember the reference price and resist increases. The selection ladder structures the decision explicitly.

## When to use
When designing pricing for a new product, reviewing pricing strategy in response to competitive pressure, or building the pricing architecture for a new market entry.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Answer six diagnostic questions: (1) Is your product differentiated or commoditised? (2) Are customers price-sensitive or value-focused? (3) Do you need volume (share) or margin (profit) priority? (4) Is your product new to market or mature? (5) Do you have real-time demand data and the technology to act on it? (6) What is your customer's economic value received from the product?
2. Map your answers to the strategy ladder: all six questions point to one or two candidate strategies.
3. Run the Van Westendorp PSM survey to validate the price range that the selected strategy implies.
4. Design the price architecture: list price, tier structure, discounting policy, and price exception governance.
5. Define the discounting policy: maximum discount authorised at each sales level (rep, manager, VP, CCO). Discounts outside policy require CFO approval.
6. Define the anti-price-war protocol: if a competitor cuts price, your default response is to improve value (features, service) rather than match the cut — unless the volume threat is existential.

## Real-life example — Adobe (Creative Suite to Creative Cloud, 2013)
Adobe held a $1,299–$2,599 Skimming price strategy for boxed Creative Suite software. By 2013, piracy reached an estimated 80% of users in some regions (indicating clear demand at lower price points) and annual upgrades were slowing. Adobe's pricing shift: from Skimming to Freemium/Subscription (Freemium for individuals at $9.99/month; $49.99/month for full access). The strategy ladder analysis showed: product was fully differentiated (supports Premium), but piracy and stagnant growth indicated Freemium penetration was needed to expand the addressable market. The transition compressed initial revenue (FY2013 revenue fell 8%) but ultimately tripled Adobe's market cap from $12bn to $37bn+ by 2020 as recurring revenue quality was re-rated by markets.

**So what:** Adobe's pricing strategy change was diagnostic, not reactive — the piracy data was the signal that Skimming was pricing out 80% of potential demand.

## Pitfalls
- Defaulting to competitive pricing (matching the market) when you have a differentiated product — this is leaving value-based margin on the table.
- Implementing dynamic pricing without customer-trust guardrails: Amazon's dynamic pricing on commodities is accepted; dynamic pricing on necessity goods during a crisis is brand-damaging.
- Setting a penetration price without a defined path to raising it: penetration pricing creates a low-price anchor that is very difficult to move.

# Porter's Five Forces

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A framework for analysing the competitive forces that shape industry profitability: Threat of New Entrants, Supplier Power, Buyer Power, Threat of Substitutes, and Rivalry Among Competitors.

**Origin:** Michael E. Porter, Harvard Business School, 1979 (Competitive Strategy)

## Why it works
Industry profitability is determined by the balance of five structural forces. Strong forces compress margins; weak forces allow them to expand. By mapping each force, strategists identify where power lies and where to compete.

## When to use
At the start of any market-entry, competitive strategy, or pricing project to establish the structural context before making strategic choices.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the industry boundary precisely — too broad (e.g., 'consumer goods') or too narrow (e.g., 'organic oat biscuits') both distort the analysis.
2. For each of the five forces, list 3–5 specific factors driving it (e.g., for Buyer Power: buyer concentration, switching costs, availability of alternatives).
3. Rate each force High / Medium / Low based on the factors, citing at least one quantitative data point per force (e.g., market share of top-3 players for rivalry).
4. Synthesise: identify the two or three dominant forces and explain the mechanism through which they compress (or protect) margins.
5. Derive strategic implications: for each dominant force, state one strategic response (e.g., if Buyer Power is High, build switching costs through data integration).
6. Validate with the leadership team; update annually or after a major market event.

## Real-life example — Netflix (streaming, 2023)
Netflix's Five Forces analysis reveals: Buyer Power is High (subscribers can cancel monthly; alternatives — Disney+, Prime — are one click away); Rivalry is High (7+ well-funded streaming services compete on content spend); Threat of Substitutes is High (gaming, social media, linear TV); Supplier Power is Medium-High (major studios license at rising rates but Netflix's own content reduces dependency); Threat of New Entrants is Low-Medium (content production costs create a barrier). Dominant forces are Buyer Power and Rivalry, driving Netflix to invest $17bn in original content annually to reduce substitutability and increase perceived switching costs through exclusive franchises.

**So what:** Two dominant forces (Buyer Power, Rivalry) explain Netflix's $17bn content spend — it is a structural response, not a whim.

## Pitfalls
- Defining the industry too broadly dilutes insight — a 'beverages' analysis misses that energy drinks face different forces than mineral water.
- Treating the framework as static: forces shift (e.g., digital distribution transformed Buyer Power in music in 2003). Re-run annually.
- Confusing industry profitability with company profitability — a company can outperform in a structurally unattractive industry through differentiation.

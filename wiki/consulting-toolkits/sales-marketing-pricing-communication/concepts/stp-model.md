# STP Model (Segmentation, Targeting & Positioning)

*Category: growth · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A three-stage marketing framework: Segment the market into homogeneous groups, Target the most attractive segments, and Position the offering distinctively in the minds of the target customer.

**Origin:** Philip Kotler, Kellogg School of Management; widely taught from the 1960s onwards and codified in Marketing Management (1967, now 16th edition)

## Why it works
Not all customers are alike. STP prevents the fatal error of treating a heterogeneous market as a single entity, which leads to messaging that resonates with no one. By forcing explicit targeting before positioning, it ensures marketing spend is concentrated where it has the highest return.

## When to use
When launching a new product, entering a new market, repositioning a brand, or redesigning a go-to-market model.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define segmentation variables: choose 2–3 dimensions that genuinely drive different purchase behaviours (e.g., for B2B software: company size + industry vertical + digital maturity; for consumer goods: age + lifestyle + frequency of use).
2. Identify 4–8 distinct segments. Each must be Measurable (data exists), Substantial (large enough to be profitable), Accessible (you can reach them), and Differentiable (they respond differently).
3. Score each segment on: market size, growth rate, competitive intensity, fit with your capabilities, and profitability potential. Select 1–3 priority target segments.
4. For each target segment, write the positioning statement using Geoffrey Moore's template: 'For [target customer], who [has this need or opportunity], [product name] is a [product category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [primary alternative], our product [primary differentiation].'
5. Validate the positioning with 5–10 target customers through interview or survey before finalising.
6. Ensure the positioning statement drives all subsequent decisions: messaging, channel, price point, and product roadmap.

## Real-life example — Lululemon Athletica
Lululemon's STP: Segmentation by lifestyle and values (not just demographics) produced a segment: 'Super Girls' — educated, high-income, health-conscious women aged 25–35 in urban markets. Targeting: this segment was small (~5% of women's apparel shoppers at the time) but had high willingness to pay and strong community influence. Positioning: 'For mindful, active women, Lululemon is the premium technical athletic apparel that integrates performance with lifestyle aesthetics. Unlike Nike (mass athletic) or Athleta (outdoor), Lululemon is the community brand for the yoga-to-street lifestyle.' This positioning drove $2bn+ revenue before any significant advertising spend.

**So what:** Lululemon's growth came from narrow, precise targeting (not broad women's apparel) and a lifestyle positioning that made premium pricing feel rational.

## Pitfalls
- Segmenting on demographics alone (age, gender) without linking to purchase behaviour — demographics predict purchase less reliably than lifestyle or situation.
- Targeting too many segments simultaneously: focus beats breadth in early-stage commercial strategy.
- Writing a positioning statement that describes features rather than benefits — the statement must resonate emotionally, not just inform.

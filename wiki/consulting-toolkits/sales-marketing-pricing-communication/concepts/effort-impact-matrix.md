# Effort-Impact Matrix (Prioritisation Matrix)

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A 2×2 decision-support tool that plots potential initiatives on two axes — Implementation Effort (Low to High) and Strategic Impact (Low to High) — to classify them into four actionable quadrants: Quick Wins, Strategic Bets, Fill-Ins, and Hard Slogs.

**Origin:** Widely attributed to management consulting practice; popularised in McKinsey and BCG problem-solving curricula from the 1980s; no single inventor

## Why it works
Resources are finite. The matrix forces an explicit trade-off between impact and feasibility, preventing organisations from either cherry-picking only easy tasks (low impact) or undertaking only ambitious projects (high effort, long time-to-value).

## When to use
When prioritising a portfolio of initiatives after ideation, building a business roadmap, or making a go/no-go decision under budget constraints.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List all candidate initiatives (typically 15–30 at this stage in a strategy process).
2. Define scoring criteria for Impact: e.g., revenue uplift potential, strategic alignment score, market share gain, risk mitigation value. Score each initiative 1–5.
3. Define scoring criteria for Effort: e.g., time to implement (months), investment required ($), cross-functional dependencies, change management complexity. Score each initiative 1–5 (inverse: high effort = high score).
4. Plot each initiative on the 2×2 grid using the two scores as coordinates.
5. Classify: Quick Wins (top-left) → immediate action plan; Strategic Bets (top-right) → detailed business case and phased implementation; Fill-Ins (bottom-left) → assign to teams as capacity permits; Hard Slogs (bottom-right) → drop or defer.
6. Validate the grid with the leadership team: challenge the effort and impact scores with data before finalising.

## Real-life example — Unilever (Sustainable Living Plan initiative portfolio, 2010)
When Unilever launched its Sustainable Living Plan, it faced a portfolio of 50+ potential initiatives across supply chain, product formulation, packaging, and consumer communication. Using an impact-effort analysis (analogous to the Effort-Impact Matrix), Unilever classified: Quick Wins included removing non-recyclable packaging from top 10 SKUs (high media impact, low formulation change needed). Strategic Bets included transitioning to 100% sustainably sourced palm oil (high impact but required 10-year supplier development programme). Hard Slogs like reformulating all detergents for cold-water use were deprioritised in the first wave. This sequencing allowed Unilever to demonstrate early wins while investing in long-horizon strategic bets, protecting CEO Paul Polman's programme from early scepticism.

**So what:** The matrix's primary value is sequencing, not just classification — Quick Wins build political capital to fund Strategic Bets.

## Pitfalls
- Scoring impact without defining criteria: 'high impact' means different things to different functions — agree the scoring rubric before the workshop.
- Gravitating to Quick Wins at the expense of Strategic Bets: programmes that only do Quick Wins never achieve transformational change.
- Not revisiting the matrix after 6 months: effort scores change as capabilities build and impact scores change as the competitive environment shifts.

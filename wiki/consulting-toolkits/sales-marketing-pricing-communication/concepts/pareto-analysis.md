# Pareto Analysis (80/20 Rule)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
An analytical technique that identifies the 20% of causes (customers, products, channels, defects) that drive 80% of effects (revenue, profit, cost, problems), enabling focused resource allocation.

**Origin:** Vilfredo Pareto, Italian economist, 1896 (observed that 80% of Italy's land was owned by 20% of the population); applied to quality management by Joseph Juran in the 1940s

## Why it works
In most complex systems, a small number of factors drive the majority of outcomes. By identifying and focusing on that small number, organisations can achieve disproportionate results with limited resources.

## When to use
Sales analysis, customer segmentation, cost reduction prioritisation, quality improvement, and any resource-allocation decision.

## Visual
`chart`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the effect to analyse (e.g., total revenue, total cost, number of support tickets) and the categories to segment by (e.g., customers, products, issue types).
2. Gather data and sort categories in descending order of the effect variable.
3. Calculate cumulative % of the total as you add categories from highest to lowest.
4. Plot the Pareto chart: bars for the volume per category, line for cumulative %.
5. Identify the 'vital few': the categories at the 80% cumulative mark. These are the highest-leverage focus areas.
6. Derive actionable concentration strategy: for sales, focus account management on the top 20% of customers; for cost reduction, target the top 3 cost categories; for quality improvement, address the top 2–3 defect types.

## Real-life example — American Express (corporate card portfolio, 2005)
American Express applied Pareto analysis to its corporate card customer base and found that 18% of corporate clients generated 79% of revenue (consistent with the 80/20 pattern). This insight drove the creation of the dedicated 'Centurion' relationship management team focused exclusively on the top-tier clients. The company invested premium service resources (dedicated account managers, tailored rewards, private event access) in the top 18% rather than distributing resources uniformly across all clients. The result was a 22% retention improvement in the top tier and a 14% increase in per-client spend.

**So what:** Pareto analysis drove a resource reallocation decision: not 'serve everyone better' but 'invest disproportionately in the 18% who drive 79% of revenue'.

## Pitfalls
- Assuming the 80/20 split is exact: in practice it is often 85/15 or 70/30 — analyse the actual data.
- Neglecting the long tail entirely: occasionally, small customers become large ones; build a watch-list for fast-growing 'long-tail' accounts.
- Applying Pareto only to revenue and ignoring profitability: some top-revenue customers may be loss-making at the margin level.

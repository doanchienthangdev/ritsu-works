# Blue Ocean Strategy

*Category: growth · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A strategy framework that seeks to create uncontested market space ('blue oceans') by simultaneously pursuing differentiation and low cost, rather than competing in overcrowded existing markets ('red oceans').

**Origin:** W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, INSEAD, 2004 (published as Blue Ocean Strategy, Harvard Business School Press, 2005)

## Why it works
In red oceans, companies fight over the same demand, compressing margins. Blue Ocean Strategy uses the Four Actions Framework (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create — the ERRC grid) and the Strategy Canvas to reconstruct industry boundaries and create new demand.

## When to use
When competitive intensity is severe and margins are declining, when entering a new market category, or when seeking growth from non-customers rather than winning share from competitors.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Draw the Strategy Canvas: list the key competing factors in your industry on the x-axis (typically 6–12 factors). Plot your current offering and top 2–3 competitors on the y-axis (Low to High performance).
2. Apply the ERRC Grid: for each competing factor, ask — should we Eliminate it (it adds cost but customers don't value it)? Reduce it below industry standard? Raise it above industry standard? Create a factor the industry has never offered?
3. Ensure your ERRC grid includes at least one Create and one Eliminate — otherwise you are only incrementally improving a red-ocean strategy.
4. Draw the new Strategy Canvas with your proposed Blue Ocean offering. Validate that it diverges meaningfully from existing curves.
5. Identify the non-customer groups who are not yet buying: why not? Use the 'Three Tiers of Non-Customers' to find the largest demand pool.
6. Test the new value curve with target non-customers before committing capital.

## Real-life example — Cirque du Soleil
The traditional circus competed on: star performers, animal shows, aisle concessions, multiple show arenas, and fun/humour. Cirque eliminated animals (high cost, low appeal to adults) and star performers (high cost). It reduced thrills and danger, multiple show arenas, and aisle sales. It raised the uniqueness of the venue and refined environment. It created a story/theme, artistic music and dance, and multiple productions — borrowing from theatre. The result: a new Strategy Canvas that attracted adult theatre-goers who would never attend a traditional circus, creating a $1bn business in a category previously in structural decline.

**So what:** Blue Ocean is not about doing more — it is about doing deliberately less in some areas to fund entirely new value dimensions.

## Pitfalls
- Only adding features (Raise/Create) without Eliminating/Reducing — this raises cost without creating a true blue ocean.
- Confusing product innovation with strategic innovation: Blue Ocean requires restructuring the value-cost frontier, not just a new feature.
- Not testing the new curve with non-customers — most blue ocean failures are demand assumptions that were never validated.

# Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM)

*Category: financial · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A survey-based price sensitivity analysis tool that identifies the acceptable price range for a product by asking four questions about price perceptions: too cheap, cheap/good value, expensive/worth considering, and too expensive.

**Origin:** Peter H. Van Westendorp, Dutch economist, presented to the ESOMAR conference in 1976

## Why it works
Price sensitivity is not captured by a single price point — it exists as a range. The PSM uses four cumulative frequency curves to define: the Acceptable Price Range (the band between 'too cheap' and 'too expensive'), the Optimal Price Point (OPP — where 'too cheap' and 'too expensive' are equidistant), and the Acceptable Price Point (where 'not expensive' and 'not cheap' cross).

## When to use
When launching a new product, setting a price for the first time, testing a price change, or building the evidence base for a value-based pricing move.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Design the survey: select a representative sample of your target segment (minimum 100 respondents for statistical validity).
2. Ask all four questions for a single described product: (1) At what price would this product be so cheap you'd question its quality? (2) At what price would it be a bargain/good value? (3) At what price would it be getting expensive but still worth considering? (4) At what price would it be so expensive you would not buy it?
3. Plot four cumulative frequency curves on the same chart: reverse the 'too cheap' and 'bargain' curves so they run left-to-right ascending.
4. Identify the four key intersections: the Acceptable Price Range lower bound (where 'too cheap' and 'too expensive' first separate), the IPP (where 'bargain' and 'expensive' cross), the OPP (where 'too cheap' and 'too expensive' cross), and the upper bound.
5. Use the Acceptable Price Range as the safe pricing band; the OPP as the psychologically optimal price; the IPP as the market-typical price.
6. Validate findings with a conjoint analysis or price experiment before finalising.

## Real-life example — Spotify (premium tier pricing research, ~2009–2011)
Spotify used price sensitivity research (methodology consistent with PSM principles) to set its €9.99/$9.99 monthly premium price. Research showed the acceptable range for an ad-free music streaming subscription was approximately €5–€15, with the optimal price point around €10 — below the €14.99–€18.99 CD equivalent but above the €3–€5 level consumers associated with lower-quality services. Setting at €9.99 (just below the €10 psychological threshold) placed Spotify within the acceptable range for the broadest segment of music listeners, driving 10m+ paid subscribers within 2 years of the premium launch.

**So what:** PSM identified that €9.99 was not arbitrary — it sat at the intersection of the acceptable range and the psychological rounding effect of the €10 threshold.

## Pitfalls
- Using hypothetical price questions without describing the product clearly — respondents cannot give meaningful WTP data without a concrete product description.
- Treating the OPP as the revenue-maximising price — it is the psychologically neutral price, not the profit-maximising one; use conjoint for revenue optimisation.
- Applying PSM to B2B enterprise pricing where purchase is driven by procurement committees, not individual WTP — PSM is most reliable for consumer and SMB pricing.

# Pricing Maturity Model

*Category: financial · Toolkit: Sales, Marketing, Pricing & Communication*

## What it is
A five-level model describing an organisation's pricing sophistication, from Level 1 (cost-plus, reactive) to Level 5 (dynamic, value-based, AI-supported), used to diagnose current capability and set a target improvement trajectory.

**Origin:** Synthesised from McKinsey's pricing practice literature and the work of pricing experts including Hermann Simon (Simon-Kucher & Partners) and Thomas Nagle (Strategy & Tactics of Pricing)

## Why it works
Pricing capability is a major lever for margin improvement — McKinsey estimates that a 1% price improvement generates a 6–8% EBITDA improvement for a typical company. The maturity model provides a structured pathway to develop pricing capability incrementally rather than attempting a step-change.

## When to use
At the start of a pricing transformation initiative, when diagnosing margin erosion, or when designing a pricing excellence programme.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Conduct a pricing maturity diagnostic: assess your current state across 5 dimensions — pricing strategy, pricing process, pricing tools/data, pricing organisation, and pricing culture.
2. Score each dimension 1–5 and identify the current average maturity level.
3. Define the target maturity level (typically 1–2 levels above current, achievable in 18–24 months).
4. For each dimension gap, identify the specific capability to build (e.g., from Level 1 to Level 3: introduce customer WTP surveys, build a price-by-segment model, train the sales force on value selling).
5. Sequence the capability build as a programme: foundation (data and process), then advanced (value-based architecture), then optimisation (dynamic pricing).
6. Define 3–4 KPIs to measure progress: price realisation rate, margin by segment, discounting rate, and win/loss ratio.

## Real-life example — Michelin (tyre manufacturer, 2000s transition to value-based pricing)
Michelin operated at Level 1–2 (cost-plus and competitive) through the 1990s. In the early 2000s, facing Asian low-cost competition, Michelin moved to Level 4 by reframing its product as a 'cost per kilometre' solution rather than a 'tyre'. For fleet operators (trucks, buses), Michelin demonstrated that premium tyres lasted 20% longer, reducing total ownership cost even at a 30% price premium. By selling on Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) rather than unit price, Michelin achieved 12–18% higher average prices than low-cost competitors while growing its fleet segment. This is textbook Level 4 value-based pricing.

**So what:** Moving from Level 2 to Level 4 required a narrative shift (cost-per-km vs. per-unit price), not just a price change — pricing maturity is as much a capability and culture shift as a numbers exercise.

## Pitfalls
- Jumping to Level 5 (dynamic pricing) without the data infrastructure and pricing culture to support it — failed dynamic pricing damages customer trust.
- Treating the maturity model as a destination rather than a journey: even Level 5 companies regress without ongoing investment.
- Focusing only on the pricing number without building the sales force capability to defend the price (value selling training is essential).

# Commitment Curve

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Lean Six Sigma*

## What it is
A diagnostic and planning tool that maps each key stakeholder's current psychological position on a progression from Awareness through Understanding, Buy-in, Commitment, to Advocacy — enabling the project team to design targeted interventions that move resistors toward commitment before a solution is implemented at scale.

**Origin:** Derived from the Prosci ADKAR change management model (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement), developed by Jeff Hiatt at Prosci in the 1990s based on research with over 1,000 organisations. The Commitment Curve representation was popularised in change management training programs in the 2000s. In Lean Six Sigma, it was incorporated as a standard Improve-phase tool to address the gap between technically sound solutions and their actual adoption.

## Why it works
Improvement projects fail most often not because the technical solution is wrong but because the people implementing it have not made the psychological transition from their old way of working. A Lean Six Sigma project can deliver a statistically significant CTQ improvement in the pilot and then see that improvement erode within 90 days as operators revert to old habits. The Commitment Curve makes this psychological transition visible and manageable: it gives the project team a language and a planning structure for change management, converting what would otherwise be a vague sense that 'people aren't bought in' into specific, actionable interventions for specific stakeholders.

## When to use
Phase IV (Improve) during solution implementation planning and ongoing monitoring. Particularly critical when the solution requires significant behaviour change from operators, when there are known political obstacles to the improvement, or when a previous improvement attempt in the same area failed despite a technically sound solution.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. List all key stakeholders identified in the Phase I (Define) stakeholder analysis. Include process operators, team leaders, department heads, and anyone whose behaviour must change for the solution to hold.
2. 2. For each stakeholder, assess their current commitment level: Awareness (have they been told about the change?), Understanding (have they been shown what it involves?), Buy-in (have they accepted its appropriateness?), Commitment (are they actively supporting it?), Advocacy (are they promoting it?). Base assessments on direct conversations or observable behaviour — not assumptions.
3. 3. Plot each stakeholder as a dot on the Commitment Curve, labelled with their name and role.
4. 4. Identify all stakeholders currently below Buy-in. For each, diagnose what is blocking progression: Is it lack of information (needs Understanding)? Is it a specific concern or fear (needs a targeted conversation or demonstration)? Is it a misaligned incentive (needs a structural change)?
5. 5. Design a specific intervention for each stakeholder below Buy-in: a one-on-one conversation, a demonstration of the solution, involvement in the pilot design, or recognition of their expertise in the solution development.
6. 6. Identify your Advocates (Commitment and above). Engage them explicitly as change agents: ask them to speak positively about the change to peers, to model the new behaviour, and to help bring specific resistors toward Buy-in.
7. 7. Reassess stakeholder positions weekly during the first month of implementation. Commitment levels change — early resistors can become advocates once they see the change working; early supporters can become resistors if unexpected problems arise.
8. 8. Do not implement at scale until all high-power/high-interest stakeholders are at Buy-in or above. A single high-power resistor can derail a technically perfect solution.

## Real-life example — Johnson & Johnson (Operations)
During a Lean Six Sigma project at a Johnson & Johnson pharmaceutical manufacturing facility in the mid-2000s, the project team identified a solution that would reduce batch processing time by 35% by changing the sequence of two production steps. The solution was technically validated in a 6-week pilot. However, the Commitment Curve assessment in Week 8 revealed that the senior process validation chemist — a high-power/high-interest stakeholder — was at 'Understanding' rather than 'Buy-in.' Her concern was that resequencing the steps might affect the batch validation status required by FDA regulations. The project team engaged her directly, involved her in the regulatory impact assessment, and brought in the regulatory affairs team to co-develop the validation protocol. After two weeks, she moved to 'Commitment' and became an advocate — she later presented the project at the American Association of Pharmaceutical Scientists conference as a model for regulatory-compliant Lean implementation.

**So what:** The Commitment Curve's value is that it makes the specific human obstacle visible and named. A vague sense that 'the validation chemist isn't on board' is much less actionable than 'she is at Understanding, her concern is regulatory impact, and the specific intervention is regulatory affairs engagement.' Named obstacles can be solved; vague resistance cannot.

## Pitfalls
- Assessing commitment levels based on what stakeholders say in group meetings rather than in private conversations — people will publicly appear supportive while privately planning to revert. One-on-one conversations reveal true commitment level.
- Assuming that people who received an email announcement are at 'Awareness' — receiving a message is not the same as registering its significance. Awareness requires confirmation that the person understands a change is coming that affects them.
- Treating the Commitment Curve as a one-time assessment rather than a weekly monitoring tool — commitment levels change throughout implementation and must be tracked dynamically.
- Focusing all change management effort on the most resistant stakeholders and neglecting the advocates — advocates need engagement and recognition too, or they lose energy and revert to passive support.

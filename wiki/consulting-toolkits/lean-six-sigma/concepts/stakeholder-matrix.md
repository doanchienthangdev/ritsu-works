# Stakeholder Power-Interest Matrix

*Category: stakeholder-management · Toolkit: Lean Six Sigma*

## What it is
A 2×2 matrix that plots key stakeholders on two axes — Power (their ability to influence the project's success or failure) and Interest (their level of concern with the project's outcome) — generating four quadrants that prescribe different engagement strategies and enabling the project team to allocate relationship management effort in proportion to stakeholder impact.

**Origin:** The Power/Interest matrix was developed by Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann in their 1998 book 'Making Strategy: The Journey of Strategic Management' at Strathclyde Business School. It was subsequently popularised in project management training and became a standard tool in PMBOK (Project Management Body of Knowledge) and in Lean Six Sigma Define-phase curricula.

## Why it works
Not all stakeholders require the same level of engagement. Spreading engagement effort equally across all stakeholders is inefficient and causes teams to under-invest in managing the relationships that most affect project outcomes. The Power/Interest matrix segments stakeholders into four groups with distinct engagement requirements: high-power/high-interest stakeholders can veto the project or drive its success (manage closely); high-power/low-interest stakeholders have the authority to intervene but currently choose not to (keep satisfied); low-power/high-interest stakeholders can be vocal supporters or detractors but lack the authority to unilaterally determine outcome (keep informed); low-power/low-interest stakeholders require minimal engagement (monitor).

## When to use
Phase I (Define) to build the initial stakeholder engagement plan. Updated monthly throughout the project. Combined with the Commitment Curve in Phase IV (Improve) to track whether Manage Closely stakeholders are progressing toward Buy-in before implementation at scale.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. In a cross-functional team session, list all individuals and groups who have a stake in the project outcome: process operators, supervisors, department heads, customers (internal and external), union representatives, IT teams, Finance, HR, and any regulatory bodies.
2. 2. For each stakeholder, assess POWER: what is their ability to influence the project's success or failure? Consider: Can they allocate resources? Can they veto the solution? Can they instruct team members? Can they withhold critical data or cooperation? Score High or Low.
3. 3. For each stakeholder, assess INTEREST: how concerned are they with the project outcome? Consider: Will the solution significantly change their workflow? Do they have a declared position on the problem? Have they raised the issue before? Score High or Low.
4. 4. Plot each stakeholder on the 2×2 matrix as a named dot. If a stakeholder is borderline, discuss as a team — the discussion itself surfaces valuable intelligence about their position.
5. 5. For each quadrant, define the engagement approach: Manage Closely (top right) → weekly briefings, involvement in key decisions, direct access to sponsor; Keep Satisfied (top left) → monthly updates, involve in milestone reviews but not daily work; Keep Informed (bottom right) → regular email updates, town hall sessions, respond to questions within 48 hours; Monitor (bottom left) → standard project communications only.
6. 6. For each Manage Closely stakeholder, identify any specific concerns or objectives they have and document them in the engagement plan. A stakeholder's concern is a hypothesis about how the project might affect them negatively — address each concern directly and explicitly.
7. 7. Identify any potential Advocates (Manage Closely stakeholders who are highly positive) and Resistors (Manage Closely or Keep Satisfied stakeholders who are opposed). Advocates need nurturing; Resistors need specific mitigation strategies before implementation.
8. 8. Update the matrix monthly throughout the project. Major scope changes, organisation restructuring, or unexpected pilot results can shift stakeholders between quadrants — an early Monitor who becomes a new department head is suddenly a Manage Closely stakeholder.

## Real-life example — Caterpillar Inc. (Lean Six Sigma program office)
During a Lean Six Sigma project to reduce inventory carrying costs at a Caterpillar parts distribution center, the project team built a Stakeholder Power-Interest Matrix in Week 1. The matrix identified the Warehouse Manager as High Power/High Interest (Manage Closely) — she had authority over warehouse operations and was concerned that the solution might displace workers. The VP of Supply Chain was High Power/Low Interest (Keep Satisfied) — he cared about the financial outcome but not the implementation details. The union shop steward was Low Power/High Interest (Keep Informed) — no veto authority but highly vocal and capable of creating workforce morale problems. The project team designed distinct engagement strategies: weekly one-on-ones with the Warehouse Manager, monthly summary emails to the VP, and a monthly open Q&A session with the union shop steward. The union engagement prevented what would have been a significant morale issue when 5S changes required moving operators' personal storage lockers.

**So what:** Stakeholder matrices save projects not in obvious situations but in edge cases — the stakeholders who are initially Low Interest but have the power to block if their interest is triggered. Proactive management of High Power/Low Interest stakeholders before they become concerned is cheaper than reactive damage control after they become blockers.

## Pitfalls
- Treating Power and Interest as fixed — stakeholders move between quadrants as the project progresses and as organisational circumstances change. The matrix must be a living document updated monthly.
- Ignoring Low Power/High Interest stakeholders — shop-floor workers, vocal team members, and informal opinion leaders lack formal authority but can create significant informal resistance or momentum. Their interest makes them influential beyond their position.
- Not distinguishing between formal power (job title) and informal power (organisational credibility, technical expertise, personal relationships with executives) — a senior expert with no direct line authority can have significant informal power.
- Using the matrix only in Define and not revisiting it in Improve — the most critical engagement risk is in the Improve phase when the solution is being implemented and stakeholder behaviour must change. The matrix must be active throughout implementation.

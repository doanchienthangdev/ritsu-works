# DOWNTIME — The 8 Lean Wastes

*Category: waste-identification · Toolkit: Lean Six Sigma*

## What it is
A structured vocabulary of eight types of non-value-adding activity (Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilised talent, Transportation, Inventory, Motion, Extra-processing) that provides a comprehensive framework for identifying all process waste during observation walks and value stream mapping.

**Origin:** The concept of waste (muda) was developed by Taiichi Ohno and Shigeo Shingo at Toyota in the 1950s–70s, originally identifying seven types. The eighth waste (Non-utilised talent) was added by Western Lean practitioners in the 1990s. The DOWNTIME mnemonic was created by Lean educators to make the eight wastes memorable.

## Why it works
In any process, activities can be classified as: (1) Value-adding — transforms the product or service in a way the customer would pay for; (2) Non-value-adding but necessary — regulatory compliance, safety checks; (3) Pure waste — consumes resources without adding value or being necessary. The 8 wastes provide a systematic classification of type 3 activities. Eliminating pure waste requires no investment in technology or headcount — it requires redesigning how work flows.

## When to use
During Phase I (Define) for scope framing and Phase II (Measure) during process observation. Value Stream Mapping is the primary vehicle; the DOWNTIME framework is the classification system applied during the walk.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Before the observation walk, brief the team on all 8 waste types with concrete sector-specific examples so they recognise waste in the form it takes in your environment.
2. 2. Walk the process physically (or virtually for digital processes) following the flow of a single unit of work (a form, a product, a patient). Time-stamp each step.
3. 3. At each step, classify the time spent as: Value-Adding (VA), Necessary Non-Value-Adding (NNVA), or Waste (any of the 8 types). Record on a Time-Observation form.
4. 4. For digital or knowledge processes, use process mining tool output or screen recording rather than physical observation — the waste categories still apply.
5. 5. Tally the percentage of total cycle time consumed by each waste type. Create a Pareto chart of waste categories by time or cost.
6. 6. For the top 3 waste categories, brainstorm root causes — this feeds directly into the Fishbone Diagram in Phase II.
7. 7. For physical environments, conduct a 5S assessment in parallel with the waste walk — disorganised workspaces are a primary driver of Motion and Extra-processing waste.
8. 8. Calculate the value-add ratio: VA time divided by Total cycle time. In manufacturing, a ratio of 5–15% is typical before Lean; the goal is 40% or more after improvement.

## Real-life example — Virginia Mason Medical Center (Seattle, Washington)
Virginia Mason Medical Center applied the Toyota Production System — which they branded the Virginia Mason Production System — starting in 2002. A waste walk in the hospital's laboratory revealed that technicians walked an average of 5 miles per day to retrieve samples, equipment, and results — a pure Motion waste. Instruments that should be adjacent were located in different rooms due to historical space allocation rather than workflow logic. After redesigning the laboratory layout to collocate instruments, supplies, and work areas by workflow rather than department, technician walking distance dropped to 0.5 miles per day. Processing time per test decreased by 53% and lab error rates dropped by 67%.

**So what:** Motion waste in healthcare — as in manufacturing and financial services — is largely invisible to managers because workers compensate for it without complaint. A structured waste walk with the DOWNTIME framework makes it quantifiable and undeniable.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing Necessary Non-Value-Adding activity with pure waste — regulatory compliance steps are NNVA, not waste. Attempting to eliminate NNVA creates compliance risk.
- Conducting the waste walk without the process operators present — operators see the waste every day and can explain why it occurs; observers alone miss the context.
- Focusing on the most visible waste type (often Motion) and ignoring higher-impact types (often Waiting or Extra-processing) — always let the Pareto chart drive prioritisation.
- Treating waste identification as a blame exercise — waste exists because the system was designed that way, not because operators are lazy. A blame culture prevents honest disclosure of waste.

# Crazy 8s

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Lean Six Sigma*

## What it is
A structured time-pressured sketching exercise in which each participant draws or writes 8 rough solution ideas in 8 minutes (one per panel on a folded sheet) — forcing rapid divergence beyond the obvious first ideas and generating high-volume, low-judgment ideation that serves as raw material for the mind map and decision matrix.

**Origin:** Developed by Jake Knapp at Google Ventures as part of the Google Design Sprint methodology, published in 'Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days' (Simon & Schuster, 2016). Adapted for use in Lean Six Sigma ideation phases as an alternative to conventional brainstorming, which suffers from anchoring bias, social inhibition, and the dominance of vocal participants.

## Why it works
Conventional brainstorming has well-documented structural flaws: participants anchor on the first idea spoken, social dynamics suppress unconventional ideas, and the linear format encourages incremental rather than lateral thinking. Crazy 8s addresses all three: the time pressure (1 minute per idea) forces participants to exhaust their obvious ideas quickly and reach less conventional ones; the individual sketching format removes social inhibition; and the requirement to generate 8 ideas forces quantity over quality, which empirically produces more diverse and creative solution options when the ideas are later filtered through a decision matrix.

## When to use
Phase IV (Improve), Step 2 (Generate potential solutions), combined with HMW question framing and followed by mind mapping. Particularly valuable when: the team has been working on the same process for years and tends to propose only incremental variations of existing approaches; or when a previous improvement attempt failed and the team needs to think differently about the solution space.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Before the session, brief participants on the confirmed root causes (from Analyze) and the ideation constraints (budget, technology platform, regulatory boundaries). Ideas generated outside these constraints are wasted effort.
2. 2. Have each participant fold an A4 sheet into 8 equal panels (fold in half three times). Give each participant a pen. No sticky notes, no team discussion yet — this is an individual activity.
3. 3. Set a visible countdown timer for 8 minutes. Instruct participants: 'In each panel, sketch or write one rough idea for how to address the root causes. You have 1 minute per panel. The goal is quantity — generate 8 different ideas, not one polished idea drawn 8 times.'
4. 4. Start the timer. Maintain silence during the 8 minutes. If participants freeze, prompt them privately: 'What would you do if the budget constraint did not exist?' or 'What would the opposite of our current process look like?' — lateral prompts unlock ideas blocked by anchoring.
5. 5. After 8 minutes, each participant shares their 8 panels briefly (30 seconds per panel — no debate yet). Capture all ideas on sticky notes on the mind map.
6. 6. After all participants have shared, allow a brief (10-minute) discussion to clarify any ideas that were unclear. Then move directly to the mind map organisation — do not evaluate or eliminate ideas at this stage.
7. 7. Combine Crazy 8s with the HMW (How Might We) question framing: run HMW questions first (5 minutes), then Crazy 8s (8 minutes). The HMW questions seed the idea space; Crazy 8s generates the volume.
8. 8. Note: in a group of 6 participants, Crazy 8s generates 48 ideas in 8 minutes. With HMW priming, the diversity of ideas is significantly higher than from a conventional brainstorm of equivalent duration.

## Real-life example — Slack Technologies (Process Design)
Jake Knapp's original Crazy 8s methodology was used at multiple technology companies including the team that designed the Slack notification system. In one sprint, the team used Crazy 8s to generate ideas for reducing notification fatigue — a problem caused by too many low-priority notifications competing with high-priority ones. In 8 minutes, each of 5 participants generated 8 ideas (40 ideas total). Three ideas that appeared on multiple participants' sheets (independent convergence on similar concepts) — AI-powered notification priority scoring, conversation threading, and focus mode scheduling — were validated in prototype testing within 2 days and became foundational Slack features. Without Crazy 8s, the team would likely have converged on the first idea discussed in group brainstorming (notification grouping), which was the obvious but less innovative solution.

**So what:** Crazy 8s' most valuable output is not the 48 ideas but the convergence signal: when multiple participants independently arrive at the same idea, it suggests the idea is both obvious enough to be broadly accessible and unconventional enough to have required the time pressure to surface. These convergent ideas are the highest-priority candidates for the decision matrix.

## Pitfalls
- Not enforcing the time limit strictly — if participants are allowed more than 1 minute per panel, they begin to polish and elaborate early ideas rather than generating new ones, defeating the purpose of the exercise.
- Running Crazy 8s before briefing participants on the confirmed root causes — ideas generated without this context are unlikely to address the actual causes of the problem.
- Allowing discussion during the 8-minute individual sketching period — the individual nature of the exercise is essential. Group discussion during the sketching period anchors participants on each other's ideas.
- Using Crazy 8s as the solution selection tool — Crazy 8s is a divergent technique. Do not evaluate or score ideas during or immediately after the exercise. Selection happens later through the decision matrix.

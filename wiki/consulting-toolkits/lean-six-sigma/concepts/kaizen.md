# Kaizen — Continuous Improvement

*Category: process-improvement · Toolkit: Lean Six Sigma*

## What it is
A philosophy and structured practice of continuous incremental improvement in which all employees contribute ideas for eliminating waste and improving quality, with a systematic process for capturing, evaluating, and rapidly implementing the best ideas — embedded in the Control phase to prevent regression and drive ongoing performance gains.

**Origin:** Kaizen (改善, Japanese: 'change for better') was developed within the Toyota Production System in the 1950s by Taiichi Ohno and Masaaki Imai. Popularised globally through Imai's 1986 book 'Kaizen: The Key to Japan's Competitive Success.' The concept of the Kaizen Event (a focused 3–5 day rapid improvement workshop) was developed by Western Lean practitioners in the 1990s as a way to implement Kaizen in organisations without Toyota's deeply embedded culture of continuous improvement.

## Why it works
Large-scale, infrequent improvements create long periods of stagnation between improvement events and generate resistance because they are disruptive. Kaizen's philosophy is that many small, daily improvements — each individually modest — compound over time into transformational capability gains. It also embeds improvement as a cultural norm rather than a project: every employee is both a process operator AND an improvement contributor. In the DMAIC Control phase, Kaizen ensures that the project's gains are not a one-time achievement but the new baseline from which the next improvement cycle begins.

## When to use
Phase V (Control) of DMAIC as the ongoing monitoring and improvement mechanism. Also triggered by out-of-control signals on SPC charts (mini-Kaizen investigation). Kaizen Events are used in Phase IV (Improve) as the implementation vehicle for 3–5 day rapid improvements on contained process problems.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Establish a Kaizen Idea Management System: a simple form or digital tool where any employee can submit an improvement idea in 5 minutes or less. The system must have a defined review cycle (weekly or bi-weekly) and a committed response time (idea acknowledged within 48 hours, decision within 2 weeks).
2. 2. In the Control phase of DMAIC, schedule monthly 30-minute Kaizen stand-up reviews of the SPC control chart with the process team. The agenda: (a) review the control chart for trends or signals, (b) celebrate recent small improvements, (c) identify one area for the next Kaizen cycle.
3. 3. For any out-of-control signal on the SPC chart, treat it as a prompt for a mini-Kaizen investigation: apply Five Whys within 48 hours, identify the special cause, implement a countermeasure, and update the SOP within 1 week.
4. 4. For larger improvement opportunities identified in the Kaizen reviews, run a Kaizen Event (3–5 day rapid improvement workshop): Day 1 — map current state and identify waste; Days 2–3 — design and implement improved state; Day 4 — pilot and refine; Day 5 — standardise, document, and present results to leadership.
5. 5. After each Kaizen improvement, update the SOP, retrain operators, and update the control chart's control limits to reflect the new (improved) process baseline. Never use pre-improvement control limits on a post-improvement process.
6. 6. Track a Kaizen idea scorecard: ideas submitted per month per employee, ideas implemented (implementation rate target: 50%+), and average time from submission to implementation.
7. 7. Recognise Kaizen contributors publicly — a weekly 'Kaizen of the Week' highlight in team meetings creates social incentive for participation and signals that improvement is genuinely valued.
8. 8. Escalate ideas that require cross-functional changes or capital investment to the appropriate forum (department leader review, capital budget process). Do not let the Kaizen system become a dumping ground for ideas that require more than 5 days of effort — those are DMAIC projects, not Kaizen improvements.

## Real-life example — Toyota Motor Manufacturing (Georgetown, Kentucky)
Toyota's Georgetown, Kentucky plant — its first full-scale North American production facility — implemented the Andon system as an operational expression of Kaizen. Any assembly worker who encounters a quality problem pulls the Andon cord, stopping the line. The team leader and process engineer respond within 60 seconds, apply Five Whys on the spot, and either resolve the issue or document it as a Kaizen project. The Georgetown plant tracks over 80,000 Kaizen ideas per year from its approximately 8,000 employees — roughly 10 ideas per employee per year. The implementation rate exceeds 90%. This system is why Toyota's Georgetown plant consistently achieves the highest quality scores in North American vehicle assembly.

**So what:** Kaizen's competitive advantage is not any individual improvement — it is the compounding of thousands of small improvements per year, implemented by the people who do the work and who therefore understand the process most deeply. No external consultant or executive initiative can replicate this at scale.

## Pitfalls
- Implementing Kaizen Events as one-time improvement events without embedding the Sustain phase — a Kaizen Event without Standardise and daily management produces a temporary spike that fades within 90 days.
- Building a Kaizen idea system that does not respond to ideas — if employees submit ideas and hear nothing for months, submissions stop permanently. Committed response times are mandatory.
- Treating all improvement as DMAIC projects and none as Kaizen — a DMAIC project on a 10-minute improvement opportunity is waste. Kaizen addresses changes that can be implemented in less than 5 days; DMAIC addresses changes requiring data collection, statistical analysis, and pilot testing.
- Kaizen without standardisation — every Kaizen improvement must be written into the SOP and reflected in the control plan. Without this, the improvement exists only in the memory of the people who made it and disappears when they move roles.

# 5S Workplace Organisation

*Category: process-improvement · Toolkit: Lean Six Sigma*

## What it is
A five-step workplace organisation methodology (Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain) that eliminates environmental waste — primarily motion, search time, and errors arising from disorganised physical or digital workspaces — creating a visual management system where abnormalities are immediately visible.

**Origin:** Developed within the Toyota Production System in the 1950s–70s, rooted in Hiroyuki Hirano's work on workplace organisation. The Japanese originals are Seiri (Sort), Seiton (Set in Order), Seiso (Shine/Clean), Seiketsu (Standardise), and Shitsuke (Sustain/Discipline). Popularised globally through Lean manufacturing books and training programs in the 1990s.

## Why it works
A disorganised workspace creates hidden waste: operators spend time searching for tools, equipment fails because cleaning reveals maintenance issues, defects are hidden in cluttered areas, and variation in workplace organisation leads to variation in output quality. 5S creates a workplace where everything has a designated location, the standard state is visible, and any deviation from standard is immediately obvious (visual management). The 'Sustain' step is where most 5S programs fail — it converts a one-time improvement event into an ongoing discipline.

## When to use
Phase IV (Improve) as a solution component for any root cause related to Motion, Transportation, or Extra-processing waste identified in the DOWNTIME analysis. Also deployed as a foundation step before higher-order Lean tools (value stream mapping, kanban) to create the stable environment those tools require.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. SORT: Conduct a Red Tag exercise — attach a red tag to every item in the workspace whose necessity is in question. Items that have not been used in 30 days and are not needed for planned work are candidates for removal. Remove, relocate, or discard tagged items after a review period of 48–72 hours.
2. 2. SET IN ORDER: For each remaining item, assign a designated storage location based on frequency of use (items used every hour within arm's reach; items used weekly in a nearby cabinet; items used monthly in remote storage). Mark locations with tape outlines, shadow boards, or labelled shelves. The test: any item out of place should be immediately visible.
3. 3. SHINE: Clean every surface, machine, and storage area thoroughly. During cleaning, identify sources of contamination (oil leaks, dust sources, corrosion). Document these as maintenance actions. Cleaning is an inspection activity — it reveals problems that disorganisation had hidden.
4. 4. STANDARDISE: Photograph the post-Sort/Set/Shine state as the visual standard. Create a 5S checklist capturing the expected state of each area. Assign zone ownership (each operator owns a defined area). Post visual standards at the point of use.
5. 5. SUSTAIN: Integrate 5S audits into the daily management routine — a 5-minute visual check at the start of each shift using the standardised checklist. Conduct a monthly formal 5S audit with scores by zone. Track audit scores over time. Address declining scores within 48 hours — declining scores signal that the 'Sustain' discipline is eroding.
6. 6. For digital/knowledge work environments: Sort = delete or archive files not accessed in 90 days; Set in Order = standardise folder taxonomy and naming conventions; Shine = clear email inboxes and outdated shared documents; Standardise = document the folder structure and enforce via SharePoint metadata; Sustain = monthly folder audit assigned to a team member.
7. 7. After initial 5S implementation, conduct a waste walk (DOWNTIME) in the same area to verify that motion, search time, and extra-processing waste have been reduced. Quantify the before/after improvement.
8. 8. Recognise and celebrate early 5S successes publicly — 5S is one of the first visible Lean improvements and its early wins build the team's confidence in Lean's practical value.

## Real-life example — Boeing Commercial Airplanes (Renton, Washington)
Boeing implemented 5S across its 737 assembly line in Renton, Washington as part of its Lean transformation in the late 1990s. Before 5S, assemblers spent an estimated 20–30% of their time searching for tools, parts, and documentation. After Sort (removing 50+ types of obsolete tools from assembly stations), Set in Order (shadow boards for every hand tool at every station), and Standardise (visual work instructions posted at eye level at each station), tool search time dropped to under 2% of work time. The 737 assembly line's move rate improved from one plane every 3 days to one plane every day — though 5S was one of several concurrent improvements including moving from a static assembly to a moving assembly line.

**So what:** 5S is the foundation of visual management — it creates the physical conditions in which all other Lean improvements can be sustained. Without 5S, a process improvement may achieve its Cpk target during the pilot but regress within months as the workplace reverts to disorganisation.

## Pitfalls
- Stopping at Shine — many organisations implement Sort, Set in Order, and Shine as a one-time 'clean-up event' without Standardise and Sustain. Within 60 days, the area reverts. 5S is a system, not an event.
- Applying 5S as punishment for a poorly performing team rather than as a structured improvement tool — this creates resentment and ensures Sustain will fail.
- Treating 5S as only a manufacturing tool — in financial services and healthcare, digital 5S (shared drives, inbox management, documentation standards) delivers equivalent waste reduction.
- Not measuring the waste-reduction impact — 5S without a before/after waste walk leaves the business benefit unmeasured and makes the investment hard to justify for future 5S events.

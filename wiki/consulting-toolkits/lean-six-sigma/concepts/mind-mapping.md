# Mind Mapping

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Lean Six Sigma*

## What it is
A non-linear idea organisation technique that uses a central node (the problem or theme) with radiating branches (categories) and sub-branches (ideas) to visually group and connect ideas — enabling a cross-functional team to organise a large raw idea bank by root cause addressed and to identify relationships between ideas that linear lists obscure.

**Origin:** Mind mapping as a structured technique was developed and popularised by Tony Buzan in the 1970s through his book 'Use Your Head' (BBC Books, 1974) and the Mind Map trademark he filed in 1990. Buzan drew on earlier radial thinking techniques from note-taking and creative thinking literature. In Lean Six Sigma, mind mapping is used in the Improve phase as an idea-organising tool between raw brainstorm output and the solution decision matrix.

## Why it works
Linear brainstorm lists have two structural problems: they anchor on the first ideas generated (availability bias), and they group ideas sequentially rather than by theme or root cause addressed. A mind map with the problem at the centre and root causes as first-level branches forces the team to organise ideas by which root cause each idea addresses — immediately revealing which root causes have abundant solution options (where the team can be selective) and which have sparse coverage (where more ideation is needed before a solution can be selected).

## When to use
Phase IV (Improve) as Step 3, after the raw idea bank has been generated (HMW questions, Crazy 8s) and before the business case is built. Also used in project retrospectives to organise learnings by theme and in team problem-solving sessions to structure unstructured input.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Write the problem statement (or the improvement objective) in the centre of a large whiteboard or digital canvas.
2. 2. Draw one branch from the centre for each confirmed root cause from Phase III. Label each branch with the root cause name.
3. 3. For each idea from the raw idea bank (HMW session, Crazy 8s, and open brainstorm), place it on the branch corresponding to the root cause it primarily addresses. If an idea addresses multiple root causes, place it on the most relevant branch and draw a dashed cross-link to the other branch.
4. 4. Once all ideas are placed, examine each branch: branches with 2 or fewer ideas may need additional ideation. Branches with 10+ ideas can be filtered — apply a quick feasibility screen (Is this technically possible? Is it within budget? Can it be implemented in the timeframe?) to reduce to 3–5 per branch.
5. 5. Look for recurring themes or combinations: can an idea from Branch 1 be combined with an idea from Branch 3 to create a more comprehensive solution? Cross-links often reveal these combinations.
6. 6. Identify the top 2–3 solution clusters across all branches — these are the candidate solutions for the business case (Step 4 of Improve) and the decision matrix (Step 5 of Improve).
7. 7. Mark all excluded ideas as 'Not Selected — Reason' (infeasible, out of budget, duplicate, etc.). Do not delete them — they may become relevant if the selected solution fails the pilot.
8. 8. Photograph or export the mind map as a permanent record of the ideation process. It serves as the audit trail showing that the selected solution was chosen from a comprehensive, structured set of options.

## Real-life example — Merrill Lynch (Credit Operations)
During a Lean Six Sigma project at Merrill Lynch's credit operations center in the early 2000s, the team used mind mapping after a Crazy 8s session generated 56 solution ideas for a credit approval delay problem. The mind map was built around three confirmed root causes: (1) manual data entry, (2) sequential approval routing, and (3) inconsistent credit criteria application. Placing ideas on the three branches immediately revealed that 38 of the 56 ideas addressed Root Cause 1 (data entry), 12 addressed Root Cause 2 (sequential routing), and only 6 addressed Root Cause 3 (criteria). The team ran a second ideation session specifically for Root Cause 3, generating 11 additional ideas. The final decision matrix evaluated 3 combined solution clusters — each addressing all three root causes — rather than optimising a single root cause at the expense of the others.

**So what:** Mind mapping revealed a critical imbalance in ideation coverage that a linear list would have obscured. Without the visual structure, the team would have selected a solution heavily biased toward Root Cause 1 (the most familiar problem) while leaving Root Cause 3 (the least familiar) under-addressed.

## Pitfalls
- Building the mind map with root causes as the centre rather than the problem statement — root causes are branches, not the centre. The centre is the improvement objective; branches are the causes to be addressed.
- Using mind mapping as a solo activity rather than a team exercise — the value is the discussion triggered when team members disagree about which branch an idea belongs on, revealing assumptions about the solution.
- Treating the mind map as the solution selection tool — the mind map organises ideas, but selection requires a decision matrix with weighted criteria. Do not allow the 'most populated branch' to drive selection without quantitative evaluation.
- Creating excessively deep mind maps (4+ branch levels) — this level of detail is premature at the ideation stage and slows the process. Limit to 3 levels maximum during Improve ideation.

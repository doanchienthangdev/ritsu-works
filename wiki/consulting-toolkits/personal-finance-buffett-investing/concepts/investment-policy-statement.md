# Investment Policy Statement (IPS)

*Category: financial · Toolkit: Personal Finance & Warren Buffett Investing*

## What it is
A formal written document that codifies all strategic investment parameters for an individual investor — objective, portfolio construction rules, mindset guardrails, decision process, investment frequency, cash reserve policy, time horizon, and exit triggers — serving as the governing constitution for all investment decisions.

**Origin:** The Investment Policy Statement is a standard instrument in institutional portfolio management, required by ERISA for pension funds and recommended by the CFA Institute for all managed portfolios. Its adaptation to individual investors was promoted by financial planners and the CFP Board. Buffett's equivalent is the internal operating discipline of Berkshire Hathaway's capital allocation process, which Buffett has described in his letters as being governed by a consistent set of principles that have not changed in 60 years.

## Why it works
The IPS solves the behavioural finance problem at source: most investment mistakes are not analytical errors but emotional decisions made in the absence of pre-committed rules. The IPS creates a constitution that the investor must consciously override rather than casually ignore — providing a friction-introducing commitment device against FOMO buying, panic selling, and strategy drift. Institutional endowments with formal IPS documents consistently outperform those without them over 10-year periods.

## When to use

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Open the IPS template. Complete Section 1 first: write a single quantified investment objective. Include: starting portfolio value, target portfolio value, timeline (years), and target CAGR. Verify this is mathematically consistent with your Phase 1 investable surplus.
2. 2. Complete Section 2 — Portfolio Strategy: specify the exact number of holdings you will maintain (Buffett advocates 8–20 for a focused portfolio), your maximum position size as % of portfolio, your sector concentration cap, and your rebalancing policy.
3. 3. Complete Section 3 — Mindset Rules: write each rule as a first-person, action-specific commitment. The Mr. Market rule, the fear/greed rule, your circle of competence definition (list the 3–5 industries explicitly), your news-blackout policy during drawdowns, and your do-nothing default.
4. 4. Complete Section 4 — Decision Process: specify that you will apply the 4-gate framework (Phases 5.1–5.5) to every investment candidate, in order, without skipping gates.
5. 5. Complete Section 5 — Frequency: set a maximum number of new positions per year (typically 2–4 for a disciplined individual investor). This prevents overtrading and forces selectivity.
6. 6. Complete Section 6 — Cash Floor: set a minimum cash percentage you will maintain in the portfolio at all times. Buffett maintains 15–25% for 'dry powder' to exploit dislocations. Set your floor (10–20% recommended).
7. 7. Complete Section 7 — Time Horizon: commit to a minimum holding period in writing (5 years minimum recommended; 10 years preferred).
8. 8. Complete Section 8 — Exit Triggers: for each position, you will specify (in the Investment Case Document) the exact conditions that trigger a sell. The IPS establishes the categories: (a) fundamental deterioration, (b) price exceeds 150% of intrinsic value, (c) reallocation to a significantly better opportunity. Price decline alone is never a trigger.
9. 9. Sign and date the IPS. Store it prominently. Review before every Phase 5 analysis.

## Real-life example — Yale Endowment (David Swensen)
The Yale Endowment under David Swensen consistently outperformed institutional benchmarks for 30+ years (1985–2021), achieving a 13.7% CAGR versus the typical US university endowment's 10.5% over the same period. Swensen attributed much of this performance advantage to Yale's formal Investment Policy Statement and the discipline to maintain it through market downturns — including maintaining a 30% allocation to alternative assets during the 2008–2009 financial crisis when other endowments were panic-selling. The IPS prevented committee-driven emotional decisions.

**So what:** The IPS is not bureaucracy — it is the most important risk management tool an investor has. It converts abstract commitment to value investing principles into enforceable, pre-committed operational rules.

## Pitfalls
- Writing the IPS but not consulting it before each Phase 5 analysis — the IPS must be an active checkpoint, not an archived document.
- Setting position size and cash floor rules but overriding them in practice — every override without amending the IPS is a policy violation; track overrides and investigate patterns.
- Failing to define the circle of competence specifically — 'technology' is not a circle of competence; 'enterprise cloud software with subscription revenue models' is.
- Setting an unrealistic CAGR target (e.g., 30%) — a target that requires extraordinary results makes the IPS aspirational fiction rather than an operating guide; 12–15% is ambitious but achievable for a skilled individual investor.

# MOAT Assessment Framework

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Personal Finance & Warren Buffett Investing*

## What it is
A structured five-source framework for identifying and classifying a company's durable competitive advantage (its economic 'moat'), assessing both the strength (Wide/Narrow/None) and durability (10-year outlook) of the advantage — the single most important qualitative criterion in Buffett's investment methodology.

**Origin:** The 'economic moat' metaphor was coined by Warren Buffett (Berkshire Hathaway annual letter, 1986, with refinements in 1996 and 1999). Morningstar formalised the five-source MOAT taxonomy (intangible assets, network effects, switching costs, cost advantage, efficient scale) in its equity research methodology, published as 'Morningstar's Economic Moat Methodology' (2020). Pat Dorsey's 'The Little Book That Builds Wealth' (2008) provides the most accessible elaboration of the five MOAT sources.

## Why it works
Competitive advantage is what allows a business to earn returns on capital above its cost of capital over a sustained period. Without a MOAT, competitive forces (as described in Porter's Five Forces) will erode above-average returns to the average over time. A Wide MOAT company can sustain excess returns for 20+ years; a Narrow MOAT for 10–20 years; a No-MOAT company's excess returns are transient. Buffett's core insight: you need to understand not just whether a business has a moat today, but whether the moat will be wider or narrower in 10 years.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. For the company under assessment, work through each of the five MOAT sources in order. For each source, ask: 'Is there credible evidence that this company benefits from this source?'
2. 2. Intangible Assets: Does the company charge a premium price for a product that costs no more to produce than a commodity alternative? Is there evidence customers buy based on brand rather than price comparison? Does the company hold patents protecting key products, or does it operate under a regulatory licence competitors cannot replicate?
3. 3. Network Effects: Does the value of the product or service to each user increase as more users join? Is there a data advantage that compounds with scale? Are there direct (same-side) or indirect (multi-sided platform) network effects? Check DAU/MAU growth and engagement metrics.
4. 4. Switching Costs: How long does it take a customer to switch to a competitor? What are the direct (contract break fees, retraining costs) and indirect (data migration risk, workflow disruption, re-certification) switching costs? A high switching cost creates 'sticky' revenue — look for multi-year contract structures and high renewal rates.
5. 5. Cost Advantage: Calculate the company's gross margin relative to industry peers. A structural cost advantage should be visible as a persistent gross margin premium. Investigate the source: proprietary process (GEICO's direct model), unique scale economics (Amazon's fulfillment density), or privileged input access (mining company with the lowest-cost ore body).
6. 6. Efficient Scale: Is the total addressable market only large enough to support 1–3 players profitably? If a new entrant captured 20% market share, would it earn an adequate return on its capital? If not, rational capital will stay out — this is efficient scale.
7. 7. Classify the MOAT: Wide (≥2 strong sources, durable 20+ years), Narrow (1 moderate source or 2 weak sources, durable 10–20 years), None (no credible source identified).
8. 8. Assess 10-year durability: for each MOAT source present, identify the most credible threat. Classify durability as Strong (threats exist but are manageable), Moderate (at least one credible threat with uncertain outcome), or Weak (clear erosion pathway visible).
9. 9. Overall verdict: proceed with investment only for Wide or Narrow MOAT companies with Strong or Moderate durability.

## Real-life example — Visa Inc. (V)
Visa's MOAT assessment illustrates multiple concurrent sources: (1) Network Effects — Visa's payment network connects 4.3 billion cardholders and 80+ million merchant locations in 200+ countries; every merchant that accepts Visa makes every Visa card more valuable, and vice versa — a classic two-sided network effect; (2) Switching Costs — merchants face significant operational disruption and consumer inconvenience from removing a major card brand; large retailers who tried (Amazon's UK Visa ban attempt in 2022) reversed the decision quickly; (3) Intangible Assets — the Visa brand is among the 10 most recognisable globally, enabling continued premium interchange rates. MOAT classification: Wide. Durability: Strong (crypto and CBDC are long-horizon threats; no near-term erosion of the card network's ubiquity). This combination of network effects + switching costs makes Visa one of the clearest Wide MOAT businesses available to individual investors.

**So what:** The most powerful MOATs combine multiple sources. Visa's network effect and switching cost combination means that even a technologically superior competitor must solve a chicken-and-egg problem across billions of users — a nearly insurmountable barrier.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing 'good products' with 'competitive moats' — a company that makes excellent products but faces easy imitation has no moat; the moat is the barrier to imitation, not the quality of the product.
- Treating market share as a proxy for moat — high market share in an undifferentiated commodity market (e.g., steel) does not imply a moat; look for sustainable pricing power, not just volume.
- Assuming technology moats are permanent — technology moats are the least durable; what was a technological moat in 2010 (e.g., feature phone dominance) can disappear in 5 years.
- Omitting durability assessment — a MOAT that is currently Wide but facing strong erosion pressures is worth less than a Narrow MOAT with no credible threats.

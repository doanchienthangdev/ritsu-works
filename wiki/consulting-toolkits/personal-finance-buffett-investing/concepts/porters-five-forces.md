# Porter's Five Forces

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Personal Finance & Warren Buffett Investing*

## What it is
A framework for analysing the competitive intensity and profit-generating potential of an industry by examining five structural forces: competitive rivalry, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, and bargaining power of suppliers — to determine whether the industry structurally allows above-average returns.

**Origin:** Developed by Michael E. Porter of Harvard Business School, published in the Harvard Business Review article 'How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy' (March–April 1979) and elaborated in 'Competitive Strategy: Techniques for Analyzing Industries and Competitors' (1980). Porter's framework remains the canonical tool for industry structure analysis in strategy and investment contexts.

## Why it works
Industry profitability is not random — it is structurally determined by the balance of power among five forces. A highly attractive industry (low competitive intensity, high barriers to entry, no viable substitutes, fragmented buyers and suppliers) allows all participants to earn above-average returns. An unattractive industry destroys returns for even the best operators. For Buffett, Porter's Five Forces analysis serves a specific purpose: identifying whether the industry structure itself provides a tailwind for the MOAT, or whether even a Wide MOAT company faces structural headwinds that will eventually erode above-average returns.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. For each of the five forces, rate it as a Competitive Advantage (Low pressure on company), Neutral, or Competitive Disadvantage (High pressure) based on specific evidence.
2. 2. Competitive Rivalry: How many direct competitors does this company face? Is the industry growing (reducing rivalry) or mature/declining (intensifying rivalry)? Are products differentiated (reducing price pressure) or commodity-like (intensifying price pressure)? Is capacity utilisation in the industry high or low?
3. 3. Threat of New Entrants: What is the minimum capital required to enter this industry at a competitive scale? How long would it take a well-capitalised new entrant to build brand recognition, regulatory approvals, or distribution relationships comparable to the incumbent? Rate the barrier height (High/Medium/Low).
4. 4. Threat of Substitutes: What alternative products or services could satisfy the same customer need? Are substitutes improving in quality or falling in price? Rate the substitute threat (High/Medium/Low). Note: substitutes are different from competitors — they come from outside the industry.
5. 5. Bargaining Power of Buyers: Who are the primary buyers? Are they concentrated (few large buyers with leverage) or fragmented (many small buyers with little leverage)? Is the product a significant portion of the buyer's total cost? Can buyers credibly threaten to switch or self-supply?
6. 6. Bargaining Power of Suppliers: Who are the primary input suppliers? Are inputs standardised or unique? Can the company switch suppliers easily? Are there supplier substitutes?
7. 7. Synthesise: Based on the five force ratings, classify the overall industry as Attractive (4–5 forces favour the company), Moderately Attractive (2–3 forces favour), or Unattractive (0–1 forces favour).
8. 8. Connect to MOAT: Does the industry structure reinforce the company's MOAT (e.g., high new-entrant barriers protect the incumbent's brand premium) or work against it (e.g., strong buyer power limits pricing power despite a brand moat)?

## Real-life example — Coca-Cola Company — industry analysis using Porter's Five Forces
Applying Porter's Five Forces to Coca-Cola's core carbonated soft drinks (CSD) business: (1) Rivalry: duopoly with PepsiCo; differentiation through brand reduces pure price competition — ADVANTAGE; (2) New entrants: building a global beverage distribution network and brand to challenge Coca-Cola would require decades and tens of billions of dollars — HIGH BARRIER, ADVANTAGE; (3) Substitutes: water, juice, energy drinks, coffee — significant substitute category; health trends increasing substitute attractiveness — MODERATE THREAT; (4) Buyer power: retail is concentrated (Walmart, Costco), creating some buyer leverage, but Coca-Cola's brand means retailers cannot credibly delist it — MODERATE NEUTRAL; (5) Supplier power: inputs are commodity (water, sugar, aluminium) — multiple suppliers available — ADVANTAGE. Overall: 3.5/5 forces favourable — Moderately to Highly Attractive industry. The substitute threat is the primary structural risk, which Coca-Cola has partially addressed by expanding into water (Dasani), juice, and other beverage categories.

**So what:** Porter's analysis reveals that Coca-Cola's most significant structural risk is not rivalry with Pepsi but the long-term shift of consumer preferences toward lower-sugar alternatives — a substitute threat that no moat can fully neutralise. This insight correctly identifies management's strategic priority and is the kind of analysis that separates a '2-sentence business description' from a genuine investment thesis.

## Pitfalls
- Analysing 'the industry' too broadly — applying Porter's Five Forces to 'technology' is uninformative; apply to the specific segment the company competes in (e.g., 'enterprise cloud ERP software for mid-market manufacturing companies').
- Using the Five Forces analysis as a static snapshot — forces shift over time; a force rated 'Advantage' today may become 'Disadvantage' due to regulatory change or technology disruption; re-assess annually.
- Confusing 'our company vs. competitors' with the Five Forces — the framework analyses industry structure, not a specific company's competitive position within that industry; the MOAT assessment handles the company-specific question.

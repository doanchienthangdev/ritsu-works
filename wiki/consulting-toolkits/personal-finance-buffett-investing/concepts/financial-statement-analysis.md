# Financial Statement Analysis (10-Year Quality Scorecard)

*Category: financial · Toolkit: Personal Finance & Warren Buffett Investing*

## What it is
A systematic 10-year review of a company's income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement, producing a quantitative quality scorecard that assesses earnings consistency, capital efficiency, leverage, cash conversion, and financial strength — forming the core of Phase 5, Gate 2.

**Origin:** Security analysis using financial statements traces to Benjamin Graham and David Dodd's 'Security Analysis' (1934), which first systematised the interpretation of financial statements for investment purposes. Modern refinements were contributed by James Montier ('Accounting for Growth'), Warren Buffett's annual letters (which articulate specific metrics he prioritises — particularly ROE, owner earnings, and leverage), and Mary Buffett & David Clark's 'Warren Buffett and the Interpretation of Financial Statements' (2008).

## Why it works
A 10-year financial history (covering at least one full economic cycle) filters for businesses with genuinely durable economics from those with temporary advantages. A company that maintains 15%+ ROE through a recession has a fundamentally different business model from one whose ROE fluctuates between 5% and 25%. Buffett's framework requires looking for 'consistency and predictability' in financial results — unpredictable earnings are a signal that the business lacks a durable competitive advantage.

## When to use
Phase 5, Gate 2. This analysis takes 2–4 hours per company; apply only after Gate 1 (personal fit) passes to avoid wasted effort.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Source 10 years of annual financial statements (10-K filings) from SEC EDGAR, Morningstar, or Macrotrends.net. Download or record the following for each year: revenue, gross profit, operating profit, net income, EPS, total assets, total liabilities, shareholders equity, capex, and operating cash flow.
2. 2. Calculate ROE for each year = Net Income / Shareholders Equity. Plot as a table. Is it consistently > 15%? Does it hold up during recession years (2008–2009, 2020)? Declining ROE trend is a MOAT-erosion signal.
3. 3. Calculate Net Profit Margin for each year = Net Income / Revenue. Is it stable or growing? Consistent margins above 15% indicate pricing power. Declining margins signal competitive pressure.
4. 4. Extract EPS for each year (use diluted EPS to account for options and convertibles). Plot the trend. Buffett requires 'the earnings power of the business should be predictable, like a franchise' — if EPS jumps between $1 and $5, the business does not qualify.
5. 5. Calculate Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures. Calculate FCF as % of Net Income each year. If FCF consistently < 80% of net income, investigate: is capex unusually high (capital-intensive business)? Are working capital movements extreme? Is the divergence a signal of revenue recognition manipulation?
6. 6. Record Debt/Equity ratio from the balance sheet for each year. Has the company been growing more or less leveraged over time? Net cash positive (cash > debt) is a strong signal; rising leverage with flat earnings is a warning.
7. 7. Record shares outstanding for each year. Is the share count declining (buybacks — positive), stable (neutral), or rising (dilution — negative unless acquisition-driven with adequate return).
8. 8. Score each metric against Buffett's threshold: 1 point for passing, 0 for failing. Maximum score = 7 points. Advance companies scoring ≥ 5/7 to the MOAT assessment (Gate 3). Require passing all revenue/margin/FCF metrics for any score to count.

## Real-life example — Johnson & Johnson (JNJ), 10-year analysis (2010–2020)
A 10-year financial statement analysis of J&J from 2010 to 2020 would reveal: ROE average 24% (ranging 18–30%, dipping to 18% in the challenging 2020 COVID year but never breaking the 15% threshold); net margin averaging 20% (ranging 15–28%, with a temporary compression in 2017 due to tax reform); EPS grew from $4.78 in 2010 to $5.51 in 2020 — a consistent upward trend with one slight dip in 2019 due to talc litigation provisions; FCF consistently above 100% of net income (J&J's low capex relative to earnings is characteristic of a diversified pharmaceutical franchise); Debt/Equity averaging 0.43 (slightly elevated but manageable for an investment-grade healthcare company); share count declining from 2.84B to 2.68B (consistent buyback programme). Score: 7/7. J&J qualifies for MOAT assessment under every criterion.

**So what:** A company that maintains > 15% ROE, > 15% net margin, consistent EPS growth, and low leverage through a decade that included the 2015 oil crash, 2016 election volatility, and 2020 COVID pandemic has demonstrated durable business economics — the financial evidence for a Wide or Narrow MOAT.

## Pitfalls
- Using only the most recent year's data — a single strong year can mask structural weakness; always use 10 years.
- Ignoring FCF in favour of net income alone — earnings are an accounting construct; FCF is cash; large divergences between the two often indicate quality issues.
- Accepting declining ROE without investigating the cause — declining ROE may indicate competitive pressure (bad), or it may indicate the company is investing in growth that has not yet matured (neutral). Investigate before scoring.
- Using GAAP EPS without adjusting for large one-time items — significant restructuring charges, impairments, or gains can distort GAAP EPS substantially; use adjusted EPS from company disclosures or calculate manually.

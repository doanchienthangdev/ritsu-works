# Portfolio Construction Rules

*Category: financial · Toolkit: Personal Finance & Warren Buffett Investing*

## What it is
A codified set of rules governing how individual stock positions are sized, combined, and maintained within an investment portfolio — addressing concentration vs. diversification, position sizing by conviction, sector limits, and rebalancing philosophy in the Buffett tradition.

**Origin:** Portfolio construction theory was formalised by Harry Markowitz in his 1952 paper 'Portfolio Selection' (Modern Portfolio Theory). Buffett explicitly rejects Markowitz-style maximum diversification, instead drawing on the concentration philosophy of Graham's book 'The Intelligent Investor' and Phil Fisher's chapter on diversification in 'Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits'. Buffett's own stated view: 'Diversification is protection against ignorance. It makes little sense if you know what you are doing.'

## Why it works
Over-diversification (owning 40–100 stocks) mathematically eliminates the ability to outperform the market — when you own the market, you earn the market return minus costs. Buffett's insight: if you apply the 4-gate due diligence process thoroughly, you will naturally identify far fewer qualifying investments (maybe 10–20 in a career) and those should receive meaningful capital. Concentration without research is gambling; concentration with rigorous research is disciplined high-conviction investing.

## When to use
Phase 3 (design) and Phase 5, Step 5 (final decision and position sizing). Verify rules before every new position is opened.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Start with your IPS. Retrieve your agreed maximum number of holdings, maximum single-position %, and sector cap.
2. 2. For each position you consider adding, assign a conviction tier: High (all 4 gates pass with wide margin, ≥ 3 strong MOAT sources, estimated IRR > 15%), Medium (all gates pass, 1–2 MOAT sources, IRR 10–15%), or Starter (all gates barely pass, new to circle of competence).
3. 3. Translate conviction tier to capital allocation: High = 15–25%, Medium = 8–15%, Starter = 3–8% of portfolio. Never allocate more than your IPS maximum.
4. 4. Before adding any new position, calculate the resulting sector concentration. If adding this position would exceed your sector cap, do not add — regardless of individual investment quality.
5. 5. Calculate your cash position after the intended purchase. If it falls below your IPS cash floor, do not add — regardless of opportunity quality. Preserve dry powder.
6. 6. Record the conviction tier and size rationale in the Investment Case Document before executing.
7. 7. Resist the urge to add a new position simply because you have cash. The default is to hold cash until a genuine high-conviction opportunity arrives.

## Real-life example — Berkshire Hathaway (Buffett's public portfolio, 1988–1995)
In Berkshire's equity portfolio during the late 1980s–1990s, Buffett concentrated heavily in a small number of positions. Coca-Cola represented over 35% of the equity portfolio by value at peak. The Washington Post Company was a multi-decade holding at a significant portfolio weight. American Express was similarly concentrated. By contrast, the typical US mutual fund in the same period held 150+ positions. Berkshire's concentrated portfolio significantly outperformed the S&P 500 index during this period. The concentration was enabled by Buffett's conviction — which itself was enabled by deep qualitative and quantitative analysis.

**So what:** Concentration is the reward of high-conviction research, not a risk management failure. The right number of positions is the number you can research thoroughly — not the number that makes portfolio theory happy.

## Pitfalls
- Equal-weighting all positions regardless of conviction — this is a lazy habit from index-fund thinking; a 3% position in a company you think is exceptional is a missed opportunity.
- Adding a position that violates the sector cap because the individual opportunity looks excellent — sector caps exist precisely because excellent individual opportunities can cluster in overvalued sectors; the cap protects you from correlated risk.
- Confusing 'reducing position size' with 'taking profit' — Buffett almost never reduces a position because it has grown to a higher % of portfolio. He holds until fundamentals change or price exceeds intrinsic value.

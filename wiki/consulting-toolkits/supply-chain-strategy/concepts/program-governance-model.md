# Program Governance Model (Three-Tier)

*Category: planning · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A three-tier governance structure for complex supply chain transformation programs comprising: a Steering Committee (executive accountability and strategic direction), a Program Management Office (PMO — cross-initiative coordination, KPI tracking, risk management, escalation), and Workstream Teams (delivery of individual initiatives). Each tier has defined roles, meeting cadences, decision rights, and escalation protocols.

**Origin:** Synthesized from PMI (Project Management Institute) program governance standards, Prince2 program management methodology, and McKinsey/BCG engagement operating models for large-scale transformation programs.

## Why it works
Supply chain transformations typically involve 15–25 concurrent initiatives across four functional pillars, spanning 2–4 years. Without explicit governance, three failure modes are common: (1) strategic drift — individual initiatives optimize locally but diverge from the overall strategy; (2) resource conflicts — competing initiatives fight for the same talent and budget without arbitration; (3) benefit leakage — individual initiatives deliver outputs (systems installed, processes documented) but the expected business benefits are never realized because no one is tracking them. The three-tier model assigns clear accountability for each failure mode: the Steering Committee for strategy alignment, the PMO for cross-initiative coordination and benefit tracking, and Workstream Teams for delivery.

## When to use
Establish the governance model in Phase V before the roadmap goes live. The governance structure must be in place, staffed, and operational before the first Wave 1 initiatives begin. Also revisit governance design when a transformation program extends beyond 18 months or when the initiative count exceeds 10 concurrent workstreams.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the Steering Committee: appoint the CSCO as chair; include the CFO (budget authority), CHRO (people/organizational authority), at least 2 business unit presidents (represent the internal customers of the supply chain), and the PMO Lead. Define Terms of Reference: monthly meeting, quorum requirements, voting vs. advisory roles, decision rights (what the SC can approve vs. what requires board-level input).
2. 2. Establish the PMO: appoint a full-time PMO Director (dedicated, not split-responsibility). Staff the PMO with: a Portfolio Analyst (consolidates RAG status and KPI data), a Financial Controller (tracks spend vs. plan), a Change Management Coordinator (tracks ADKAR progress), and an IT Integration Lead (manages technical dependencies). The PMO is the 'nerve centre' of the transformation.
3. 3. Set up Workstream Teams: each initiative has a dedicated Project Manager and a cross-functional team. Define team composition, working norms, sprint/milestone cadence, and how the team escalates to the PMO.
4. 4. Design the governance calendar: Steering Committee monthly (Day 1 of month); PMO weekly (every Monday); Workstream Teams twice-weekly. The cadence creates a predictable rhythm for issue resolution and decision-making.
5. 5. Build the master dashboard: the PMO owns a real-time dashboard tracking — for each initiative — RAG status (Red/Amber/Green for scope, schedule, budget, and risk), milestone progress, benefit realization vs. plan, and top-3 risks. The dashboard is the primary tool for Steering Committee monthly reviews.
6. 6. Define escalation protocols: if an issue remains unresolved for 48 hours at Workstream level, it escalates to PMO. If unresolved at PMO within 5 business days, it escalates to Steering Committee. Define what constitutes an 'issue' requiring escalation (scope change, budget overrun >10%, critical-path delay >2 weeks, unresolvable cross-initiative dependency conflict).
7. 7. Establish change-control: scope changes ≤5% of initiative budget are within Workstream authority; 5–20% within PMO authority; >20% require Steering Committee approval. Document all scope changes in a change log.
8. 8. Run the first Steering Committee as a baseline session: present the approved roadmap, confirm governance model, set the KPI baselines for each initiative, and get executive commitment to the governance structure and its time requirements.

## Real-life example — Unilever
Unilever's Connected 4 Growth (C4G) supply chain transformation (2016–2021) established a global PMO that was reported as a competitive differentiator in the program's success. The PMO was staffed with 45 professionals globally — a significant investment that leadership initially challenged. The counter-argument: the total transformation portfolio was €2.4B of investment and €1B of annual benefit. A PMO of 45 FTEs (cost ~€10M/year) represented 0.4% of portfolio value — well within industry norms for complex program governance (0.5–2% is standard). The Steering Committee (chaired by the CSCO) met monthly with a structured agenda: first 20 minutes on metrics (RAG dashboard review), next 30 minutes on decisions (escalations requiring SC authority), final 10 minutes on strategy alignment (is the portfolio still aligned to corporate priorities?). The discipline of that format — metrics first, decisions second, strategy last — prevented meetings from devolving into status updates.

**So what:** The PMO is not overhead — it is the mechanism that prevents 25 concurrent initiatives from becoming 25 local optimizations that add up to zero transformation. Under-invest in the PMO and the transformation's benefits leak before they can be measured.

## Template
Complete the governance design template for the transformation program. All roles must be named individuals, not job titles. The Terms of Reference for each tier should be approved by the Steering Committee at the program's first formal meeting.

- [ ] Steering Committee Chair (named individual)
- [ ] Steering Committee Members (names + roles)
- [ ] Steering Committee Meeting Cadence and Format
- [ ] Steering Committee Decision Rights (what can be approved at this level)
- [ ] PMO Director (named individual)
- [ ] PMO Team Composition (names + roles)
- [ ] PMO Weekly Meeting Agenda Structure
- [ ] Escalation Threshold from Workstream to PMO (define the trigger)
- [ ] Escalation Threshold from PMO to Steering Committee (define the trigger)
- [ ] Dashboard Platform (tool used for real-time RAG tracking)
- [ ] Benefit Realization Tracking Method (how benefits are validated, not just claimed)
- [ ] Change-Control Authority Thresholds (by tier)

## Pitfalls
- Part-time PMO Directors: a transformation program with 15+ initiatives and $100M+ in investment requires a full-time, dedicated PMO Director. A part-time Director managing a day job alongside the PMO role produces delays, escalation failures, and governance gaps.
- Steering Committee as a status-report session: if the Steering Committee spends 60% of its time receiving status updates, it is not governing — it is spectating. Restructure: send the dashboard in advance; use the meeting for decisions and strategic alignment.
- Benefit tracking without benefit validation: the PMO often tracks that an initiative has 'gone live' and reports the expected benefits from the business case. Actual benefits must be measured independently (Finance-owned) and compared to the business case projection. Without this, the program never learns whether its business cases were accurate.
- Weak escalation culture: in many organizations, project managers are reluctant to escalate issues because it signals failure. This culture traps problems at Workstream level until they are too large to fix without major impact. Explicitly reward early escalation — treat it as good project management, not a sign of incompetence.

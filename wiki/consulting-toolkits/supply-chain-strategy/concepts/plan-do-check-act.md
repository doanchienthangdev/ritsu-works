# Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA / Deming Cycle)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A four-stage iterative management cycle for continuous improvement: Plan (identify an improvement opportunity and design a change), Do (implement the change on a small scale), Check (measure the results against the expected outcome), and Act (standardize successful changes or iterate if results are unsatisfactory).

**Origin:** Developed by Walter Shewhart (1930s) and popularized by W. Edwards Deming during Japan's post-war industrial rebuilding (1950s). Fundamental to Toyota Production System, ISO 9001 Quality Management, and Lean Six Sigma.

## Why it works
Most supply chain improvement efforts fail to sustain because they are one-time events — a consultant recommends a solution, it is implemented, and the organization moves on. The PDCA cycle embeds improvement as a permanent operating discipline. By iterating through Plan-Do-Check-Act continuously, organizations learn from each implementation cycle, accumulate improvement knowledge, and prevent the post-go-live performance drift that undermines transformation benefits. The 'Check' stage is especially critical in supply chain contexts: without measuring actual results against plan, teams cannot distinguish between a successful implementation and a completed one.

## When to use
Use throughout Phase V for continuous improvement cycles on all supply chain KPIs that are below target. Also use as the governing framework for the ongoing CI engine after the transformation program formally closes.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. PLAN — Define the specific improvement opportunity: use a KPI-gap analysis to identify a supply chain metric that is below target (e.g., forecast accuracy at 68% vs. 85% target). State the improvement objective as a SMART goal.
2. 2. PLAN — Conduct root cause analysis: use 5 Whys (ask 'Why?' five times, each time drilling deeper) or an Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram to identify the root cause of the gap. Resist the temptation to implement the first plausible solution — ensure the root cause is confirmed before designing the change.
3. 3. PLAN — Design the change: specify what will be changed, how, by whom, and over what time period. Define the leading metric that will indicate whether the change is working (before the lag metric reflects it). Plan the pilot scope (which site, product, route, etc.).
4. 4. DO — Implement the change at pilot scale: run the change for a sufficient duration to generate statistically meaningful data (minimum 4–8 weeks for most supply chain KPIs). Assign a named owner for data collection.
5. 5. CHECK — Measure results: compare the actual pilot results to the target metric. Investigate any variance: did the change underperform because the implementation was flawed (execution gap) or because the root cause hypothesis was wrong (diagnosis gap)?
6. 6. ACT — Standardize or iterate: if the pilot achieved the target, document the change as a new standard operating procedure and plan the rollout to all relevant sites. If the pilot underperformed, refine the Plan and run a second cycle. Document all learnings regardless of outcome.
7. 7. Build PDCA into the governance cadence: include a PDCA cycle review in the monthly PMO meeting — what improvements are in Do phase? What Check results came in this month? What standardizations are in Act phase?
8. 8. Use PDCA to drive continuous improvement after transformation close: once the transformation program ends, PDCA becomes the primary mechanism for sustaining and advancing supply chain performance through the line organization.

## Real-life example — Toyota
Toyota's application of PDCA — called 'Plan-Do-Check-Adjust' in the Toyota Production System — is institutionalized through the 'A3 Report' (named after the paper size). Every improvement initiative, regardless of scale, is documented on a single A3 sheet: the current situation, the root cause analysis (5 Whys), the proposed countermeasure, the implementation plan, the results measured, and the lessons learned. The A3 process forces the iterative discipline of PDCA — no improvement is declared complete until the Check stage confirms the target has been achieved. Toyota engineers reportedly complete thousands of A3 cycles per year across the global manufacturing network, creating a learning flywheel that sustains its operational excellence over decades.

**So what:** The PDCA cycle's power is in the 'Check' stage — it is the most commonly skipped. Organizations implement changes (Do) and assume success, never measuring whether the target was actually achieved. Making the Check stage explicit and mandatory is what separates sustained improvement from completed projects.

## Template
Complete one PDCA Record per improvement initiative. The Check stage must include actual measured data — not anecdotal feedback. The Act stage is only complete when the change has been documented as a standard and the rollout plan is confirmed.

## Pitfalls
- Skipping the Check stage: the most common PDCA failure is declaring success after the Do stage without measuring whether the change actually worked. The Check stage is mandatory — results must be measured against the specific target set in Plan.
- Root cause analysis that stops at symptoms: 5 Whys must drill to the actual root cause, not the first plausible explanation. If the first 'Why?' answer would have been obvious to anyone without analysis, you have not done root cause analysis.
- Piloting at unrepresentative scale: a pilot at the most cooperative, best-resourced site tells you nothing about how the change will perform at the average or worst site. Choose a pilot scope that is representative of the full rollout population.
- PDCA as a one-time project tool rather than a continuous management discipline: PDCA's value compounds over time through accumulated learning. Organizations that apply it project-by-project but do not embed it in their ongoing management cadence lose most of its benefit.

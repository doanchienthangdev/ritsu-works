# Supply Chain Vision-Mission-Values Canvas

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A one-page structured canvas for articulating the supply chain function's aspirational direction (Vision), its daily purpose (Mission), and the behavioural principles that guide decision-making when trade-offs arise (Values). Used as the foundation of Phase I before objectives and KPIs are set.

**Origin:** Adapted from Collins & Porras (Built to Last, 1994) and the Simon Sinek Golden Circle (Start With Why, 2009), customized for supply chain strategy contexts by Domont Consulting and other tier-1 operations consultancies.

## Why it works
Strategy without direction is a list of initiatives without a north star. The Vision-Mission-Values canvas forces leadership to answer three different questions — where are we going (vision), what do we do every day to get there (mission), and how do we behave when no one is watching (values) — before any objectives or KPIs are defined. Without this foundation, KPI targets can be set that are internally contradictory (e.g., maximize cost savings AND maximize service level without defining which wins when they conflict). The Values statement is the most operationally important element: it is the algorithm that resolves trade-off decisions at the frontline without requiring escalation.

## When to use
Use in Phase I Step 2, immediately after the strategic implications analysis is complete. The V/M/V is the bridge between 'what corporate strategy requires of us' and 'what specific objectives and KPIs we will be held accountable for.'

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Prepare the workshop: send participants (CSCO, VPs of each pillar, 1–2 business unit leaders) the Phase I implications table and 3 examples of world-class supply chain vision statements (Amazon, Unilever, IKEA) 48 hours before the session.
2. 2. Draft the Vision in the workshop: present the strategic implications table. Ask: 'Given these implications, what should our supply chain be known for in 5 years?' Brainstorm 10–15 candidate statements. Cluster into themes. Vote to select the top 3. Refine into one sentence that is: (a) aspirational but achievable, (b) specific to your industry context, (c) resonant with the customer value proposition.
3. 3. Draft the Mission: ask 'What does our supply chain do every day to make the Vision real?' The Mission describes the function's operating purpose — it is present-tense, action-oriented, and describes the supply chain's role in the business ecosystem.
4. 4. Define the Values: ask 'When our operations teams face trade-off decisions — speed vs. cost, service vs. inventory, short-term efficiency vs. long-term resilience — what principles should guide them?' Draft 3–5 values, each with a one-sentence behavioural definition. Values must be specific enough to generate different decisions in a real trade-off scenario. 'Customer first' is not a value — it is a platitude. 'Customer fill-rate takes precedence over inventory efficiency in every trade-off decision' is a value.
5. 5. Test with real scenarios: present 2–3 real recent supply chain trade-off decisions to the group. Ask: 'If our Values had been in place, would we have decided differently?' If the answer is 'No' — the values are not differentiated enough. If 'Yes' — document the scenario as an example for the values communication.
6. 6. Circulate for input: share the draft V/M/V statement with the broader supply chain leadership team (all directors and managers) for 1 week of comment. Incorporate substantive edits; preserve the intent.
7. 7. Gain executive sign-off: present the final V/M/V to the CEO or COO for endorsement alongside the CSCO. Dual sign-off signals that the supply chain strategy is a business strategy, not just a functional plan.
8. 8. Publish and embed: publish the V/M/V on the supply chain intranet, in all-hands presentations, and in the governance documents for Phase V. Reference it explicitly whenever a cross-pillar trade-off arises.

## Real-life example — IKEA
IKEA's supply chain vision — to use sustainable, cost-effective, and responsible sourcing and manufacturing to offer well-designed home furnishings at prices that many people can afford — is directly embedded in every supply chain design decision. The 'many people can afford' constraint is a value that explicitly governs trade-offs: IKEA will redesign a product rather than allow a cost increase that would push the price above the target. This is not corporate aspiration — it is an operational decision rule that supply chain engineers use when choosing materials, production locations, and logistics designs. The vision-mission-values framework works when values are specific enough to be decision rules, not inspiration.

**So what:** The test of a good Value statement is whether a frontline operations manager, facing a real trade-off, would make a different decision because of it. If the Value does not change decisions, it is decorative.

## Pitfalls
- Generic values that apply to every company: 'Integrity, Excellence, Innovation' are not supply chain values — they are corporate boilerplate. Values must be specific enough to resolve a real supply chain trade-off differently from the default outcome.
- Vision statements that are aspirational without being measurable: 'World-class supply chain' is a vision that cannot be tracked. Add a measurable dimension: 'Top-quartile OTIF and the lowest cost-to-serve in our peer group by 2027.'
- Process without conviction: the V/M/V canvas only works if the CSCO and executive team genuinely believe the Vision and are willing to make resource decisions that advance it. A V/M/V created to satisfy a consulting deliverable and filed away is worse than no V/M/V — it creates cynicism.
- Setting Values before understanding the strategic implications: values must be designed to resolve the specific trade-offs that arise from the organization's strategy. Completing Phase I Step 1 (implications analysis) before the V/M/V workshop ensures the values are grounded in real operational context.

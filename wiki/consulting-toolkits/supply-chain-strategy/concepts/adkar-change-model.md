# ADKAR Change Model

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
ADKAR is an individual-centric change management model with five sequential milestones: Awareness (of the need to change), Desire (to participate and support the change), Knowledge (of how to change), Ability (to implement new skills and behaviours), and Reinforcement (to sustain the change). An organization changes only when a critical mass of individuals have moved through all five stages.

**Origin:** Developed by Jeff Hiatt, founder of Prosci, first published in 2003. The model emerged from Prosci's research into why change management interventions succeeded or failed across hundreds of organizations.

## Why it works
Most change management models focus on the organizational or process level (phases, milestones, readiness). ADKAR is deliberately individual-centric because organizations do not change — people change. A supply chain transformation that deploys a new TMS perfectly on schedule still fails if logistics managers continue using workaround spreadsheets. By diagnosing precisely where each individual or group is stuck in the ADKAR sequence, managers can apply targeted interventions rather than generic communications. The sequential nature matters: you cannot build Knowledge before Desire, and Ability before Knowledge.

## When to use
Use ADKAR whenever a supply chain transformation requires significant behavioural change — new processes, new systems, new roles, or new ways of working. It is especially effective for technology implementations (ERP, TMS, IBP), lean/operational excellence rollouts, and supplier relationship model changes.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Identify all stakeholder groups impacted by the change (e.g., planning team, plant operators, procurement managers, distribution staff, IT).
2. 2. Diagnose the current ADKAR position for each group: conduct structured interviews or brief pulse surveys asking questions mapped to each ADKAR stage (e.g., 'Do you understand why this change is happening? On a scale of 1–5, how motivated are you to make this change work?').
3. 3. Identify the 'barrier point' for each group — the first ADKAR stage where the score falls below 3 out of 5. All interventions must address the barrier point first; providing Knowledge to a group that lacks Desire is ineffective.
4. 4. Design interventions targeted at each group's barrier point: Awareness → CEO/CSCO communications explaining the burning platform; Desire → leadership conversations, peer testimonials, site visits to best-practice operations; Knowledge → training programmes, coaching, standard operating procedures; Ability → supervised practice, go-live support, coaching during the first weeks of use; Reinforcement → performance management alignment, recognition systems, public celebration of milestones.
5. 5. Build a Change Champion network: identify 1–2 respected individuals per functional area who will model the change and provide peer-level support. Champions address Desire and Ability gaps far more effectively than external consultants.
6. 6. Track progress on a Change Readiness Scorecard: at 30, 60, and 90 days post-intervention, re-assess ADKAR scores for each group. Visualize as a heat map (group × ADKAR stage × score).
7. 7. Escalate persistent resistance to the Steering Committee: if a group remains blocked at Desire after 2 intervention cycles, the root cause is typically a structural concern (job security, performance management, incentive misalignment) that requires executive intervention.
8. 8. Close the ADKAR loop at go-live: Reinforcement mechanisms (recognition programs, KPI linkage, new routines in team meetings) must be in place before go-live to prevent post-go-live drift.

## Real-life example — Nestlé
Nestlé's GLOBE program (Global Business Excellence, 2000–2010) was a $2.4B ERP and process standardization initiative across 150+ countries. The change management approach was explicitly ADKAR-based (though Nestlé branded it differently). Awareness was built through a 'burning platform' narrative: fragmented systems were costing Nestlé €200M/year in unnecessary complexity. Desire was built through a Change Champion network of 2,000 'Local Project Leaders' embedded in each country — peers who vouched for the change. Knowledge was delivered through 4 million hours of training. Ability was built through a 'go-live buddy' system pairing experienced users with new users for the first 90 days. Reinforcement was achieved by embedding system usage into performance reviews. The result: Nestlé standardized 70% of its global processes within 10 years — one of the largest ERP transformations ever successfully completed.

**So what:** The Change Champion network is the most underrated element of ADKAR. External communications and training matter, but peer-level support is what moves people from Knowledge to Ability. Budget for it explicitly.

## Pitfalls
- Skipping Desire and going straight to Knowledge: launching training before the audience understands why the change is necessary and wants to participate results in low retention and active non-compliance. The sequential logic of ADKAR is non-optional.
- Over-relying on email communications for Awareness: a CEO email is not sufficient to build Awareness. Two-way conversations (town halls, Q&A sessions, manager cascade discussions) are required so people can process and ask questions.
- No Change Champions: organizations that rely on a central change team without a Change Champion network fail to reach frontline workers effectively. Champions must be respected peers, not HR-appointed representatives.
- Forgetting Reinforcement: most transformation programs put all their effort into Awareness through Ability and then declare success at go-live. Without reinforcement mechanisms, 60–70% of behaviors revert within 6 months.

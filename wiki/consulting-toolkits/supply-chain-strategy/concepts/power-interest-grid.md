# Power-Interest Grid (Stakeholder Mapping)

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A 2×2 stakeholder mapping tool that classifies all individuals and groups affected by a transformation on two dimensions — power to influence outcomes and level of interest in the change — to guide the design of differentiated stakeholder engagement strategies.

**Origin:** First published by Aubrey Mendelow, 'Environmental Scanning — The Impact of the Stakeholder Concept,' Cambridge, MA, 1981. Popularized in management practice by Johnson & Scholes (Exploring Corporate Strategy, 1992). Standard in McKinsey, BCG, and Bain change management methodologies.

## Why it works
Not all stakeholders require the same engagement effort. Treating the board chair and a frontline warehouse operator with the same communication approach wastes resources on one and neglects the other. The grid makes engagement resource allocation decisions explicit: high-power/high-interest stakeholders (Manage Closely) require individual relationship management; high-power/low-interest stakeholders (Keep Satisfied) need periodic updates and must be brought in when decisions affect them; low-power/high-interest (Keep Informed) need regular communications to avoid anxiety and rumours; low-power/low-interest (Monitor) are managed through standard communications.

## When to use
Use at the start of Phase VI (Change Management) and update throughout the transformation. Also use at any major milestone (technology go-live, organizational restructuring, supplier transition) when the stakeholder landscape shifts significantly.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Identify all stakeholders: brainstorm all individuals and groups (internal: C-suite, business unit leaders, functional managers, frontline employees, unions/works councils; external: key suppliers, 3PL partners, major customers, regulatory bodies, investors) who are affected by the supply chain transformation.
2. 2. Assess Power: rate each stakeholder's power to influence transformation outcomes on a High/Medium/Low scale. Power sources include: formal authority (can approve/veto decisions), resource control (controls budget, headcount, or systems), informal influence (respected by peers or the executive team), and coalition-building capability.
3. 3. Assess Interest: rate each stakeholder's level of interest in the transformation. Interest is the degree to which the transformation affects them: directly impacts their work processes/role (High), indirectly impacts their function's outputs (Medium), or minimal direct impact (Low).
4. 4. Plot each stakeholder on the 2×2. For groups (e.g., 'plant managers in Region A'), create one bubble representing the group, sized by headcount.
5. 5. Assess current sentiment for all 'Manage Closely' and 'Keep Satisfied' stakeholders: are they Champions, Supporters, Neutral, Skeptics, or Blockers? Document in the stakeholder register.
6. 6. Define engagement strategies per quadrant, then individualize for all 'Manage Closely' stakeholders — each should have a named executive relationship owner, a tailored engagement plan, and a target sentiment by each transformation milestone.
7. 7. Identify early advocates (Champions in the Manage Closely quadrant) — these individuals become the transformation's public sponsors and change enablers. Engage them in shaping decisions, not just receiving communication.
8. 8. Monitor for shifts: update the grid quarterly. A plant manager who was initially neutral may become a Blocker when their team is disrupted by a new system go-live. Early detection allows preemptive intervention.

## Real-life example — Siemens
During Siemens' supply chain digital transformation (2018–2023), the stakeholder mapping exercise revealed a critical pattern: CFOs of individual business units sat in the 'Manage Closely' quadrant (high power, high interest) and were uniformly rated 'Skeptic.' Their concern: the centralized supply chain investment required diverting BU capital budgets. The engagement strategy was redesigned around CFO-specific sessions showing BU-level NPV decomposition — how much of the transformation's total €500M benefit pool accrued to each BU. Within 60 days, 7 of 9 BU CFOs shifted from Skeptic to Supporter. Without the Power-Interest Grid to identify these stakeholders as the critical group, the project team would have continued the standard all-hands communications approach and encountered sustained BU-level resistance throughout implementation.

**So what:** The Power-Interest Grid is most valuable for surfacing the 'silent Blockers' — high-power stakeholders who have low visibility but can derail a transformation through budget vetos or political opposition. Identify them early; engage them individually.

## Pitfalls
- Forgetting external stakeholders: supply chain transformations always involve major changes to how suppliers, 3PLs, and customers interact with your systems. Treating the Power-Interest Grid as internal-only misses critical external relationship risks.
- Assuming power is fixed: power dynamics change during a transformation. A new CEO appointment, a union election, or a restructuring announcement can dramatically shift who holds power. Update the grid quarterly.
- Confusing communication with engagement: 'Keep Satisfied' stakeholders receive updates — but they must also be consulted on decisions that affect them before the decision is made, not after. Surprises convert Satisfieds into Blockers.
- One-size-fits-all communication for the 'Manage Closely' group: the highest-power, highest-interest stakeholders require individually tailored engagement — not the same all-hands message as everyone else.

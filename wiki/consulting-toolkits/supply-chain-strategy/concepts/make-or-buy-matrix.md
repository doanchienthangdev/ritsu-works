# Make-or-Buy Matrix

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A 2×2 strategic decision framework that classifies supply chain activities on two dimensions — strategic importance and operational capability — to determine whether each activity should be performed in-house (Make), outsourced (Buy), or developed through partnership (Build/Partner).

**Origin:** Synthesized from transaction cost economics (Oliver Williamson, 1975), resource-based view of the firm (Barney, 1991), and core competency theory (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990). Applied in supply chain strategy by McKinsey, Bain, and major operations consulting practices.

## Why it works
Not all activities a company performs are equally strategic. Core competence theory (Prahalad & Hamel) argues that sustainable competitive advantage comes from activities where the firm has unique, hard-to-imitate capabilities. Non-core activities, even when performed competently, dilute management attention and capital. The Make-or-Buy Matrix operationalizes this by forcing an explicit two-dimensional classification — separating 'important' from 'important and we're best-in-class at it' — and prescribing the right governance model for each cell.

## When to use
Use in Phase I of supply chain strategy design to establish the operating model boundaries, and whenever the organization is considering: (1) large-scale outsourcing to a 3PL or contract manufacturer, (2) vertical integration of a currently outsourced activity, or (3) restructuring the supply chain network after an M&A event.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. List all material supply chain activities: compile a full list of activities performed by the supply chain function, grouped by SCOR process domain (Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return, Enable). Include both operational activities (running a warehouse, running production) and enabling activities (demand planning, procurement analytics, transportation management).
2. 2. Score each activity on Strategic Importance (1–5): an activity is strategically important if (a) it is a source of customer value proposition (e.g., speed, quality, customization), (b) it is difficult for competitors to replicate, or (c) losing control of it would expose a material business risk.
3. 3. Score each activity on Operational Capability (1–5): assess whether your organization performs this activity at best-in-class levels (peer benchmarks, cost-per-unit data, error rates, technology sophistication). Score 1 if you are clearly below median; 5 if you are demonstrably top-quartile.
4. 4. Plot each activity on the 2×2 matrix using the scores. Use bubble size to represent annual cost or resource intensity of the activity.
5. 5. For each quadrant, apply the prescribed decision: Keep In-House (top-right), Build/Partner (top-left), Selective Outsourcing (bottom-right), Outsource (bottom-left).
6. 6. Challenge the placements with the executive team: the most important quadrant is the 'Build or Partner' (top-left) — these are activities that are critical but where you are underperforming. They require either a capability-building program or a strategic partnership in your Phase III initiative list.
7. 7. Document the rationale for every decision in a Make-or-Buy Decision Log, including the key assumptions, the data supporting the capability score, and the review date.
8. 8. Define transition plans for the 'Outsource' decisions: a decision to outsource is not immediate — it requires 3PL RFP, contract negotiation, knowledge transfer, and a service-level agreement.

## Real-life example — Nike
Nike's legendary asset-light supply chain model is a textbook Make-or-Buy decision executed at scale. Nike assessed its activity portfolio in the 1970s–1980s and concluded that design, marketing, and brand management were top-right (Keep In-House): strategically critical and world-class execution. Manufacturing, however, was assessed as top-left (Build/Partner — eventually Outsource): strategically important for quality and speed, but Nike could not achieve manufacturing best-in-class economics at scale in the US. The decision: outsource manufacturing entirely to a network of contracted factories in Asia. By 2023, Nike owned zero factories but managed 300+ contract manufacturing partners through stringent supplier standards (Nike Manufacturing Index). The strategic clarity of the Make-or-Buy decision freed Nike to invest its capital in the activities that actually create competitive advantage.

**So what:** Make-or-Buy decisions are not permanent — they depend on the relative capability of the firm vs. market alternatives. As manufacturing capabilities in offshore markets improved, Nike's 'outsource manufacturing' decision became more correct over time, not less.

## Pitfalls
- Outsourcing strategically important activities to reduce short-term cost: outsourcing activities that belong in the 'Keep In-House' quadrant to chase cost savings is the most common and most damaging Make-or-Buy error. Once the capability is transferred to a 3PL or contract manufacturer, rebuilding it in-house takes years.
- Not reassessing as the market evolves: a decision to keep in-house is only correct as long as the organization remains best-in-class. If a 3PL or technology provider achieves dramatically superior capability (e.g., AI-enabled warehouse operations), the decision should be revisited.
- Conflating 'we have always done it' with 'we are best-in-class': many activities are kept in-house out of organizational inertia, not strategic logic. The capability scoring step must be based on external benchmarks, not internal perception.
- Ignoring switching costs in outsourcing decisions: the analysis must include the one-time cost of transitioning an activity to an outsourcer (knowledge transfer, redundancy, IT integration) — not just the ongoing operational cost comparison.

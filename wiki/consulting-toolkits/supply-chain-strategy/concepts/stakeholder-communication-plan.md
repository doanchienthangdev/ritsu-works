# Stakeholder Communication Plan

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A structured multi-audience communication schedule that maps key transformation messages to audience segments, delivery channels, frequency, and ownership — ensuring that all stakeholders receive timely, consistent, and relevant information throughout the supply chain transformation.

**Origin:** Standard in organizational change management practice (Prosci, CIPD), public relations methodology, and McKinsey/BCG communications design for large-scale transformation programs.

## Why it works
Communication vacuums in a transformation fill with rumour, speculation, and resistance. A structured communication plan prevents this by making the communication intent explicit: who needs to know what, when, through which channel, and who is accountable for delivering it. Critically, the plan distinguishes between different audience segments — the Steering Committee needs strategic progress; frontline warehouse operators need to know how their daily work will change and when. One-size-fits-all communications fail both audiences. The plan also ensures feedback mechanisms (pulse surveys, Q&A sessions, suggestion boxes) are built in — communication is a two-way process.

## When to use
Build the initial communication plan in Phase VI Step 3, running alongside the ADKAR planning. Update at every major transformation milestone. Also use whenever a significant change in scope, timeline, or leadership occurs that requires stakeholder re-engagement.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Identify all audience segments: group stakeholders from the Power-Interest Grid into communication audiences based on information needs and preferred channels. Typical segments: Steering Committee, senior leadership (Directors+), middle management (Managers), frontline employees (operations staff, planners, buyers), key suppliers, external partners.
2. 2. Define message categories by audience: for each audience, identify the 3–5 categories of information they need to receive (e.g., strategic context, initiative-specific impact on their work, timeline and milestones, resources and support available, progress and wins).
3. 3. Select channels: match channels to audience preferences and message formality — CEO/CSCO video for all-staff Awareness building, town halls for interactive Q&A, manager cascade briefings for team-level impact, intranet for reference information, email for formal notifications, digital dashboards for ongoing progress tracking.
4. 4. Set frequency: Steering Committee — monthly formal update + ad hoc as needed; senior leadership — biweekly update; middle management — monthly cascade briefing + transformation newsletter; frontline — quarterly town hall + manager briefings; suppliers — quarterly partner update.
5. 5. Assign communication owners: each communication must have one named owner responsible for drafting, reviewing, and delivering it. The PMO Communication Lead coordinates the calendar; individual owners deliver their communications.
6. 6. Design feedback mechanisms: for each audience, build in at least one feedback channel — Steering Committee: open discussion at monthly meeting; senior leadership: monthly open Q&A session with CSCO; frontline: pulse survey (5 questions, monthly), suggestion box (physical and digital), team leader feedback at manager cascade.
7. 7. Create the content calendar: map all planned communications onto a monthly calendar for the first 12 months. Align communication milestones with transformation milestones (e.g., system go-live announcements, milestone achievement celebrations, phase transitions).
8. 8. Measure communication effectiveness: track open rates for emails/newsletters, attendance at town halls, pulse survey completion rates and sentiment scores. Adjust channels and frequency based on what is resonating — and what is not.

## Real-life example — Diageo
Diageo's global supply chain transformation (2016–2020) established a communication architecture that was explicitly differentiated by audience. For the 6,000 frontline employees impacted (distillery workers, logistics operators, planners), Diageo designed a 'Big Picture' communication — a visual one-pager showing the supply chain before and after the transformation, using plain language and no financial jargon, translated into 12 languages. For senior leadership, a monthly digital dashboard showed the transformation KPI trajectory in real time. For key suppliers, Diageo created a 'Partner Update' — a quarterly email with relevant supply chain changes, timeline implications, and the specific asks of each supplier partner. The differentiated approach was credited in the post-program evaluation as a key factor in achieving 84% employee 'Ability' stage ADKAR scores by go-live — significantly above the 70% industry benchmark for transformations of comparable scale.

**So what:** The most impactful communication investment is in the frontline employee audience — they are simultaneously the most impacted, the most often neglected in communication design, and the group whose behaviour change determines whether the transformation's benefits are realized.

## Template
Complete the communication plan for the first 12 months of the transformation. Every planned communication must have a named owner, a defined channel, and a feedback mechanism. Update the plan quarterly.

## Pitfalls
- Communication planning as a one-time exercise: the communication plan must be updated at every major milestone — system go-live, organizational restructuring, leadership change. A plan written at Month 1 and never updated becomes irrelevant by Month 6.
- One-way communication only: communications that inform but do not invite response create an illusion of transparency. Feedback mechanisms are not optional — they are the early-warning system for emerging resistance.
- Jargon-heavy communications for frontline audiences: business transformation communications are typically written by people who are immersed in the transformation's language. 'Moving to Level 4 IBP maturity' means nothing to a warehouse picker. Translate every message for the specific audience before it is sent.
- Over-communicating to senior leaders and under-communicating to frontline: the communication plan should invest proportional energy in frontline communications, even though senior leaders are the easiest audience to reach.

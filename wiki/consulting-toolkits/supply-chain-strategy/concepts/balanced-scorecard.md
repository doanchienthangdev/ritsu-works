# Balanced Scorecard

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A strategy measurement and management framework that translates vision and strategy into four linked performance perspectives — Financial, Customer, Internal Process, and Learning & Growth — each with objectives, measures, targets, and initiatives.

**Origin:** Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton, Harvard Business Review, January–February 1992. Expanded into a full management system in their 1996 book 'The Balanced Scorecard.'

## Why it works
Financial metrics are lag indicators — they tell you what happened, not what will happen. The Balanced Scorecard adds three leading perspectives: Customer (how must we appear to customers to achieve our financial goals?), Internal Process (what processes must we excel at to satisfy customers and shareholders?), and Learning & Growth (what capabilities and culture do we need to excel at our key processes?). The linkage between perspectives creates a 'strategy map' — a causal chain from intangible assets (people, systems, culture) to financial outcomes.

## When to use
Use when translating a supply chain vision or strategy into a concrete set of measurable objectives — especially when the leadership team needs to align on what 'success' looks like across financial, customer, operational, and capability dimensions simultaneously.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Start with the Financial perspective: define 2–3 financial objectives that represent success for the supply chain in the 3-year horizon (e.g., 'Reduce supply chain cost per unit by 15%,' 'Improve working capital by reducing inventory days from 60 to 42').
2. 2. Define the Customer perspective: what outcomes must the supply chain deliver to internal and external customers for the financial objectives to be achieved? (e.g., '97% OTIF,' '60-day time-to-market for new SKUs,' '99% order accuracy').
3. 3. Define the Internal Process perspective: what supply chain processes must perform at a step-change level to deliver the customer outcomes? Focus on 3–5 breakthrough process objectives (e.g., 'Demand forecast accuracy ≥88%,' 'OEE ≥80% across anchor plants,' 'Supplier on-time delivery ≥96%').
4. 4. Define the Learning & Growth perspective: what capabilities, systems, and culture are required to achieve the process objectives? (e.g., 'Deploy AI demand-sensing tool,' 'Train 80% of supply chain workforce in lean principles,' 'Achieve Level 4 digital maturity in S&OP').
5. 5. For each objective in all four perspectives, define: the specific KPI that measures progress, the current baseline, the 3-year target, and the initiative that will close the gap.
6. 6. Draw the strategy map: connect each Learning & Growth objective to the Internal Process objective it enables; connect each Internal Process objective to the Customer outcome it drives; connect each Customer outcome to the Financial result it generates. If you cannot draw the causal chain, you may be missing an objective.
7. 7. Present the scorecard to the executive team for validation: does it tell a coherent story from capability investment to financial return? Are there any missing objectives that break the causal chain?
8. 8. Use the scorecard as a monthly management tool — review KPI results, discuss variance, and update initiatives as needed.

## Real-life example — Unilever
Unilever's supply chain transformation (2010–2015, under Keith Weed's sustainable living plan) used a balanced-scorecard-style framework to align supply chain performance with both financial and sustainability objectives. The Financial perspective targeted €1B in cost savings. The Customer perspective targeted service levels and shelf availability improvements across 190 countries. The Internal Process perspective targeted manufacturing waste reduction and energy efficiency (e.g., zero waste-to-landfill from manufacturing sites by 2020). The Learning & Growth perspective targeted building sustainable-sourcing capability across the procurement function. By making sustainability a first-class scorecard objective — not a separate CSR report — Unilever was able to track and deliver on it with the same rigour as cost and service.

**So what:** The power of the Balanced Scorecard is the discipline of making the causal chain explicit. When sustainability or resilience objectives are added alongside financial ones, they get managed — not just reported.

## Pitfalls
- Too many objectives: organizations often put 20–30 objectives on the scorecard, making it unmanageable. Restrict to 4–8 total — if everything is strategic, nothing is.
- Metrics without baselines: a target of '95% OTIF' is meaningless without knowing the current level. Always establish the baseline before setting the target.
- The scorecard as a measurement tool only: Kaplan & Norton intended it as a management system — strategy review meetings should use it to discuss what to change, not just to report what happened.
- Missing the strategy map: without drawing the causal connections between perspectives, the scorecard is just four lists of KPIs with no internal logic.

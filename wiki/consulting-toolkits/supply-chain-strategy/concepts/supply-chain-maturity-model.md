# Supply Chain Maturity Model

*Category: diagnosis · Toolkit: Supply Chain Strategy*

## What it is
A structured capability assessment framework that rates a supply chain's people, process, technology, and data dimensions on a 1–5 maturity scale across each of the four functional pillars (Demand & Supply Planning, Sourcing & Procurement, Manufacturing, Logistics & Distribution), identifying current state gaps vs. best-in-class.

**Origin:** Derived from CMM (Capability Maturity Model, SEI, Carnegie Mellon, 1991) and adapted for supply chain by firms including Gartner (Supply Chain Planning Maturity Model), ASCM/SCOR, Deloitte, and AT Kearney. Multiple proprietary versions exist; the structure below synthesizes the consensus model used by the major consulting firms.

## Why it works
Supply chain capability is not binary — organizations progress through predictable stages from reactive firefighting to integrated, data-driven excellence. The maturity model makes this progression explicit, allowing leadership to: (1) accurately diagnose where they are today (not where they think they are), (2) define where they need to be in 3 years given strategic objectives, and (3) identify the specific capability gaps that the Phase III initiative list must close. Critically, the model reveals that technology investment without process and people maturity rarely delivers full value.

## When to use
Use at the start of Phase II to establish a rigorous, evidence-based baseline for each functional pillar. Revisit every 12–18 months to track improvement. Especially valuable when executive self-perception of capability is misaligned with operational reality.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Form an assessment team: include the pillar lead, 2–3 SMEs from within the function, and an external benchmark contributor (consultant or industry peer).
2. 2. Select the assessment dimensions: for each pillar, assess four dimensions — (a) People & Skills (talent depth, capability building, organizational design), (b) Process Design (process documentation, standardization, efficiency), (c) Technology & Systems (system coverage, integration, automation), (d) Data & Analytics (data quality, KPI tracking, analytics sophistication).
3. 3. Score each dimension on the 1–5 maturity scale using the level descriptors above as anchors. Do not average — each dimension gets its own score. Validate scores with artifact evidence (process maps, system screenshots, KPI reports).
4. 4. Conduct structured interviews with 5–10 stakeholders per pillar to validate or challenge the initial scoring. Triangulate: the function leader's self-assessment is typically 0.5–1 level above the objective evidence.
5. 5. Plot the results on a spider/radar chart: one axis per dimension, showing current score vs. best-in-class (Level 4 for most dimensions; Level 5 is aspirational for only 1–2 dimensions per pillar).
6. 6. Define the target maturity level per dimension based on Phase I strategic objectives — not all dimensions need to reach Level 4 or 5. Prioritize dimensions that directly enable the strategic objectives.
7. 7. Calculate the maturity gap per dimension: gap = target level − current level. Dimensions with gap ≥2 are high priority for Phase III initiative generation.
8. 8. Document the maturity narrative per pillar: write a 1-page summary of strengths (current maturity ≥3) and critical gaps (current maturity <2) with specific examples.

## Real-life example — Maersk
A.P. Møller-Maersk's supply chain integration journey (2014–2022) illustrates the maturity model in practice. In 2014, Maersk's logistics business assessed itself at Level 2–3 across most dimensions: functional siloes between container shipping, terminals, and landside logistics, limited data integration, and no cross-functional demand planning. The strategic target was Level 4 across all dimensions by 2020 — a unified logistics company with real-time customer visibility and data-driven operations. Maersk's acquisition of Hamburg Süd (2017) and series of logistics acquisitions (Damco, HUUB, Senator) moved the strategy into execution. By 2022, Maersk's integrated logistics platform served customers with end-to-end visibility — demonstrating a full Level 4 achievement across data and technology dimensions, though people and process maturity required sustained investment through the decade.

**So what:** Maturity assessments consistently show that technology jumps ahead of people and process maturity. Maersk's experience confirms: investing in data platforms and systems before the people and process dimensions reach Level 3 delays value realization and creates adoption failures.

## Pitfalls
- Self-assessment inflation: functions consistently rate themselves 0.5–1 level higher than objective evidence supports. Always triangulate with external benchmarks and artifact review — not just leadership interviews.
- Setting the same target maturity for all dimensions: not every dimension needs to reach Level 5. Over-investing in AI/ML planning tools before basic process standardization (Level 3) is in place is a common and costly mistake.
- One-time assessment without tracking: the maturity model is a diagnostic tool that should be re-run every 12–18 months to track progress and recalibrate targets.
- Ignoring the People dimension: technology maturity (new systems) almost always races ahead of people maturity (skill to use them). Maturity assessments that show Level 4 in Technology but Level 2 in People predict failed implementations.

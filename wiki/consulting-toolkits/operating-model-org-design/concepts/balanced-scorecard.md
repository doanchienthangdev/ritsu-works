# Balanced Scorecard

*Category: performance-management · Toolkit: Operating Model & Organization Design*

## What it is
A strategic performance management framework that translates strategy into a balanced set of measurable objectives across four perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Process, and Learning & Growth.

**Origin:** Robert Kaplan (Harvard Business School) and David Norton, 1992. Published in 'The Balanced Scorecard—Measures that Drive Performance' (HBR, Jan–Feb 1992) and expanded in their 1996 book.

## Why it works
Pure financial measurement is a lagging indicator—by the time revenue drops, the strategic problems that caused it (low customer satisfaction, broken processes, talent gaps) have been building for 12–24 months. The Balanced Scorecard adds leading indicators in the Customer, Internal Process, and Learning perspectives, giving leaders an earlier warning system and a more complete view of organizational health.

## When to use
Use in Phase 1 (define objectives and KPIs) and Phase 7 (design the strategic dashboard architecture).

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Confirm the corporate strategy in one sentence (the strategic theme).
2. For each of the four perspectives, run a workshop with the relevant stakeholders: 'What does success look like in this perspective if our strategy is working?'
3. Draft 3–5 objectives per perspective (12–20 total). Ensure a logical cause-and-effect chain: Learning → Internal Process → Customer → Financial.
4. For each objective, define one lead and one lag KPI, the current baseline, the target (end of year 3), and the measurement method.
5. Build a Strategy Map: a visual showing how objectives in each perspective cause objectives in the next perspective up.
6. Assign an owner for each objective. Embed the objectives in quarterly business reviews.
7. Review and update the scorecard annually as strategy evolves.

## Real-life example — Hilton Hotels
Hilton's adoption of the Balanced Scorecard in the early 2000s aligned 300,000 employees across 3,000 properties to a common strategic framework. The Learning & Growth perspective focused on hospitality training completion rates and employee engagement scores. These drove Internal Process improvements (room cleanliness, check-in speed) that drove Customer metric gains (guest satisfaction, repeat stays) that ultimately drove Financial results (RevPAR growth). The scorecard made it possible to trace a 1% improvement in employee engagement to a measurable RevPAR outcome.

**So what:** The Balanced Scorecard's most powerful feature is the Strategy Map—the causal chain from Learning to Financial. Without the map, the four perspectives are just four separate measurement systems.

## Pitfalls
- Creating a scorecard with 40+ KPIs—the point is focus, not comprehensiveness. Cap at 20 objectives and 25 KPIs.
- Leaving the Learning & Growth perspective thin because it is 'hard to measure'—this is the leading indicator for everything above it; invest in making it measurable.
- Using the scorecard only for annual reviews—it should drive the monthly operational rhythm.

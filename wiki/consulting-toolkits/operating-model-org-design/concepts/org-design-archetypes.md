# Organizational Design Archetypes

*Category: design · Toolkit: Operating Model & Organization Design*

## What it is
A structured decision framework for selecting among four fundamental organizational structures—Functional, Divisional, Matrix, and Network/Team-of-Teams—each with distinct advantages, disadvantages, and strategic fit conditions.

**Origin:** Synthesized from Alfred Chandler's 'Structure Follows Strategy' (1962), Jay Galbraith's Star Model (1973), and Stanley McChrystal's 'Team of Teams' (2015).

## Why it works
No structure is universally best. The right archetype depends on where the organization derives its competitive advantage: efficiency (functional), product/market focus (divisional), dual accountability (matrix), or speed and adaptability (network). Misaligning structure and strategy is the single most common cause of reorganization failure.

Use in Phase 2, Step 2 (Structure & Governance pillar design) as the primary design decision framework.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the 5–7 most important organizational capabilities required by the strategy (e.g., speed to market, cost leadership, customer intimacy, innovation).
2. Score each of the four archetypes against each capability requirement using a 1–3 scale (1=poor fit, 3=strong fit). Sum the scores.
3. Identify the top-scoring archetype as the primary structure. Note the archetype's key weakness and design mitigations for it.
4. Select a hybrid approach if needed: e.g., a primarily functional structure with cross-functional product teams (a light matrix) to preserve efficiency while improving coordination.
5. Design the first three levels of the org chart within the selected archetype: define each unit's mandate, key activities, decision rights, and reporting relationships.
6. Define the coordination mechanisms: governance committees, integrating roles (e.g., Chief of Staff), shared services, and rotation programs.
7. Validate the design against the spans-and-layers benchmark: managers should have 6–10 direct reports; organizations with fewer than 1,000 employees rarely need more than 5 organizational levels.

## Real-life example — Spotify
Spotify designed its 'Squad Model'—a variant of the Network/Team-of-Teams archetype—around autonomous squads (8–12 people, cross-functional) organized by feature area (e.g., Search, Playlists, Mobile Client). Tribes (collections of squads working on related areas), Chapters (functional communities of practice), and Guilds (interest-based networks) provided horizontal coordination without destroying squad autonomy. This enabled Spotify to scale from 200 to 5,000+ employees while maintaining the speed of a startup.

**So what:** The Network/Team-of-Teams archetype succeeds only when combined with strong shared purpose, cultural cohesion, and explicit coordination mechanisms (Chapters/Guilds)—autonomy without alignment becomes chaos.

## Pitfalls
- Defaulting to a matrix structure because 'we need both functional depth and product responsiveness'—matrix structures have the highest management overhead and work only when leaders have strong conflict-resolution skills.
- Designing the structure around current people rather than future strategic requirements—'the org chart should reflect the strategy, not the team roster'.
- Skipping the coordination mechanism design—structure alone doesn't integrate the organization; integrating roles, governance bodies, and shared services do the work.

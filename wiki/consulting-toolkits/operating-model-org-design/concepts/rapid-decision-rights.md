# RAPID Decision-Rights Framework

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Operating Model & Organization Design*

## What it is
A tool for clarifying who plays which role in any significant decision: Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, and Decide. RAPID eliminates the ambiguity that causes slow decision-making and accountability gaps.

**Origin:** Developed by Rogers and Blenko at Bain & Company. Published in Harvard Business Review, January 2006: 'Who Has the D?'

## Why it works
In most organizations, the biggest decision-making problem is not the quality of decisions but the speed and clarity of who decides. RAPID separates the 'Decide' role (one person, clearly accountable) from the 'Input' and 'Agree' roles (consulted but not blocking). This prevents the common failure of decisions by committee (no one accountable) and decisions by fiat (no one consulted).

## When to use
Use in Phase 2 (Structure & Governance pillar) for all significant cross-functional decisions. Re-validate when the organizational structure changes.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List the 10–15 most important recurring decisions in the organization (e.g., capital allocation above $1M, hiring at Director+ level, pricing changes, new product launch).
2. For each decision, identify the current pain point: Too slow? No accountability? Conflict between functions?
3. Assign RAPID roles for each decision: start with D (one person only), then R (who builds the recommendation), then I (who must be consulted), then A (who has veto—use sparingly), then P (who executes).
4. Validate the RAPID assignments with the people in each role: do they agree with their level of involvement? Are there gaps or overlaps?
5. Publish the RAPID register and train all involved parties on the framework. Clarify that 'Input' does not mean approval.
6. Run a 90-day pilot: track decision cycle times before and after. Target: 30% reduction in decision cycle time for the top 10 decisions.

## Real-life example — Reckitt (formerly Reckitt Benckiser)
Reckitt used RAPID to re-architect decision rights following its merger with Mead Johnson. The key tension was between global brand strategy and local market execution. RAPID clarified that global brand teams had 'Recommend' and 'Input' rights on pricing, but local market leaders had 'Decide' rights within a defined band. This ended a pattern of extended price negotiations between global and local teams that had been adding 6–8 weeks to pricing decisions.

**So what:** RAPID works best when the 'Agree' role is used sparingly—every person with 'A' is a potential veto and a potential bottleneck. Design the Agree role only for decisions where a functional area bears disproportionate risk.

## Pitfalls
- Assigning 'Agree' to too many people—every A is a veto. Reserve it for situations where a function genuinely bears disproportionate risk from the decision.
- Using RAPID for tactical, daily decisions—it is designed for strategic and cross-functional decisions. Operational decisions should have simpler authority matrices.
- Publishing the RAPID register without training—people confuse 'Input' with 'Approval' and continue to slow decisions down.

# 9-Box Talent Grid

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Operating Model & Organization Design*

## What it is
A talent management tool that plots employees on a 3×3 matrix of performance (x-axis) vs. potential (y-axis) to classify talent into nine categories—from 'Underperformer' to 'Top Talent'—and drive differentiated talent development and succession decisions.

**Origin:** Developed by McKinsey & Company and GE in the 1970s as part of GE's strategic talent management system.

## Why it works
Not all employees contribute equally to the organization's strategic value. The 9-box forces a relative talent calibration, preventing the common bias of classifying everyone as 'good'. It identifies High Potentials who need accelerated development, Solid Performers who form the reliable backbone, and Underperformers who need support or managed exit. This segmentation enables differentiated investment of limited L&D and succession capital.

## When to use
Use in Phase 2 (Talent pillar design) and run as an annual HR calendar event thereafter.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define 'performance' using objective data: last 12-month performance rating, goal achievement %, and 360 feedback score. Define 'potential' using three criteria: learning agility, leadership behavior, and scope ambition.
2. Rate each employee 1–3 on performance and 1–3 on potential using calibrated manager assessments. Require evidence for any 3 rating.
3. Hold a talent calibration session with all managers and HR. Use the evidence to challenge assessments and ensure consistent standards—especially for potential, where manager biases are strongest.
4. Plot the resulting matrix. For each of the nine boxes, define the talent action: accelerate (Box 9), develop (Box 8), retain (Boxes 5/6), support (Boxes 2/3), or transition out (Box 1).
5. Identify critical roles and check whether Boxes 8 and 9 employees are in succession pipeline for those roles.
6. Build individual development plans for all Boxes 7, 8, and 9 within 30 days.
7. Repeat the calibration annually and measure movement—aim for 15–20% of employees improving one box per year.

## Real-life example — General Electric (GE)
GE's Session C process under Jack Welch was the most rigorous 9-box calibration in corporate history. Welch personally reviewed talent across 600+ senior leaders annually, using the 9-box to identify the top 20% (Stars), vital 70% (Core), and bottom 10% (who were managed out). GE's ability to deploy talent rapidly across its diverse business portfolio was directly attributed to this systematic mapping.

**So what:** The value of the 9-box is not the classification itself but the calibration conversation it forces—it prevents the grade inflation that makes talent data useless for succession planning.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing current performance with potential—a high performer in a narrow role may have low potential for broader leadership, and vice versa.
- Failing to calibrate across managers—without a cross-manager session, every manager's team will look like a Bell curve centered at 'good'.
- Using the 9-box as a punitive tool rather than a developmental one—Box 1 employees deserve a clear improvement plan before any exit conversation.

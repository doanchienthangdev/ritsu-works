# ADKAR Change Management Model

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Operating Model & Organization Design*

## What it is
A goal-oriented change management model that describes the five building blocks an individual must achieve for a change to be successfully adopted: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement.

**Origin:** Jeff Hiatt, founder of Prosci, developed ADKAR in 1998 based on research into 700+ organizations. Published as 'ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and our Community' (2006).

## Why it works
Change fails at the individual level before it fails at the organizational level. ADKAR is sequential—each building block is a prerequisite for the next. An employee who has Awareness (knows why change is happening) but lacks Desire (personally chooses to support it) will not sustain the change even if trained. This sequential logic diagnoses where resistance is truly located and targets interventions precisely.

## When to use
Use throughout Phase 6 (Change Management) and as a go/no-go assessment before each major go-live milestone.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. For each major stakeholder group (e.g., front-line managers, IT team, sales force), administer a 5-question ADKAR assessment: one question per building block, scored 1–5.
2. Identify the 'barrier point'—the first building block where average score drops below 3. All interventions should target this building block first.
3. Design targeted interventions: Awareness = communication campaigns and town halls; Desire = leadership endorsement, WIIFM messaging, early wins; Knowledge = training programs, job aids; Ability = coaching, practice opportunities, feedback loops; Reinforcement = recognition, incentive alignment, performance management.
4. Execute interventions over 4–6 weeks per wave, then reassess the ADKAR score.
5. Repeat until all five building blocks score ≥ 4 for ≥ 80% of the population before a major go-live.
6. At go-live, shift focus to Reinforcement: ensure the performance management system rewards the new behaviors and that managers are calling out examples of the new way of working.

## Real-life example — Ford Motor Company
During Ford's 2006–2009 turnaround under Alan Mulally, the change management approach followed ADKAR logic. Mulally used weekly Business Plan Reviews (BPR) to build Awareness of the company's dire financial situation (he showed real P&L data, not sanitized summaries). He personally championed the 'One Ford' vision to build Desire among a leadership team accustomed to protecting divisional turf. Structured training and process documentation built Knowledge; leadership coaching built Ability; and the BPR ritual itself served as weekly Reinforcement.

**So what:** ADKAR's power is that it separates the question 'Do they know what to do?' (Knowledge) from 'Can they do it?' (Ability)—a distinction that most training programs ignore, leading to knowledge that never converts into changed behavior.

## Pitfalls
- Jumping straight to Knowledge (training) without addressing Awareness or Desire—the most common change management mistake. Training people who don't want to change is waste.
- Treating ADKAR as a one-time measurement rather than a repeated diagnostic throughout the change journey.
- Applying a single change management plan to all stakeholder groups—different groups will have different barrier points and need different interventions.

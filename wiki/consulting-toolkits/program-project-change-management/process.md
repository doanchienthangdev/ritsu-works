# Program, Project & Change Management

**One-liner:** End-to-end execution system: program governance, traditional and agile project delivery, and structured change management for Fortune-100-grade results.

**Core value:** A practitioner who can run this toolkit can take any initiative from vague idea to closed project — building the business case, mobilising the team, managing risk and issues, running Agile sprints, and bringing people through the change — without relying on an external consultant.

---

## When to Use This Toolkit

Use this toolkit whenever an organisation needs to: (a) govern a portfolio of related projects as a program; (b) scope, justify, initiate, and close a single project using either traditional waterfall or Agile; or (c) help individuals and teams adapt to a business or technical change.

**Typical triggers:** Digital transformation, post-merger integration, ERP roll-out, operational improvement, new market entry, regulatory compliance overhaul, or any initiative that cuts across functions and requires deliberate stakeholder and change management.

**Audience:** Program Directors, Project Managers, Change Managers, PMO Leads, Transformation Officers, senior executives sponsoring major initiatives, and management consultants supporting these roles.

**The problem this toolkit solves:** Only 29% of projects succeed. The primary causes of failure are poor communication and change management (31%), unrealistic schedule and budget (18%), insufficient resource planning (18%), and poor requirements (10%). This toolkit addresses each root cause with a structured, executable process.

## Phase 1: Establish Program Governance — The Portfolio Foundation

**Goal:** Align all initiatives to strategy, prioritise with rigour, and create oversight structures that keep projects on track and visible to leadership.

**Key question:** Which initiatives should we fund, in what order, and how do we govern them as a coherent program?

### Step 1.1 — Identify and catalogue potential initiatives
Facilitate a structured ideation session using a two-axis scan: strategic fit versus value potential. Collect initiative names, sponsors, rough scope, and estimated benefit into an initiative register. Input: strategic plan, departmental pain-points. Output: initiative register with 10–30 candidates. Owner: PMO Lead.

### Step 1.2 — Build business cases for shortlisted initiatives
For each initiative clearing the initial strategic filter, build a one-page business case: situation, solution, objectives, KPIs, scope, team, and high-level financials (NPV, ROI, payback). Output: business cases for the top 5–10 initiatives.

### Step 1.3 — Prioritise using a scoring matrix
Score on four weighted dimensions: strategic alignment (30%), financial value (30%), ease of implementation (20%), risk (20%). Segment into Must-do / Do next / Defer / Drop. Output: prioritised portfolio.

### Step 1.4 — Build the program roadmap
Plot approved initiatives on a 12–36 month Gantt. Show milestones, dependencies, and resource peaks per business unit. Check for sequencing conflicts and over-allocation. Output: program roadmap.

### Steps 1.5–1.8 — Governance, dashboard, improvement, evaluation
Establish the Steering Committee cadence (monthly), PMO operating rhythm (weekly), RACI at program level, and escalation thresholds. Build a one-page RAG dashboard refreshed weekly. After each major milestone, run a 90-minute lessons-learned session. At program end, compare actuals against business cases and present findings to the Executive Committee.

**KPIs:** % initiatives with approved business cases; % milestones RAG Green; portfolio budget variance %; Steering Committee attendance.

**Gate:** Executive Committee approves the portfolio, roadmap, and governance before any project initiation.

## Phase 2: Build a Business Case That Gets Approved

**Goal:** Produce an executive-quality business case that quantifies value and secures funding.

**Key question:** Is this project worth the investment?

### Step 2.1 — Write the situation and proposed solution
A 3–5 sentence situation statement: current state, problem, root causes, proposed solution. Test: a new executive should understand in 60 seconds.

### Step 2.2 — Define SMART objectives and KPIs
Set 3–5 SMART objectives. For each, define 1–2 KPIs with baseline, target, and measurement frequency. Balance financial, operational, and customer KPIs.

### Step 2.3 — Define scope, approach, and team
Explicit In-Scope / Out-of-Scope lists. Delivery approach (Traditional vs. Agile). Project team org chart with FTE commitments.

### Step 2.4 — List key activities via WBS
Work Breakdown Structure at two levels: phases and major deliverables. 15–30 deliverables at business-case stage.

### Step 2.5 — Score strategic alignment
Rate alignment to 3–5 strategic priorities (High/Medium/Low). Add: cost of NOT doing the project.

### Step 2.6 — Build the financial model
Three-to-five-year cash flow: initial investment, project costs, revenue impact, cost savings. Compute NPV (at WACC), IRR, ROI, payback. Show base/upside/downside scenarios.

### Step 2.7 — Rate ease of implementation
Three dimensions: complexity, readiness, change burden. Score 1–5 on each. Any dimension rated Low is flagged as a risk.

**KPIs:** NPV > 0; payback within planning horizon; strategic alignment ≥ Medium on 3+ priorities; business case formally approved.

**Gate:** Executive Committee formally approves the business case and releases funding.

## Phase 3: Initiate the Project — No Ambiguity Before You Start

**Goal:** Stand up the project so every stakeholder and team member knows the objectives, their role, the plan, and the risks before the first day of execution.

### Step 3.1 — Write the project charter
One-page document: project name, executive summary, objectives, scope, milestones, budget, team, sponsor, and decision authority matrix. Must be signed by the sponsor — this is the project's constitution. Input: approved business case.

### Step 3.2 — Identify challenges and key success factors
Pre-mortem workshop (2 hours): imagine the project has failed in 12 months — what went wrong? Reverse failure modes to derive Key Success Factors. Top 5 of each. Input: charter and team knowledge.

### Step 3.3 — Build the RACI matrix
One deliverable or decision per row, one role per column. Mandatory rules: exactly one A per row, at least one R per row. Eliminates accountability ambiguity for 20–50 deliverables.

### Step 3.4 — Develop the stakeholder engagement strategy
Power/Interest grid: High Power/High Interest → manage closely (weekly). High Power/Low Interest → keep satisfied (monthly). Low Power/High Interest → keep informed. Low Power/Low Interest → monitor. Document preferred channels and key messages per stakeholder.

### Step 3.5 — Plan capacity and resources
For each project role: required skills, FTE %, start/end date, source (internal/hire/contractor). Build a resource histogram (demand vs. supply per month). Resolve over-allocation conflicts with functional managers before the project starts.

### Steps 3.6–3.8 — Risk and issue management
Define the risk management framework (Identify → Assess → Respond, ISO 31000 aligned). Populate the risk log: probability (1–5) × impact (1–5) = risk score. Build a 5×5 heat map. Set escalation thresholds (any High risk unresolved after 1 week → Steering Committee). Create the issue log for risks that have materialised.

**KPIs:** Charter signed before Day 1; 100% of RACI roles assigned to named individuals; risk log ≥15 entries; all High-power stakeholders contacted Week 1.

**Gate:** Kick-off meeting complete; charter signed; risk log live; all team members briefed.

## Phase 4: Execute and Close the Project — Discipline Through Delivery

**Goal:** Deliver scope, schedule, and budget with full transparency; close the project cleanly with lessons captured.

### Steps 4.1–4.2 — High-level and detailed project plans
High-level Gantt (phases, milestones, critical path — one page). Detailed plan (3–5 day task granularity; baselined in PPM tool). The baseline is the reference for variance tracking throughout.

### Step 4.3 — Weekly project dashboard
One-page status report: overall RAG + 2-sentence narrative, milestone tracker, budget (spent vs. planned), top 3 risks, top 3 issues, decisions needed, key wins. Distributed every Monday at 9am.

### Step 4.4 — Parking lot management
The parking lot captures scope additions and undecided items. Each entry: description, requester, date, priority, disposition. Reviewed at every steering meeting. Prevents scope creep by making the cost of additions explicit.

### Step 4.5 — Punch list (final 20%)
All remaining open items in the final phase: bugs, incomplete deliverables, sign-offs. Every item has an owner and a hard close date. Nothing ships until the punch list reaches zero or items are formally deferred.

### Steps 4.6–4.7 — Closure and post-project evaluation
Formal closure checklist: all deliverables accepted, contracts closed, team released, lessons learned captured, financials reconciled, knowledge transferred. Sponsor sign-off required. Post-project evaluation report (submitted within 30 days): actual vs. planned on scope/time/cost/quality/benefits.

**KPIs:** Schedule variance %; cost variance %; scope change requests vs. baseline; stakeholder satisfaction ≥4/5; benefits realisation at 6 months.

**Gate:** Sponsor signs closure document confirming all deliverables accepted.

## Phase 5: Agile Delivery — Inspect and Adapt Every Sprint

**Goal:** Deliver working product increments every 2–4 weeks, incorporating real stakeholder feedback so the end result reflects actual needs.

### The Three Roles
**Product Owner:** owns the backlog, prioritises by business value, accepts completed stories. **Scrum Master:** facilitates ceremonies, removes blockers (within 24 hours), coaches the team. **Development Team:** cross-functional, self-organising, 3–9 people.

### The Seven Ceremonies
1. **Product Backlog Grooming** (ongoing): user stories in 'As a [role], I want [feature] so that [benefit]' format. Top 20% of backlog must be 'Ready' (estimated, acceptance criteria written) before each sprint.

2. **Sprint Planning** (2–4 hours): select stories fitting team velocity. Commit to a sprint goal — one sentence. Break stories into tasks.

3. **Daily Stand-up** (15 minutes): What did I do yesterday? What will I do today? Any blockers? Blockers resolved within 24 hours by Scrum Master.

4. **Sprint Execution**: update burndown chart daily. If behind by Day 5, alert Product Owner to consider de-scoping.

5. **Sprint Review** (1–2 hours): demo working software. Product Owner accepts/rejects each story. Stakeholder feedback updates backlog.

6. **Potentially Shippable Increment**: deploy if release criteria met (performance, security, UAT passed). Document release notes.

7. **Sprint Retrospective** (60–90 minutes): Start/Stop/Continue. Select 1–2 improvements for the *next* sprint — never defer process improvements.

**KPIs:** Sprint velocity (trending stable/improving); sprint goal achievement rate; defect escape rate; backlog health (% top 20% in Ready state); cycle time per story.

**Gate:** Product Owner confirms increment meets Definition of Done and sprint goal is achieved.

## Phase 6: Define the Change Management Strategy — Ground It in Reality First

**Goal:** Understand the depth of change, assess readiness, and build a strategic foundation before any change plan is written.

### Step 6.1 — Articulate the strategic context
Document in 1 page: the strategic goal, the competitive pressure, and the specific initiative. Link every change activity back to this 'why' — people adopt change faster when the reason is credible and visible.

### Step 6.2 — Assess change management maturity
Prosci's 5-level maturity model (Absent → Ad Hoc → Defined → Managed → Optimised). Survey PMO and HR leadership on 5 dimensions. Level 1–2 organisations need more hands-on support and longer lead times.

### Step 6.3 — Map the change using McKinsey 7S
For each of the 7 elements (Strategy, Structure, Systems, Staff, Skills, Style, Shared Values): current state → target state → gap size. Identify the most highly-connected elements as the transformation priorities.

### Step 6.4 — Build the change management timeline
Integrate with the project Gantt: when does the technical change go live, when are which groups impacted, and when do the change management milestones occur (communications, training, go-live support, hyper-care)?

### Step 6.5 — Assess readiness using ADKAR
Survey 10–20 people across impacted groups. Score each ADKAR dimension (1–5). Segment results by division or role to identify pockets of resistance. The barrier point — the lowest-scoring dimension — determines the primary intervention.

### Steps 6.6–6.10 — Team design, guiding principles, Lean Change Canvas, impact assessment, ADKAR objectives
Size the change team (1 change manager per ~500 impacted employees). Define roles and RACI. Co-create 5–7 guiding principles. Complete the Lean Change Canvas (vision, stakeholders, insights, options, experiments). Build the change impact assessment (what is changing, magnitude, people affected, support required — by role group). Set ADKAR objectives (targets per dimension, per role group, by milestone date).

**KPIs:** Readiness score ≥3.5/5 by role group; 80% aware of the change; change team fully staffed; 100% of role groups assessed.

**Gate:** Sponsor approves change management strategy before plan development begins.

## Phase 7: Develop the Change Management Plans — Six Plans That Cover Every Gap

**Goal:** Build six concrete, owned, scheduled plans that address every ADKAR dimension for every impacted group.

### Plan 1 — Master change management plan
Integrates all six sub-plans on a single Gantt. Shows dependencies: communication (Awareness) must precede training (Knowledge); training must precede coaching (Ability).

### Plan 2 — Communication plan
Per audience segment: key messages, channel, sender, frequency, timing. Test first 3 communications with a sample audience. Establish a 2-way feedback mechanism (Q&A inbox, Yammer group).

### Plan 3 — Sponsorship plan
3–5 executives as active sponsors. For each sponsor: target audience, activities (town halls, 1:1s, video messages), frequency, talking points. Measure sponsor coalition health monthly.

### Plan 4 — Coaching plan
Manager briefing kit: what is changing, manager's role, top 10 employee questions with suggested responses, team conversation guide. Mandatory 60-minute manager briefing session at T-8 weeks.

### Plan 5 — Training plan
Training matrix (role × course × date × format). Training runs T-6 to T-3 weeks. Leave 2 weeks between final training and go-live. Track completion by HR — mandatory completion before go-live is the Ability-building gate.

### Plan 6 — Resistance management plan
Top 5–8 sources of resistance with early-warning signals and response strategies. Distinguish active resistance (must be managed one-on-one) from passive resistance (often convertible through involvement and information).

**KPIs:** All 6 plans documented T-8 weeks before go-live; 100% managers have coaching kit; communication plan reaches ≥80% of impacted employees Wave 1; ≥85% of sponsor activities completed.

**Gate:** Change Manager confirms all 6 plans are documented and sponsor-reviewed.

---

## Phase 8: Implement, Track, and Sustain — Make the Change Stick

**Goal:** Execute all plans, monitor adoption in real time with ADKAR pulse surveys, sustain the change post go-live, and formally close.

### Governance cadence
Bi-weekly change steering review: RAG across all 6 plans, ADKAR progress, top risks. Any group scoring Readiness <3/5 at T-4 weeks is automatically escalated to the Steering Committee.

### Execute and report across all six plans
Track completion (% activities done) and effectiveness (open rates, training completion, survey scores). Monthly status report: one row per plan with RAG, % complete, trend, and key risk/action.

### Stakeholder re-engagement through go-live
ADKAR pulse surveys at T-4 weeks, T-2 weeks, T+2 weeks. Assign Change Champions to each high-risk group. Visible Q&A mechanism with 48-hour response SLA.

### Sustainment — the phase where most programmes fail
Remove the old way (turn off legacy system, update job descriptions). Activate reinforcement: manager recognition, performance dashboard showing new behaviour, follow-up microlearning, 30/60/90-day check-in surveys. Target: no significant ADKAR regression at T+90.

### Formal closure
Change Management Closure Checklist: ADKAR targets achieved for all groups; all plans completed; team demobilised; lessons learned archived; sponsor formally acknowledges closure.

**KPIs:** ADKAR ≥4/5 all role groups at T+90; training completion ≥90% before go-live; sponsor activity completion ≥85%; daily adoption rate ≥80% at T+30.

**Gate:** Sponsor formally closes change management workstream after ADKAR targets are confirmed.

## The Frameworks and Tools

### Kotter's 8-Step Model of Change
John Kotter's sequential eight-step process addresses the most common failure modes in transformations: insufficient urgency (Step 1), weak coalition (Step 2), no vision (Step 3), under-communication (Step 4), uncleared obstacles (Step 5), no short-term wins (Step 6), declaring victory too soon (Step 7), and failing to anchor change in culture (Step 8). The model's power is in sequencing — skipping any step creates a predictable failure mode.

**Tutorial (abbreviated):** Create urgency → Build the guiding coalition → Form and communicate the vision → Enable action → Generate short-term wins → Sustain acceleration → Institute change in culture. See the full tutorial and template in the Frameworks section.

### McKinsey 7S Framework
Seven interdependent elements — Strategy, Structure, Systems, Staff, Skills, Style, Shared Values — that must align for a transformation to succeed. Shared Values is the central hub. The critical insight: changing Strategy and Structure without addressing Style and Shared Values produces a map without territory — the organisation structurally looks different but behaves identically.

### ADKAR Model (Prosci)
The most diagnostically precise change tool in the toolkit. ADKAR identifies exactly where an individual or group is stalling in their change journey. The barrier point concept — measuring each dimension separately and finding the first dimension below the threshold — enables targeted interventions rather than generic blanket training. ROI is maximised when interventions address the barrier point, not the entire ADKAR chain simultaneously.

### Prosci 3-Phase Process
The professional change management methodology: Prepare (assess, strategise, sponsor), Manage (5 plans), Reinforce (sustain, close). Pairs with ADKAR as the diagnostic tool. The PCT (Project Change Triangle) model shows that all three elements — effective project management, effective change management, and strong sponsorship — must be present for transformation success.

### Burke-Litwin Change Model
The 12-factor causal model distinguishes transformational factors (strategy, leadership, culture — take 2–7 years to change durably) from transactional factors (structure, systems, management practices — addressable in 3–6 months). The most common error the model prevents: using a transactional intervention (process redesign) for a transformational root cause (culture misalignment).

### Lewin's Change Management Model
The foundational three-stage model: Unfreeze (reduce restraining forces by creating dissatisfaction with the status quo), Change (make the transition with visible leadership support), Refreeze (anchor new behaviours in formal systems — incentives, job descriptions, performance reviews). The Force-Field Analysis (listing and scoring driving vs. restraining forces) is the model's most practical diagnostic tool.

### Change Commitment Curve
Maps the eight stages from Unawareness to Internalisation. The key diagnostic use: identify which stage each stakeholder group is at, and design stage-appropriate interventions. The minimum safe stage at go-live is Testing (willing to try the new way). The sustainment target is Institutionalisation (new way is the default).

### Change Personal Transition Curve (Kübler-Ross adapted)
The emotional journey through change: Shock → Denial → Frustration → Depression → Experiment → Decision → Integration. The 'Depression' stage is a necessary letting-go stage — attempting to rush employees through it creates fragile compliance. The critical management skill: validate the loss explicitly, do not minimise it. Used primarily in manager coaching kits.

### Innovation Adoption Curve (Rogers)
Segments the impacted population into Innovators (2.5%), Early Adopters (13.5%), Early Majority (34%), Late Majority (34%), and Laggards (16%). The critical mass tipping point occurs at ~16% adoption (after Innovators + Early Adopters). Investment strategy: win Early Adopters first, use their visible success as social proof for the Early Majority. Do not over-invest in Laggards at the expense of the majority.

### Maslow's Hierarchy and Heart-and-Mind Drivers
Change management interventions address multiple levels of human motivation simultaneously. Physiological/safety-level concerns (job security, workload impact) must be addressed before employees can engage with higher-level change messages (growth, meaning, belonging). The 'heart and mind' framework acknowledges that rational arguments (mind) alone are insufficient — emotional connection to the change's purpose (heart) is equally required for durable adoption. The coaching plan and personalised manager conversations are the primary vehicles for this.

### Four Stages of Team Development (Tuckman)
Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing. Leaders must match their style to the team's stage. The Storming stage is the most frequently mismanaged: artificially suppressing conflict creates extended Norming without genuine cohesion. Key practical tool: the Team Charter workshop (2–4 hours) compresses Forming and early Storming by explicitly agreeing operating norms, communication preferences, and conflict resolution protocols.

### RACI Matrix
The single most effective tool for eliminating accountability ambiguity. Hard rule: exactly one A (Accountable) per deliverable or decision row. Common failure: multiple As per row, creating the 'social loafing' effect where everyone assumes someone else is accountable. The RACI should be built before the project begins and updated after every major scope change.

### Agile Scrum Framework
The seven Scrum events in a sprint cycle. The three roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team) form a self-organising unit. The Definition of Done — agreed acceptance criteria for every story — is the mechanism that prevents technical debt from accumulating invisibly. The Retrospective is the team's continuous improvement engine; selecting only 1–2 improvements per sprint ensures they are actually implemented rather than accumulating in a backlog.

## Top Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

**1. Starting execution before the business case is approved.** Projects that begin work before formal approval have no baseline — scope creep is invisible, and the team has no mandate to say no to changes. The business case is not a formality; it is the project's mandate.

**2. Skipping stakeholder analysis.** The most expensive project failures involve a high-power stakeholder who was not identified until they raised a blocking concern in month 3. Map all stakeholders in Week 1 of initiation; do not rely on memory.

**3. Treating the RACI as a one-time document.** Team changes, scope changes, and phase transitions all invalidate the RACI. Rebuild or review it at the start of each phase.

**4. Treating go-live as the end of change management.** Adoption is lowest immediately after go-live. The sustainment phase (T+30 to T+90) is where most change programmes fail. Schedule and resource the change management workstream through this entire period.

**5. Running training before building Desire.** Training completion metrics (Knowledge/Ability) look good while adoption remains low because employees completed the training without ever wanting to change. Always measure and address ADKAR's Desire dimension before investing in training.

**6. Suppressing Storming in project teams.** Teams that are prevented from surfacing conflict in their early weeks will surface it at the worst possible time — typically during a critical delivery milestone. The Team Charter workshop and deliberate Storming facilitation are the preventive investment.

**7. Declaring victory after early wins.** Kotter's research documents that programmes which declare the change complete after the first year almost always regress. Refreeze (Lewin) and Institutionalisation (Commitment Curve) require sustained leadership attention for 2–3 years in major transformations.

**8. Agile without empowered Product Owners.** Scrum is not productive when the Product Owner cannot set priorities without multi-level approval chains above them. Resolve the authority question before adopting Scrum — otherwise the sprint planning becomes theatre.

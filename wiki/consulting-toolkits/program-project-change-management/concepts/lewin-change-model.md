# Lewin's Change Management Model (Unfreeze–Change–Refreeze)

*Category: change · Toolkit: Program, Project & Change Management*

## What it is
A three-stage model that views organisational change as analogous to reshaping ice: first unfreeze the current state (overcome inertia), then move to the desired state (make the change), then refreeze the new state (embed it).

**Origin:** Kurt Lewin, social psychologist, 1947 — published in 'Frontiers in Group Dynamics,' Human Relations, Vol 1, No 1. One of the foundational theories of organisational psychology.

## Why it works
Lewin's force-field analysis shows that every current behaviour is maintained by a balance of driving forces (pushing toward change) and restraining forces (resisting change). The most effective way to change is not to add more driving forces (which increases tension) but to reduce restraining forces. Unfreezing reduces restraining forces by creating dissatisfaction with the status quo; Change makes the new behaviour visible and supported; Refreezing reinforces the new behaviour until it becomes the new equilibrium.

## When to use
Most applicable for incremental culture and process changes, or as a conceptual overlay to other frameworks. The Force-Field Analysis is especially useful in workshops to surface hidden resistances.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Unfreeze — Diagnose the restraining forces: run a Force-Field Analysis workshop. List all forces driving change and all forces resisting it. Score each force's strength (1–5). Plan to reduce the top 3 restraining forces — this is more effective than amplifying driving forces.
2. Unfreeze — Communicate the burning platform: share data, competitive intelligence, or customer evidence that makes the status quo feel dangerous. The goal is to reduce the perceived safety of not changing.
3. Unfreeze — Involve impacted people early: invite key resistors into the design process. Participation reduces resistance because people support what they help create.
4. Change — Execute with visible leadership: every leadership interaction during the Change phase should model the new behaviour. Inconsistency between leader words and actions is the primary cause of 'change exhaustion'.
5. Change — Provide just-in-time support: make the new way easier than the old way during the transition — extra coaching, simplified processes, help desks, job aids, super-users.
6. Refreeze — Anchor in formal systems: update job descriptions, KPIs, incentives, and performance reviews to require the new behaviour. Remove the option to revert to the old way (e.g., turn off the legacy system).
7. Refreeze — Celebrate and recognise: publicly reward the teams and individuals who have fully adopted the change. This creates social proof and accelerates laggard adoption.

## Real-life example — General Electric (Six Sigma culture change, 1995–2000 under Jack Welch)
Welch applied Lewin's model explicitly. Unfreeze: he communicated that GE's quality gap versus competitors was an existential threat (burning platform) and declared that 'any manager not committed to Six Sigma will not be promoted.' Change: invested $500M in Six Sigma training and made Black Belt certification a prerequisite for promotion. Refreeze: linked 40% of senior executive bonuses to Six Sigma metrics, revised the leadership performance framework, and embedded 'statistical thinking' in GE's operating language. The refreezing was so thorough that Six Sigma persisted as a core competency for 15+ years.

**So what:** Refreezing through executive incentives and promotion criteria — not just training — is what separated GE's lasting culture change from typical training programmes that fade within 12 months.

## Pitfalls
- Skipping the Unfreeze stage: launching change activities before people feel the need to change leads to compliance without commitment — participation but no behaviour change.
- Not Refreezing: treating change as complete at go-live means the organisation gradually reverts to old habits — formal system changes (incentives, job descriptions, policies) are required to lock in the change.

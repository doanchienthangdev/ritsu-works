# Burke-Litwin Change Model

*Category: change · Toolkit: Program, Project & Change Management*

## What it is
A 12-factor causal model of organisational performance and change that distinguishes 'transformational' factors (driven by external environment: strategy, leadership, culture) from 'transactional' factors (driven by internal management: structure, systems, management practices, climate, motivation, task requirements, skills, individual performance).

**Origin:** W. Warner Burke and George Litwin, 1992 — published in 'A Causal Model of Organizational Performance and Change,' Journal of Management, Vol 18, No 3.

## Why it works
Unlike the 7S model, Burke-Litwin specifies causal direction: external environment drives transformational factors, which cascade down to transactional factors, which together drive individual and organisational performance. Transformational change (strategy, leadership, culture) is fundamentally different from transactional change (processes, structure, systems) — mixing them up leads to misaligned interventions. Deep culture change requires addressing transformational factors first.

## When to use
When a transformation involves deep culture change, leadership transition, or a fundamental strategic pivot — situations where the 7S identifies misalignment but does not explain the causal sequence to fix it.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Map the external environment forces (competitive, regulatory, technological, economic) driving the need for change. These are the root causes that must be communicated as the 'why' for the transformation.
2. Assess the three transformational factors: Is the Mission & Strategy still aligned to the external environment? Is Leadership modelling the new behaviours? Is Culture (unwritten rules and values) aligned with the strategy?
3. If transformational factors need to change, plan a 12–24 month programme — these require deep, sustained effort and executive leadership.
4. Assess the six transactional factors: Structure, Management Practices, Systems & Policies, Work Unit Climate, Task Requirements/Skills, and Motivation. These can often be addressed in 3–6 month cycles.
5. Map the causal chain: for each performance gap identified, trace the cause upward through the model — is it ultimately a transformational or transactional root cause? This determines the right intervention level.
6. Design interventions at the correct level: do not address a transformational problem (e.g., culture mismatch) with a transactional solution (e.g., a new process) — it will fail.

## Real-life example — British Airways (mid-1980s turnaround under Lord King and Colin Marshall)
The turnaround required changes at every level of the Burke-Litwin model: External environment (deregulation forcing competitive service); Mission shift ('Fly the Flag' → 'World's Favourite Airline'); Leadership model (Marshall personally modelled customer obsession); Culture change (from bureaucratic state-airline to service-excellence); Management Practices (performance management linked to customer satisfaction); Individual Skills (intensive 'Putting People First' training for 37,000 employees). The causal model was used to ensure leadership change preceded culture change, which preceded process and skills change.

**So what:** Attempting the skills training (transactional) before the culture and leadership change (transformational) would have had no lasting impact — the Burke-Litwin model prevented this sequencing error.

- [ ] Causal map: Which transformational gaps are root causes of transactional gaps?

## Pitfalls
- Treating transformational change as a project with a fixed end date: culture change takes 3–7 years and does not have a go-live — leaders who declare victory after 18 months typically see regression.
- Using transactional interventions for transformational problems: process redesign cannot fix a leadership alignment gap; it only masks it temporarily.

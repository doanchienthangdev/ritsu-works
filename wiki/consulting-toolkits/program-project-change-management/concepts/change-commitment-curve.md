# Change Commitment Curve

*Category: change · Toolkit: Program, Project & Change Management*

## What it is
A visual model depicting the emotional and behavioural stages individuals move through when exposed to an organisational change — from initial awareness through commitment — enabling leaders to tailor interventions to each stage.

**Origin:** Adapted from Conner and Patterson (1982), Research Press; popularised by Daryl Conner in 'Managing at the Speed of Change' (1993). Related to and often confused with the Change Personal Transition Curve (which is emotion-based); the Commitment Curve is action/behaviour-based.

## Why it works
Commitment to change is not a binary switch — it builds progressively through stages. Interventions that are right for one stage are wrong for another: providing training to someone in 'Unawareness' is wasted; providing information to someone in 'Testing' is insufficient. The curve provides leaders with a diagnostic framework to assess where each stakeholder group is and target the right intervention.

## When to use
Planning stakeholder engagement strategy; prioritising change management resources; diagnosing why adoption is lagging in specific groups.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Map each key stakeholder group to its current stage on the curve using survey data or leader interviews.
2. For each group, define the target stage at go-live and at T+90 days. The minimum safe stage at go-live is 'Testing' (willing to try the new way); 'Institutionalisation' is the sustainment target.
3. Design stage-appropriate interventions: Unawareness→Awareness = communications; Awareness→Understanding = manager briefings; Understanding→Positive Perception = Q&A, addressing fears; Positive Perception→Testing = piloting, sandbox environments; Testing→Adoption = coaching, reinforcement; Adoption→Institutionalisation = system changes, performance management.
4. Re-measure at T-4 weeks and T+30 days to track movement along the curve.
5. Flag any group that is not progressing (stuck for >4 weeks at the same stage) as high risk and escalate to the change steering committee.

## Real-life example — Procter & Gamble (Connect + Develop open innovation, 2001)
When P&G introduced its 'Connect + Develop' open innovation model — asking scientists to co-invent with external partners — the majority of R&D employees were at the 'Understanding' stage (they understood the rationale) but had not moved to 'Positive Perception' because they feared external collaboration would threaten their jobs. P&G's change team designed focused manager-led team conversations addressing job security explicitly (moving from Understanding to Positive Perception), followed by 20 high-visibility pilot projects featuring internal scientists as leads (Testing stage). Within 18 months, 35% of new products had an external partner.

**So what:** Diagnosing the specific barrier — fear of job threat at the Understanding-to-Positive Perception transition — enabled a targeted intervention instead of more training.

## Pitfalls
- Assuming all groups move at the same pace: executive-level stakeholders often reach Adoption before frontline employees have even reached Awareness — segment and track each group separately.
- Confusing compliance with commitment: people can comply with a new process (Testing) without being committed to it — measure actual behaviour quality, not just activity completion.

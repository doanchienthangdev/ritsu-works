# Design Thinking Methodology (Applied to Finance)

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Financial Modeling, Planning & Analysis*

## What it is
A five-stage, human-centered problem-solving process (Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test) applied to FP&A process redesign and financial tool development — solving financial problems by starting with the end user's (CFO's, BU head's, initiative owner's) actual experience, not with the finance team's preferred output.

**Origin:** Design Thinking formalized by IDEO design firm (David Kelley) and Stanford d.school (2004). Applied to business transformation by Roger Martin ('The Design of Business', 2009). Applied to finance function transformation in McKinsey's 'Finance 2025' and Accenture's finance transformation practice.

## Why it works
Finance processes are typically designed by finance people for finance people. The CFO's internal customers — the CEO, BU heads, investors — are not consulted on what information they actually need. The result: dashboards nobody uses, reports nobody reads, and models too complex for the decisions they support. Design Thinking fixes this by starting with empathy (understanding the user's actual job-to-be-done) rather than the data available.

## When to use
Use when a finance process or tool has low adoption, generates frequent complaints, or fails to enable fast decisions. Apply specifically to dashboard redesign, planning process redesign, and finance team operating model transformation.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Identify the FP&A process or tool that is underperforming: low dashboard usage, long cycle time, frequent ad-hoc requests that suggest regular reports are inadequate, or recurring complaints in Finance Committee meetings.
2. Empathize phase: shadow 3–5 users (CFO, BU heads, or initiative owners) for one day each. Observe how they actually use financial information — which reports they open, which they ignore, where they manually recalculate numbers. Do not interview them about what they want; observe what they do.
3. Define phase: synthesize your observations into a 'How Might We' (HMW) problem statement: 'How might we enable the CFO to make a go/no-go decision on a budget reallocation request in under 5 minutes?' This is more actionable than 'improve financial reporting.'
4. Ideate phase: run a structured brainstorm with the FP&A team. Generate 20+ solution ideas (quantity over quality). Cluster by theme. Have users vote on which ideas would most help them. Select the top 3 for prototyping.
5. Prototype phase: build the minimum viable version of the solution in 1–2 days. For a dashboard redesign: a PowerPoint mock-up or a rough Power BI prototype. For a new report: a manually assembled Excel version. Do not build the full solution before testing.
6. Test phase: give 5 users the prototype with a specific task ('Using this dashboard, identify the top 3 actions needed for next week'). Measure task completion time and ask for qualitative feedback. Iterate based on what you learn. The goal is 3–5 rapid iterations before full build.

## Real-life example — ING Bank finance transformation (2016)
ING Bank applied Design Thinking to its finance function transformation starting in 2016. The Empathize phase revealed: BU heads were ignoring the monthly finance pack (25-page PDF) and sending informal WhatsApp messages to their finance partners asking for the 3 numbers they actually needed. The Define phase framed the problem: 'How might we reduce the CFO's decision latency from 3 days (wait for the report) to 3 minutes (real-time access to the key number)?' The Ideate and Prototype phases produced a mobile-first CFO dashboard showing 5 KPIs with drill-down. After 3 test-and-iterate cycles, the new dashboard achieved 80% daily active usage among senior leaders — vs. 20% for the legacy PDF pack. ING reported a 40% reduction in ad-hoc data requests to the FP&A team following the dashboard launch.

**So what:** Design Thinking in finance is not about aesthetics — it is about starting with the user's job-to-be-done and working backward to the solution. The most common finance transformation failure is building technically correct outputs that nobody uses because the user's actual need was never understood.

## Pitfalls
- Skipping the Empathize phase and going straight to Ideate — the most common design failure. 'We already know the problem' is almost always wrong; the real problem is revealed by observation, not assumption.
- Prototyping before defining the problem — a well-executed solution to the wrong problem is waste. The 'How Might We' statement is the quality gate between Empathize and Prototype.
- Designing by committee — too many stakeholders in the Design Thinking process produces consensus-driven, bland solutions. Keep the core design team to 3–5 people; gather broader input at the Test phase.

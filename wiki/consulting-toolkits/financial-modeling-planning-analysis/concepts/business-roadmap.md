# Business Roadmap

*Category: planning · Toolkit: Financial Modeling, Planning & Analysis*

## What it is
A multi-year, Gantt-style plan that sequences the execution of the prioritized initiative portfolio against a timeline, shows the financial impact of each initiative by quarter, and provides a single visual reference for the link between strategy, initiatives, financial plan, and execution calendar.

**Origin:** Roadmapping as a planning tool was formalized in technology product management (Motorola's Steve Willyard and Catherine McClees, 1987). Applied to corporate strategy as 'strategic roadmaps' by McKinsey and BCG in the 1990s. The corporate transformation roadmap is a standard output of any major strategy engagement.

## Why it works
The roadmap answers the question that the financial plan alone cannot: 'How exactly will we get from here to there, step by step, quarter by quarter?' Without a roadmap, the financial plan is a promise with no plan for delivery. The roadmap creates accountability by attaching a timeline and a responsible owner to every financial improvement in the plan.

## When to use
Use at the end of Phase 3 as the execution plan for the financial plan. Present at every quarterly Board meeting as the single source of truth for execution progress. Update when major initiatives are delayed, accelerated, or de-prioritized.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List all approved initiatives from the prioritized portfolio in priority order (Quick Wins first, then Major Projects).
2. For each initiative, define the four phases: Design (requirements, project planning, team formation), Pilot (limited-scope test with measurable results), Scale (full rollout), Sustain (business-as-usual). Assign start and end dates to each phase.
3. Plot the initiative timeline on the roadmap: use a Gantt-bar format with distinct colors for each phase. Each initiative is one row; each column is one quarter.
4. Overlay financial impact milestones: for each initiative, mark the quarter when financial benefits begin appearing in the P&L (not when the project starts — when the P&L impact is first measurable). This is the 'financial go-live' date.
5. Build the cumulative financial impact chart at the bottom: a quarterly bar chart showing the incremental cumulative benefit from all active initiatives, summing to the total financial plan target by Year 3.
6. Identify resource conflicts: are multiple Major Projects requiring the same key teams or systems in the same quarter? If so, one must be delayed. The roadmap is the tool that makes these conflicts visible before the fact.
7. Review the roadmap with the CEO, BU heads, and initiative owners: each owner must confirm their initiative's timeline is executable. This review is the operational contract behind the financial plan.
8. Publish the roadmap as the first slide of the quarterly Board update. It is the single most important governance document connecting strategy to execution.

## Real-life example — Siemens 'Vision 2020' corporate transformation
Siemens CEO Joe Kaeser launched 'Vision 2020' in 2014 — a 6-year corporate transformation targeting 11–15% EBIT margin. The business roadmap sequenced: Year 1 (2014–2015) = portfolio clarification (sell non-core businesses including Siemens Audiology, Siemens IT Solutions). Year 2–3 = cost structure improvement (SG&A reduction, supply chain consolidation, ZBB). Year 4–5 = revenue quality improvement (premium pricing for digital industrial services). Year 6 = SPUN divisions MedTech (Healthineers, IPO 2018) and energy (Siemens Energy, IPO 2020) to unlock valuation. Each roadmap phase had specific financial milestones: 'Achieve 10% EBIT by end of Year 3 before the portfolio carve-outs.' The public roadmap created accountability that an internal financial plan alone could not.

**So what:** A business roadmap is most powerful when it is time-specific and financially sequenced. 'We will transform the portfolio' is a strategy. 'We will divest 4 business units in Year 1, generating $5B of proceeds; reinvest $3B in automation CapEx in Years 2–3; achieve 12% EBIT margin by Year 4' is a roadmap.

## Pitfalls
- Building an aspirational roadmap that no one uses — a roadmap must be reviewed at every quarterly Board meeting, with RAG-status updates on each initiative. If the roadmap is a one-time planning artifact, it is a waste of time.
- Plotting too many initiatives simultaneously — if every quarter has 15 simultaneous 'Design' phases, the organization will choke. Sequence initiatives to manage organizational bandwidth; typically no more than 3–5 active Major Projects at any one time.
- Not linking financial impact dates to the P&L — if the initiative 'scales' in Q3 Year 2 but the financial benefit does not appear in the model until Q1 Year 3, the model is inconsistent with the roadmap. Align them explicitly.

# Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking

**One-liner:** Build a world-class customer experience strategy and adopt design thinking across your organization in two interlocking tracks.

**Core value:** A structured, end-to-end system that converts raw user observations into a validated CX strategy and a pipeline of design-thinking-tested initiatives — so leaders can stop relying on opinion and start improving customer satisfaction, conversion, and cost-to-serve with evidence.

---

## When to Use This Toolkit

Activate this toolkit when one or more of the following triggers apply:

- Customer satisfaction (NPS, CSAT) has declined or plateaued for two or more consecutive quarters
- The company is launching a new product, service, or digital channel and wants to get the experience right the first time
- A post-merger integration requires harmonizing two distinct customer experiences
- Leadership has committed to 'becoming customer-centric' but lacks a structured process to execute that commitment
- Innovation velocity is low and the organization needs a repeatable process for generating user-validated ideas
- Customer churn is elevated and the root cause is known to be experience-related (not price or product feature)

This toolkit is for: Chief Customer Officers, VP/Directors of CX, management consultants advising on service design or digital transformation, product managers launching new user experiences, and innovation leaders piloting design thinking.

---

## The Process at a Glance

The toolkit runs two parallel but interlocked tracks.

**Track A: CX Strategy (4 phases, 8 modules)**
Establishes the strategic context, ambition, measurement framework, organization, and portfolio of initiatives. Think of it as 'what to do and why.'

**Track B: Design Thinking (5 phases, following the Stanford d.school double-diamond)**
Validates and designs each initiative identified in Track A. Think of it as 'how to design it so it actually works for customers.'

The tracks meet at the 'List of Initiatives' output: Track A defines the initiative portfolio; Track B takes each initiative through Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test before it enters development.

## Phase 1 — Anchor on Strategy Before Designing for Customers

### Goal
Establish the strategic context and constraints within which the CX strategy will operate. This phase prevents the most common CX failure mode: investing in customer experience improvements that the business strategy cannot sustain or that don't move the metrics that matter.

### Key Question
What does our corporate and business strategy demand from the customer experience, and what constraints must we respect?

### Steps

**Step 1.1 — Summarize the Corporate & Business Strategy.** Extract and synthesize three strategic layers: (1) corporate strategy (where the company competes: markets, geographies, M&A posture); (2) business-unit strategy (how each unit wins: cost leadership, differentiation, or focus per Porter's Generic Strategies); (3) the value proposition to customers. Use a one-page Strategic Context Brief that forces the team to state the top three strategic priorities and top three constraints. This brief becomes the non-negotiable boundary for all CX investment decisions. *Input: Annual reports, investor presentations, CEO communications. Output: 1-page Strategic Context Brief. Owner: CX Strategy Lead + CFO office.*

**Step 1.2 — Assess Current CX Maturity.** Score the organization against a 5-level CX Maturity Model across six dimensions: customer understanding, strategy and governance, design and delivery, culture and talent, measurement, and technology. Conduct structured interviews with 10–15 cross-functional leaders using a 40-question scorecard. Average scores per dimension, identify the two largest gaps, and establish the current maturity level (1=Naïve → 5=Customer-Centric). *Input: NPS/CSAT data, org chart, technology inventory. Output: CX Maturity Radar Chart. Owner: CX Strategy Lead + HR.*

**Step 1.3 — Define CX Vision, Mission & Values.** Facilitate a half-day executive workshop using the 'Future Press Release' technique: 'What would the Wall Street Journal write about our customer experience in 5 years if we got this right?' Draft a 1-sentence vision (aspirational, time-bound), a 2-sentence mission, and 3–5 CX values. Validate against the Strategic Context Brief for coherence. *Input: Maturity results, competitive benchmarks. Output: Approved Vision/Mission/Values statement. Owner: CCO + Executive team.*

### Key Frameworks
- **Strategic Context Brief**: one-page alignment tool forcing strategic priority and constraint articulation
- **CX Maturity Model**: 5-level, 6-dimension diagnostic tool
- **CX Vision / Mission / Values**: aspirational framing tool using Future Press Release technique

### Deliverable
Strategic Context Brief + CX Maturity Assessment + Approved Vision/Mission/Values Statement

### KPIs
- Executive alignment score: ≥4/5 rating from C-suite on vision
- All 6 maturity dimensions baselined

### Exit Gate
Executive sponsor formally approves the Strategic Context Brief and CX Vision before moving to objective-setting.

## Phase 2 — Translate Vision Into Measurable Commitments

### Goal
Translate the CX vision into a structured set of strategic objectives with measurable KPIs, a governance model, and an approved budget — so the organization has clear accountability and resources.

### Key Question
What does success look like in measurable terms, and who is accountable for delivering it with what resources?

### Steps

**Step 2.1 — Set Strategic Objectives & KPIs.** Apply the OKR-for-CX framework: define 3–5 strategic objectives (qualitative ambitions tied to the vision) and 2–3 key results per objective (quantified, time-bound). Use a three-tier KPI hierarchy: Outcome KPIs (NPS, CSAT, Customer Effort Score, churn, CLV); Experience KPIs (interaction satisfaction, resolution rate, wait time); Operational KPIs (first-contact resolution, on-time delivery). Benchmark against industry leaders for target-setting. *Input: CX Vision/Mission, industry benchmarks, current baseline metrics. Output: CX Balanced Scorecard. Owner: CX Strategy Lead + CFO.*

**Step 2.2 — Design the CX Team & Governance.** Map current CX-relevant roles across the organization. Identify ownership gaps in the customer journey. Design the target CX organizational model (centralized CoE, federated chapters, or hybrid). Define RACI for key decisions. Estimate budget using zero-based budgeting: headcount + technology + research + training. *Input: Current org chart, maturity gaps, competitor benchmarks. Output: CX Org Design Blueprint + approved budget. Owner: CCO + CHRO + CFO.*

### Deliverable
CX Balanced Scorecard + CX Org Blueprint + Budget Approval

### Exit Gate
CFO and CCO co-sign the budget and KPI framework.

## Phase 3 — Know Your Customer Segments and Their Journey

### Goal
Deeply understand which customer segments to prioritize and what their actual end-to-end experience looks like today, including pain points and Moments of Truth.

### Key Question
Who are our most strategically important customer segments, and where does the experience break down or delight them most?

### Steps

**Step 3.1 — Define and Prioritize Customer Segments.** Combine behavioral segmentation (transactional data: frequency, recency, spend) with needs-based segmentation (survey/interview data: jobs-to-be-done). Plot segments on a 2x2 matrix: Current Value vs. Strategic Potential. Select 2–4 priority segments for journey mapping. Document each as a rich customer persona. *Input: CRM data, surveys, NPS verbatims. Output: Segment prioritization matrix + 2–4 personas. Owner: CX Strategy Lead + Analytics.*

**Step 3.2 — Map Current-State Customer Journeys.** Facilitate a 3-hour cross-functional journey-mapping workshop for each priority segment. Structure the map across 5 layers: customer stages, customer actions, thoughts/questions, emotions (emotion curve 0–10), and internal touchpoints/systems. Identify Moments of Truth (the 3–5 interactions that most influence satisfaction or churn). Validate with 5–10 customer interviews. *Input: Customer personas, voice-of-customer data. Output: Current-state journey map per segment. Owner: CX Design Lead + cross-functional team.*

### Deliverable
Priority Segment Matrix + Customer Personas + Current-State Journey Maps

### Exit Gate
Leadership team aligns on which Moments of Truth are 'must-fix' priorities.

## Phase 4 — Build the Initiative Portfolio and Roadmap

### Goal
Generate, size, and prioritize a portfolio of CX improvement initiatives, build business cases, and create a sequenced implementation roadmap.

### Key Question
Which initiatives will most efficiently close the gap between the current experience and the CX vision, and in what sequence?

### Steps

**Step 4.1 — Generate and Screen Initiatives.** Run a 4-hour ideation workshop using 'How Might We' (HMW) questions anchored to each pain point. Generate 30–60 ideas; apply a 3-criteria quick screen (Moment of Truth relevance, technical feasibility, vision alignment). Eliminate ideas failing two or more criteria. Typically yields 15–25 candidate initiatives. *Input: Journey maps, Moments of Truth analysis, CX Vision. Output: Long-list of 15-25 screened initiatives. Owner: CX Strategy Lead + cross-functional team.*

**Step 4.2 — Build Business Cases.** For the top 10–15 initiatives, build a one-page business case: problem statement, proposed solution, expected impact on Outcome KPIs (use McKinsey benchmarks: 10–20% CSAT improvement, 10–15% conversion uplift, 20–50% cost-to-serve reduction), rough investment estimate, and break-even timeline. Use conservative, base-case, and stretch-case scenarios. *Input: Initiative long-list, KPI baselines, finance templates. Output: Business case per initiative. Owner: CX Strategy Lead + Finance.*

**Step 4.3 — Prioritize & Build the Roadmap.** Plot all initiatives on an Impact vs. Effort 2x2 matrix. Categorize: Quick Wins (high impact, low effort), Major Projects, Incremental, Deprioritize. Sequence Quick Wins in Wave 1 (0–6 months), Major Projects in Waves 2–3 (6–24 months). Build a Gantt-style roadmap with initiative owner, wave, budget, and KPI target. *Input: Business cases, budget envelope, capacity. Output: Prioritized portfolio + 24-month roadmap. Owner: CCO + CFO + Programme Management.*

### Deliverable
Prioritized CX Initiative Portfolio + Business Cases + 24-Month Roadmap

### Exit Gate
Executive Steering Committee approves roadmap and allocates budget.

## Phase 5 — Empathize: Go Deeper Than Surveys

### Goal
Gain a deep, empathic understanding of the users affected by the initiative being designed — going beyond surveys to observe actual behavior and emotional context.

### Key Question
What do our users actually experience, feel, and need — not what they say they need?

### Steps

**Step 5.1 — Set Up the Research Plan.** Define a Project Canvas specifying: the design challenge, target users, key research questions, methods, timeline, and team. Build a Research Plan matrix: one row per method, with columns for purpose, sample size, recruiting criteria, guide, and output. Prioritize 3–4 complementary methods (interviews for 'why', DILO for 'how', diary study for 'when'). *Input: Initiative brief, target segment definition. Output: Approved Project Canvas and Research Plan. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + User Researcher.*

**Step 5.2 — Conduct Primary User Research.** Execute the plan using prioritized methods. For User Interviews: use a semi-structured guide with 8–12 open questions; probe with 'Tell me more' and five 'Why?' iterations. For DILO: shadow the user for a full day, photographing every interaction and emotion. For Service Safari: experience the service as a customer across all channels, scoring each step 1–5 on an emotion scale. For Contextual Inquiry: observe users in their natural environment while asking them to narrate their actions. *Input: Research Plan, recruited participants. Output: Raw notes, transcripts, photos, emotional scores. Owner: User Researcher.*

**Step 5.3 — Synthesize Empathy Data.** Conduct a 4-hour synthesis session. Post all raw observations. Sort using Affinity Mapping: cluster similar notes into themes, name each with an insight statement ('Users feel X when Y because Z'). Build an Empathy Map: What they Say, Think, Do, Feel. Use Emotional Response Cards (50-item deck of emotion descriptors) to accurately label user emotional states. *Input: Raw research data. Output: Insight clusters, Empathy Map, Emotional Journey Map. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + full team.*

### Deliverable
Research Synthesis Report with Insight Clusters + Empathy Map + Emotional Journey

### Exit Gate
Team reaches consensus on the top 5 user insights.

## Phase 6 — Define: Zero In on the Right Problem

### Goal
Converge on a precise, human-centered problem statement that focuses creative energy on the right problem to solve.

### Key Question
What is the single most important problem we must solve for our users, framed in a way that invites creative solutions?

### Steps

**Step 6.1 — Build User Personas & Annotated Journey Maps.** Synthesize research into 1–3 archetypal User Personas: name, age/role, core goal, key frustrations, tech comfort, characteristic quote, and 'day in the life' narrative. Map each persona's current journey using research-validated observations: stages, actions, thoughts, emotions, touchpoints, pain points, opportunities. Mark highest-pain moments as 'How Might We' anchor points. *Input: Research synthesis, empathy map. Output: 2–3 Personas + Annotated Journey Maps. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + UX Researcher.*

**Step 6.2 — Run Root-Cause Analysis.** Apply Five Whys: state the pain point, then ask 'Why does this happen?' five times. Apply 5W+H (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How) to fully characterize the problem context. Document root causes on a problem tree: symptoms → proximate causes → root causes. *Input: Pain points from journey maps, research transcripts. Output: Root-cause analysis + Problem tree. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + SMEs.*

**Step 6.3 — Craft the Problem Statement & Value Proposition.** Write the Problem Statement: '[User] needs [a way to do X] because [insight/root cause], but today [barrier]. We'll know we've solved it when [measurable criterion].' Test with Card Sorting: 10 team members sort the statement as 'clearly solvable', 'too broad', or 'too narrow' — aim for 80% 'clearly solvable'. Conduct a Landscape Review of how competitors and analogous industries address similar problems. Draft the Value Proposition Canvas: Jobs → Pains → Gains (customer side); Products & Services → Pain Relievers → Gain Creators (company side). *Input: Root-cause analysis, competitive intelligence. Output: Validated Problem Statement + Value Proposition Canvas. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + Product Manager.*

### Deliverable
Validated Problem Statement + Value Proposition Canvas + Annotated Journey Map

### Exit Gate
Sponsor approves the Problem Statement as the design brief.

## Phase 7 — Ideate: Generate the Unexpected Before the Obvious

### Goal
Generate a broad, diverse, and imaginative range of solution ideas anchored to the problem statement, then evaluate and select the most promising concepts.

### Key Question
What are all the possible ways we could solve this problem, and which ones are most worth prototyping?

### Steps

**Step 7.1 — Frame Success & Constraints.** Before ideation, define two anchors: (1) Define Success — a 1-paragraph description of what the world looks like when the problem is solved, including which KPI moves by how much; (2) Ideation Constraints — the 3–5 non-negotiable constraints (regulatory, budget, technology) that ideas must respect. Share both with the full team before the session. *Input: Problem Statement, KPI targets, business constraints. Output: Success definition + Constraint charter. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + Sponsor.*

**Step 7.2 — Run the Ideation Workshop.** Execute a 3-hour structured workshop using sequenced techniques: HMW Questions (rephrase each pain point: 'How might we help users X without Y?'); Brainstorming (20-min rapid-fire, no evaluation); Mind Mapping (radial map from problem statement); Crazy 8s (each participant sketches 8 concepts in 8 minutes); Design Sprint 'Lightning Demo' (each participant presents an inspiring analog from a different industry). Capture all ideas on sticky notes or Miro. *Input: HMW questions, Success Definition, Constraint Charter. Output: Ideation wall with 50–100+ ideas. Owner: Design Thinking Lead (facilitator) + team of 6–10.*

**Step 7.3 — Evaluate & Select Ideas.** Round 1 — Dot Voting: 5 votes per team member; ideas with ≥3 votes advance. Round 2 — NABC Evaluation: score each surviving idea on Need, Approach, Benefit, Competition (1–5 each). Select top 3–5 for prototyping. For each, complete an Idea Evaluation canvas: description, target user, key assumption, expected benefit, key risk. *Input: Ideation wall, NABC scoring rubric. Output: Top 3–5 ideas with Idea Evaluation canvases. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + Sponsor.*

### Deliverable
Prioritized Idea Portfolio (3–5 concepts) with NABC Evaluation Canvases

### Exit Gate
Sponsor endorses selected ideas for prototyping investment.

## Phase 8 — Prototype: Make It Tangible Before You Build It

### Goal
Build fast, low-cost representations of selected solutions — from rough sketches to working models — to make ideas tangible enough to learn from, not to build perfectly.

### Key Question
What is the fastest, cheapest way to test whether our solution idea actually works for users?

### Steps

**Step 8.1 — Create Low-Fidelity Prototypes.** Start with the cheapest, fastest representation: Sketching (paper wireframes of each screen or touchpoint, 15 minutes per sketch); Storyboarding (6–10 panel comic strip showing the user's journey with the proposed solution); Role-Playing (act out the service scenario with team members as user and service provider, scripted from the storyboard). Low-fi answers: 'Does the concept make sense to users?' *Input: Selected idea concepts. Output: Paper wireframes + storyboards + role-play script. Owner: UX Designer + Design Thinking Lead.*

**Step 8.2 — Build High-Fidelity Prototypes.** For ideas surviving low-fi testing: High-Fidelity Prototyping (interactive digital mockups in Figma — clickable, not coded, covering all key user flows); Co-Creation Workshops (6–8 users co-design specific components alongside your design team); Proof of Concept (minimum coded version of the core mechanic for technical validation). Document each with a spec sheet: prototype ID, hypothesis, the interaction, expected user response. *Input: Low-fi outputs, user feedback. Output: Clickable hi-fi prototypes + co-creation outputs + PoC demo. Owner: UX Designer + Developer.*

**Step 8.3 — Define the MVP.** List all features in the full solution. Rate each MoSCoW (Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, Won't Have). The MVP includes all M features only. Write the MVP Definition document: user story per M feature, acceptance criteria, technical dependencies, success metrics. *Input: Hi-fi prototypes, feasibility assessment. Output: MVP Definition document. Owner: Product Manager + Tech Lead.*

### Deliverable
Lo-Fi Prototypes + Hi-Fi Prototypes + MVP Definition Document

### Exit Gate
At least one prototype per idea is ready for user testing.

## Phase 9 — Test: Validate With Real Users Before Building at Scale

### Goal
Return prototypes to real users, gather structured feedback, identify remaining gaps, and iterate until the solution is validated for development or scaling.

### Key Question
Does our solution actually work for users in practice, and what must we fix before we build it at scale?

### Steps

**Step 9.1 — Plan and Execute Usability Testing.** Build a Testing Sheet for each prototype: hypothesis, tasks the user will attempt, success metrics, observation prompts. Conduct Usability Testing with 5–8 participants (Nielsen's law: 5 users find 85% of usability issues). Run 60-min sessions: 5-min intro, 10-min background, 35-min task-based testing (think aloud), 10-min debrief. Run Heuristic Evaluation in parallel: 3 UX experts evaluate independently against Nielsen's 10 heuristics, rating violations as severity 0–4. *Input: Hi-fi prototypes, user recruitment. Output: Usability test report, heuristic evaluation. Owner: UX Researcher.*

**Step 9.2 — Run Specialized Testing Methods.** For digital prototypes: A/B Testing (two variants, equal samples, ≥95% statistical significance required to declare a winner); Eye Tracking (Tobii or equivalent, generates heatmap overlay showing where users look vs. don't look); Accessibility Testing (WCAG 2.1 AA: color contrast, screen-reader, keyboard navigation, touch targets); Performance Testing (page load and interaction latency — flag > 3 seconds). Document all findings in the Feedback Capture Grid: Positives / Negatives / Questions / Ideas. *Input: Hi-fi prototypes, analytics tools. Output: A/B results, heatmaps, WCAG compliance report, Feedback Capture Grid. Owner: UX Researcher + Analytics.*

**Step 9.3 — Synthesize, Iterate & Decide.** Consolidate findings into a prioritized issues list: Critical (blocks task completion — must fix), Major (significant friction — fix next iteration), Minor (annoyance — fix if time). Generate design fix per critical/major issue, update prototype, re-test. After 2–3 rounds: if ≥90% task completion and no Critical issues, proceed to development. Write Go/No-Go recommendation with rationale. *Input: All testing outputs, prioritized issues. Output: Iteration log, updated prototype, Go/No-Go recommendation. Owner: Design Thinking Lead + Sponsor.*

### Deliverable
Validated Solution + Testing Evidence Package (test reports, heatmaps, iteration log, Go/No-Go recommendation)

### Exit Gate
Sponsor approves Go/No-Go. 'Go' triggers development resourcing; 'No-Go' returns to Ideate or Prototype.

## Worked End-to-End Example: Airbnb's Revenue-Doubling CX Redesign

This example traces how Airbnb applied both tracks of this toolkit to redesign the host and guest experience, reportedly doubling revenue within a week of one critical design intervention.

**Phase 1 (Strategic Foundation):** In 2009, Airbnb's corporate strategy was growth-at-all-costs in the short-term rental market. The CX vision was: 'Be the world's most trusted platform for unique travel experiences.' The Maturity Assessment revealed a critical gap: 'design and delivery' was at Level 1 (naïve) — hosts were uploading blurry phone photos of their listings, making bookings nearly impossible.

**Phase 2 (Objectives & Organization):** The strategic objective became: 'Improve host listing quality to drive first-time guest booking conversion.' The primary KPI: listing-to-booking conversion rate. Budget: a $3,000 experiment (professional photography for 20 NYC listings).

**Phase 3 (Segments & Journey Mapping):** The priority segments were (1) first-time hosts in major cities and (2) first-time guests browsing but not booking. Journey mapping revealed the critical Moment of Truth: the 'browsing listings' stage had a massive emotional drop — guests were interested but distrusted low-quality photos.

**Phase 4 (Initiatives & Roadmap):** The Quick Win initiative: 'Professional Host Photography Program' — offer free professional photography to hosts in top 10 markets. Impact estimate: 2–3x improvement in listing conversion (based on analog from hotel booking sites).

**Phase 5 (Empathize):** Co-founders Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia personally visited NYC hosts (DILO), photographing apartments and interviewing both hosts and potential guests. Research insight: 'Guests don't trust listings with phone-camera photos. They need to see the space as they would a hotel room.'

**Phase 6 (Define):** Problem Statement: 'First-time guests browsing Airbnb need a way to trust that what they see online matches reality, because today's photos make listings look unprofessional — we'll know we've solved it when listing conversion rate doubles.' Root cause via Five Whys: hosts don't know how to photograph their space (→ don't own the right equipment → → believe Airbnb should handle it → → Airbnb has no standard → → Airbnb hasn't prioritized host enablement). Value Proposition: for hosts — 'Get more bookings with no effort'; for guests — 'Book with confidence.'

**Phase 7 (Ideate):** HMW questions included: 'How might we make every listing look as trustworthy as a hotel?' Ideas generated: (1) mandatory photo standards (low effort, rejected — unenforceable); (2) AI photo enhancement (high effort, long timeline); (3) free professional photography (medium effort, immediate quality impact — selected). NABC evaluation scored it highest on Need and Benefit.

**Phase 8 (Prototype):** Low-fi prototype: a simple email to 20 NYC hosts offering free photography. Hi-fi: an on-platform booking flow where 'Verified Photos' badge appeared on photographed listings. Storyboard: guest sees 'Verified Photos' badge, clicks listing, books with confidence.

**Phase 9 (Test):** Usability testing (20 users): task 'find and book a listing in NYC' — listings with professional photos completed the booking task at 3x the rate of unverified listings. A/B Test: professional-photo listings vs. self-photo listings — conversion rate +240%, statistical significance p < 0.001. Go decision: scale the photography program globally.

**Result:** Revenue doubled within one week of deploying professional photography at scale. This is cited as one of the most impactful CX interventions in startup history — enabled by the discipline of observing real users (Empathize), diagnosing the root cause (Define), selecting the right solution (Ideate), testing cheaply (Prototype), and validating before scaling (Test).

## Pitfalls & Best Practices

### The 8 Most Common CX Transformation Failure Modes

**1. Strategy without empathy.** Building a CX roadmap from internal opinions rather than from observed user behavior. Every initiative in Wave 1 must trace back to a validated pain point from the research phase.

**2. Journey maps as wallpaper.** Spending weeks building beautiful journey maps that are never used to drive decisions. Fix: mandate that every initiative in the portfolio must reference at least one Moment of Truth from the journey map.

**3. Skipping the Define phase.** Going straight from observations to solutions without crafting a precise Problem Statement. Solving the wrong problem at high fidelity is expensive. The Problem Statement Card Sort test (80% 'clearly solvable' threshold) is non-negotiable.

**4. Low-fi to production.** Skipping the prototyping phase entirely and building the full solution based on the idea alone. Five users in a 3-hour usability test will find issues that would have cost 10x more to fix in production.

**5. One round of testing.** Running one usability test and declaring success. Design thinking requires 2–3 test-and-iterate cycles. Expect critical issues in round 1.

**6. No executive gate structure.** Design thinking projects without explicit phase gates drift indefinitely. Enforce the gates in this toolkit: sponsor sign-off at Problem Statement (Phase 6), idea selection (Phase 7), and Go/No-Go (Phase 9).

**7. Confusing CX and UX.** Customer Experience is the full lifecycle relationship (awareness → advocacy), including people, process, and technology. UX is the digital interaction layer. The CX Strategy track (Track A) must not be reduced to a UX redesign.

**8. Building the wrong MVP.** MVPs bloated with 'should have' features cost 2–3x more and take 2–3x longer. Enforce MoSCoW ruthlessly in Phase 8: only M features go into the MVP.

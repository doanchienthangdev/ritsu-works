# Crazy 8s

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A rapid visual ideation technique in which each participant sketches eight distinct solution concepts in eight minutes — forcing the team to move quickly past the obvious first ideas to more creative and unexpected solutions.

**Origin:** Popularized by Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky, and Braden Kowitz in the Google Ventures Design Sprint methodology (2016); rooted in the IDEO tradition of 'quantity before quality' divergent ideation.

## Why it works
The first 2–3 ideas that come to anyone's mind are typically the obvious, safe, and already-tried solutions. The pressure of generating 8 concepts in 8 minutes (1 minute per concept) forces participants to exhaust the obvious and reach for more creative territory. Sketching (rather than writing) is key: visual thinking activates different cognitive pathways and produces concrete, testable concepts rather than abstract descriptions.

## When to use
Use in the Ideate phase immediately after HMW questions have been defined and before the full Brainstorming session, to activate visual thinking and establish a quantity-first norm for the workshop.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Give each participant one A4 sheet of paper. Ask them to fold it twice (creating 8 equal panels) and number each panel 1–8.
2. 2. Announce the HMW question or Problem Statement that will anchor the exercise.
3. 3. Set a timer for 8 minutes (1 minute per panel). Announce that participants must fill all 8 panels — quality is less important than quantity and variety.
4. 4. Participants sketch one concept per panel using simple visuals: boxes, arrows, stick figures, speech bubbles. Add a 1–3 word label below each sketch. Absolutely no detailed explanation — this is a sketch, not a wireframe.
5. 5. When the timer ends, each participant presents their 8 concepts in 2 minutes: simply say what each one is, without defending it.
6. 6. Post all sheets on the wall. Use dot voting (2 dots per person) to identify the most promising concepts.
7. 7. Ask participants to select their one strongest concept (voted or personal choice) and develop it into a 3-panel storyboard for the Prototype phase.
8. 8. Document all sketches digitally before the session ends — photographs, not redrawing.

## Real-life example — Slack
During an early design sprint for Slack's notification system, a Crazy 8s session produced 64 concepts from 8 participants in 8 minutes. The obvious first ideas (concept 1 in each participant's sheet) were all variations of the existing notification model. Concepts 6–8 from two participants — generated when they'd exhausted the obvious — independently converged on a 'snooze until I'm done with this conversation' idea. This concept, which appeared in 2 of 64 sketches and would never have survived a verbal brainstorm dominated by engineers, became Slack's 'Do Not Disturb' feature after user testing confirmed it was the most requested missing capability.

**So what:** The ideas in positions 6–8 of a Crazy 8s session are the ones worth testing — they come from beyond the obvious.

## Pitfalls
- Stopping at 4 or 5 panels: participants often 'run out of ideas' at panel 5 and leave the remaining panels blank. Counter: enforce the rule that blank panels are not allowed — even a placeholder concept ('something completely different') counts.
- Detailed sketches instead of rapid concepts: participants who draw detailed wireframes finish 2 panels in 8 minutes. Counter: explicitly say 'I should be able to understand this sketch in 5 seconds' before starting the timer.
- The most senior person's sketch getting all the votes: in hierarchical organizations, dot voting can be influenced by status. Counter: collect all sketches anonymously (remove names) before posting them on the wall.

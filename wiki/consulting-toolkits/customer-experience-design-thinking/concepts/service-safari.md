# Service Safari

*Category: research · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
An experiential research technique in which the researcher personally experiences a service as a real customer would — going through the full end-to-end journey across all channels — and scores each step using an emotion scale to identify the moments that delight, frustrate, or confuse real customers.

**Origin:** Developed at the Design Council (UK) and popularized in service design practice by Engine Service Design and IDEO. A standard component of the Empathize toolkit in the Stanford d.school Design Thinking Bootleg.

## Why it works
Desk research and analytics tell you where customers drop off; a Service Safari tells you what it feels like to drop off and why. Experiencing the service firsthand — not through reports or recordings — creates genuine empathy and often reveals structural problems (like being unable to speak to a human without a 30-minute hold) that internal staff have stopped noticing because they navigate the service differently from customers.

## When to use
Use in the Empathize phase at the start of any CX or service design initiative, before building journey maps. Especially valuable when the team lacks first-hand customer experience of the full service journey. Also use quarterly as a CX monitoring tool.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the journey scope: which customer journey will you experience, and across which channels? Write a complete channel list (e.g., website → email sign-up → onboarding call → first use → support → renewal).
2. 2. Create a fictional customer profile that matches your primary persona — name, goal, context — and play this role throughout the safari. Do not reveal you are a researcher.
3. 3. Execute the full journey as a real customer would, including every wait, every click, every phone hold, and every physical interaction. Do not shortcut any step.
4. 4. At each touchpoint, record: what you did, how long it took, what you expected vs. what happened, and an emotion score (1=very negative, 5=very positive). Photograph every screen, physical space, or document encountered.
5. 5. After completing the journey, plot the emotion curve: a line graph showing emotion score at each touchpoint, revealing where the journey peaks (delight) and valleys (frustration).
6. 6. Annotate the valleys: these are candidate Moments of Truth and pain points. For each valley, write a 1-sentence description of why the emotion dropped.
7. 7. Present the photo-annotated emotion curve to the team. Use 'I felt...' statements to convey the emotional reality, not just the logical events.
8. 8. Use the Service Safari findings as validation data for the Customer Journey Map and as input to the Affinity Mapping session.

## Real-life example — Ritz-Carlton
The Ritz-Carlton uses a form of competitive Service Safari as part of its annual 'Competitive Intelligence' process: employees are sent to experience competitor luxury hotels as regular guests, scoring each touchpoint on a 1–5 emotion scale. In one documented safari, the research revealed that a competitor scored 5/5 on room quality and 1/5 on check-in process — specifically, the 20-minute wait at the front desk destroyed the first impression even for guests who loved the room. Ritz-Carlton's response: the 'Warm Welcome' protocol — a concierge meets guests at their car, handles the paperwork before they reach the desk, and delivers them directly to their room. This protocol was designed in direct response to Service Safari findings from a competitor visit.

**So what:** Experiencing competitors' service is as valuable as experiencing your own — the emotion curve reveals what no analytics report can.

## Pitfalls
- Staying in researcher mode: researchers who don't commit to the customer role (skipping steps, using back channels, revealing their identity) produce invalid data. Counter: commit to the fictional persona for the full journey duration.
- Scoring before experiencing the full touchpoint: teams rate 'phone hold' as 1/5 before experiencing the hold music and resolution. Counter: record the score only at the end of the touchpoint, not during it.
- Safari of only your own service: the most valuable Service Safaris include at least one direct competitor and one 'analogous' service (a company in a different industry that solves a similar emotional problem). Counter: plan at least 3 safaris: your service, your top competitor, and an analog.

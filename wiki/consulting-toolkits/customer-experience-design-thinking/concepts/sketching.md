# Sketching (UX / Service Design Sketching)

*Category: prototyping · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
The fastest form of prototyping — hand-drawn visual representations of user interfaces, service interactions, or physical experiences using paper, pens, and markers — allowing design teams to make ideas tangible and testable within minutes, before any digital tools are involved.

**Origin:** A foundational practice in industrial and product design; formalized for UX and service design by Bill Buxton in 'Sketching User Experiences' (2007) and adopted by IDEO as the first step in the Prototype phase.

## Why it works
The cost of a sketch is near zero; the cost of a coded feature is 100–1000x higher. Sketching moves the design conversation from 'what do you mean by that?' to 'do you mean this?' — making abstract concepts concrete enough for meaningful feedback in minutes. The roughness of a sketch also signals to users that the design is not final, making them more willing to offer honest critical feedback.

## When to use
Use as the first prototyping step in Phase 8, before any digital tools are used. Also use throughout the ideation phase to make abstract ideas concrete during discussion.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Gather materials: paper, pens (fine-tip black for detail, thick-tip for borders), and sticky notes for labels.
2. 2. Start with a bounding rectangle representing the screen or physical space.
3. 3. Sketch the key elements of the interface or service at the most important step in the storyboard. Use boxes for images, lines for text, rectangles for buttons, and an 'X' through a box for images.
4. 4. Label each element clearly. Add arrows to show navigation flow or user actions.
5. 5. Set a 15-minute time limit per sketch — rough is better than refined. If you can explain the sketch in 5 seconds, it is complete.
6. 6. Show the sketch to 2–3 team members and one user (if available): 'Walk me through what you think is happening on this screen.' Where they misunderstand, revise.
7. 7. Iterate: 3–4 sketch revisions can be done in the time it takes to open a design software application.

## Real-life example — Twitter
Twitter's original product concept was communicated as a sketch on a napkin by Jack Dorsey in 2006: a central field for a status update, a list of friends below it, and a send button. This sketch was shown to potential investors and early users before any code existed. The feedback from the sketch — 'What does it mean to post to all your followers vs. just one friend?' — directly shaped the decision to make all tweets public by default. A coded prototype of this question would have taken weeks; the sketch answered it in hours.

**So what:** A sketch communicates the core concept faster than any description — and gets honest feedback before any investment is locked in.

## Pitfalls
- Spending too long on a single sketch: teams who sketch for 60+ minutes are drawing, not prototyping. Counter: if a sketch takes more than 15 minutes to communicate clearly, the concept needs to be simplified, not the sketch refined.
- Skipping sketching and going straight to digital: digital wireframes in Figma or Balsamiq take 5–10x longer to produce than paper sketches. Counter: use paper sketching for all idea validation; use digital tools only for hi-fi prototyping after the concept is validated.

# User Interviews

*Category: research · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A qualitative research method in which a trained researcher conducts one-on-one structured conversations with representative users to understand their goals, behaviors, frustrations, and mental models in relation to a specific experience or challenge.

**Origin:** Rooted in clinical psychology interview techniques (Carl Rogers, 1940s) and adapted for market and UX research; standardized in UX practice by Steve Portigal ('Interviewing Users', 2013) and established as the primary design research method by IDEO.

## Why it works
Surveys tell you what people do; interviews tell you why. The one-on-one format creates psychological safety for users to reveal frustrations, workarounds, and mental models they would never share in a group. Open-ended questions and active probing with 'Tell me more' and 'Why?' unlock the latent needs that drive behavior — the needs users cannot articulate in a checkbox survey.

## When to use
Use as the primary research method in the Empathize phase of every Design Thinking initiative. Use as a supplement to surveys whenever quantitative data reveals a 'what' but not the 'why.' Use every 6–12 months to refresh personas.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define your research questions: what are the 3–5 things you must understand by the end of the research? These drive your guide, not the other way around.
2. 2. Recruit 8–12 participants who represent your target segment. For consumer research: use a screener survey with 5–7 qualifying questions. For B2B: use purposive sampling from your CRM.
3. 3. Write a semi-structured interview guide with 8–12 open questions. Start with 'warm-up' questions (role, context, routine) before the core questions. Never include yes/no questions.
4. 4. Pilot the guide with one person before the full research begins — adjust questions that feel confusing or leading.
5. 5. Conduct each interview in the user's natural environment where possible (their home, office, or the location where they use the product). Remote interviews are acceptable with video.
6. 6. Open with: 'There are no right or wrong answers. We're here to learn from your experience, not to test you.' This reduces social desirability bias.
7. 7. Use the TEDW probe sequence: Tell me more; Explain what you mean; Describe what that was like; Walk me through that moment. Never ask 'Why did you do that?' (accusatory) — instead ask 'What was going through your mind when you did that?'
8. 8. Probe five levels deep on any answer that reveals an insight — this is the Five Whys in conversational form.
9. 9. After each session, write a 'debrief note' within 30 minutes: top 3 surprises, top 3 confirmations, one unexpected observation.
10. 10. Transcribe all sessions (use AI transcription + human review for accuracy) before beginning affinity mapping.

## Real-life example — Intercom
When Intercom was designing its onboarding experience for new SaaS customers, user interviews revealed that 70% of participants felt 'abandoned' after signup — a word that appeared in 9 of 12 interviews without prompting. The key probe that unlocked this: 'Walk me through the first time you logged into Intercom.' Respondents described opening the dashboard, seeing a complex UI with no guidance, and feeling responsible for figuring it out alone. This single insight drove the creation of Intercom's onboarding 'activation checklist,' which is now a standard feature. The word 'abandoned' became the design team's rallying cry for the entire redesign.

**So what:** A word that appears unprompted in 9 of 12 interviews is a signal, not a coincidence — user interviews create statistically grounded qualitative insight when conducted rigorously.

## Template
Adapt the questions below to your specific design challenge. Always start with warm-up questions. Never show the product before completing the open-ended exploration.

- [ ] Research questions this guide addresses: ___
- [ ] Participant profile (screener criteria): ___
- [ ] Warm-up (5 min): 'Tell me a bit about yourself and your role / typical day.'
- [ ] Context question 1: 'Walk me through the last time you [relevant experience].'
- [ ] Context question 2: 'What was going through your mind when [critical moment]?'
- [ ] Pain point probe: 'What's the most frustrating part of [experience]?'
- [ ] Frequency probe: 'How often does [problem behavior] happen?'
- [ ] Workaround probe: 'What do you do when [pain point occurs]?'
- [ ] Ideal state probe: 'If you could change one thing about [experience], what would it be?'
- [ ] Debrief (5 min): 'Is there anything else you'd like to share that we haven't talked about?'
- [ ] Post-session debrief note: Top 3 surprises: ___ | Top 3 confirmations: ___ | Unexpected observation: ___

## Pitfalls
- Leading questions: asking 'Don't you find the checkout process confusing?' biases the answer. Counter: review every question in the guide and mark any that contains the answer — rephrase as 'Tell me about your experience with the checkout process.'
- Stopping at the first answer: most users give socially acceptable first answers ('It's fine'). The real insight is 2–4 probes deeper. Counter: never accept a first answer — always use one TEDW probe before moving on.
- Too many participants, too little synthesis: 25 poorly synthesized interviews produce less insight than 10 deeply analyzed ones. Counter: allocate equal time to synthesis (2–3 days) as to field work.

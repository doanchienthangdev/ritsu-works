# CX KPI Hierarchy

*Category: measurement · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A structured, three-tier framework for defining and cascading customer experience performance metrics — from boardroom Outcome KPIs (NPS, CSAT, churn) through Experience KPIs (interaction-level satisfaction) to Operational KPIs (process efficiency drivers) — creating a coherent measurement system that connects day-to-day operations to strategic CX ambitions.

**Origin:** Synthesized from Bain & Company's NPS System (Fred Reichheld, 2003), Forrester's Customer Experience Index, and Temkin Group's CX measurement frameworks. The three-tier hierarchy structure is widely used in McKinsey and Bain CX engagements.

## Why it works
CX programs fail when the metrics they measure are too far removed from the levers teams can actually pull. A nurse cannot directly improve NPS — but she can reduce wait time (Operational KPI) and be more empathetic during handoffs (Experience KPI), both of which roll up to NPS (Outcome KPI). The three-tier hierarchy makes this connection explicit, giving every team a measurable target that they can influence while maintaining a clear line of sight to the outcome metrics that matter to the board.

## When to use
Use in Phase 2 (CX Strategy: Objectives & Organization) to build the measurement architecture. Use as the foundation for the CX Balanced Scorecard and the CX dashboard. Review and recalibrate annually.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the Outcome KPIs first: select 3–5 metrics that are directly tied to the CX Vision and that the board can track. NPS and CSAT are almost always included; add CLV or churn rate for retention-focused strategies.
2. 2. For each Outcome KPI, identify the 2–3 customer journey stages that most influence it (from the Journey Map). These stages become the focus of Experience KPIs.
3. 3. Define Experience KPIs for each priority journey stage: a satisfaction score specific to that stage (post-interaction survey) plus 1–2 time or resolution metrics (e.g., time-to-first-resolution for the support stage).
4. 4. For each Experience KPI, identify the 2–3 operational processes or frontline behaviors that most drive it. These become the Operational KPIs.
5. 5. Assign an owner to every KPI: Outcome KPIs are owned by the CCO; Experience KPIs by business unit heads; Operational KPIs by team leads.
6. 6. Set baselines (current performance) and targets (12-month goal) for every KPI, using industry benchmarks for target-setting.
7. 7. Map the full KPI hierarchy in a visual tree: one branch per Outcome KPI, expanding to Experience and Operational sub-metrics. Every leaf node (Operational KPI) should have a traceable path to at least one Outcome KPI.

## Real-life example — Amazon
Amazon's CX measurement hierarchy is one of the most cited examples in the industry. The Outcome KPI is 'Customer Lifetime Value' (CLV). This cascades to Experience KPIs including 'percentage of orders delivered by promise date' (the #1 driver of CLV per Amazon's internal research) and 'percentage of contacts resolved on first interaction.' These cascade to Operational KPIs including 'warehouse pick accuracy rate', 'carrier on-time pickup compliance', and 'agent first-contact resolution rate.' Amazon's legendary 'customer obsession' is operationalized through this hierarchy — every warehouse worker can see how their pick accuracy rate affects delivery promise fulfillment, which affects CLV.

**So what:** The KPI hierarchy makes 'customer obsession' measurable at every level of the organization.

## Pitfalls
- Too many KPIs: organizations that track 40+ KPIs track nothing — focus is lost. Counter: enforce a maximum of 5 Outcome KPIs, 10 Experience KPIs, and 20 Operational KPIs. Every KPI beyond these limits must replace an existing one.
- Outcome KPIs without operational linkage: a board that tracks NPS but whose frontline teams have no KPIs traceable to it will see NPS move for reasons no one can explain. Counter: never approve an Outcome KPI without tracing it to at least 2 Operational KPIs.
- Measuring what is easy, not what matters: teams gravitate toward KPIs they can already measure (call handle time) rather than KPIs that actually drive satisfaction (post-interaction resolution quality). Counter: start with the Outcome KPI and work backward to determine what must be measured, not forward from what is already available.

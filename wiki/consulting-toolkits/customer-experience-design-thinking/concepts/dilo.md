# DILO (Day in the Life Of)

*Category: research · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
An ethnographic research technique in which a researcher shadows a specific user or customer through their complete working day (or a representative portion of it), observing and documenting every action, interaction, emotion, and decision in their natural environment — producing a rich, context-grounded picture of their real experience.

**Origin:** Originally a management consulting tool (used by McKinsey, Bain, and KPMG to map employee workload and process inefficiency); adapted into design thinking and CX research as a primary empathy tool. IDEO and the Stanford d.school formalized it as the 'shadowing' approach in the Empathize phase.

## Why it works
What people do and what they say they do are different. Surveys and interviews capture self-reported behavior, which is filtered through memory, social desirability, and limited self-awareness. DILO captures actual behavior in real time: the workarounds users have normalized and forgotten to mention, the moments of frustration they've accepted as inevitable, and the contextual factors (interruptions, environment, social dynamics) that shape the experience.

## When to use
Use as a primary research method in the Empathize phase, especially when the design challenge involves a complex, multi-step user process (e.g., professional workflows, multi-channel customer journeys). Use when user interviews alone produce overly abstract descriptions of the experience.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Brief the participant 48 hours in advance: explain the purpose (learning about their experience, not evaluating their performance), what you will do (observe, photograph, take notes — not participate or interfere), and what you need from them (narrate their actions where natural, but continue their normal work).
2. 2. Arrive 15 minutes early. Set up observation materials: notepad with time-stamped log, camera/phone for photos, and an observation template (columns: Time | Activity | Quote/Verbatim | Emotion | Observer note).
3. 3. During the observation: record every action with a timestamp. Photograph artifacts (screens, documents, physical tools) with permission. Annotate emotional states using a 1–5 scale at each major activity change.
4. 4. Do not help, suggest, or intervene — even when the participant makes an obvious mistake or asks for assistance. Note the assistance-seeking behavior itself.
5. 5. Run a 15-minute midday check-in: ask 'What's been the most frustrating part of your morning?' and 'What task do you most look forward to this afternoon?' This surfaces context the observation alone might miss.
6. 6. Run a 30-minute end-of-day debrief: 'Walk me through the moment today where you felt most frustrated' and 'What would make tomorrow significantly easier?' Record verbatim answers.
7. 7. Write your observer debrief note within 1 hour of leaving: top 3 surprises (things you observed that you didn't expect), top 3 confirmations (things that matched your prior assumptions), and the single most impactful observation of the day.
8. 8. Use the DILO data as input to the Empathy Map and Affinity Mapping session.

## Real-life example — Airbnb (founding team)
In 2009, Brian Chesky conducted informal DILO sessions by visiting 20 Airbnb hosts in New York, spending 2–4 hours at each listing and observing how hosts prepared their space, interacted with the Airbnb platform, and felt about hosting strangers. Key observation: hosts were uploading blurry phone photos because they had no guidance and no professional equipment — they didn't realize this was destroying booking conversion. Hosts didn't mention this in any survey because they didn't know what 'good' photography looked like. The DILO observation, not any interview or survey, revealed the photo quality problem that the Professional Photography Program later fixed.

**So what:** The most valuable DILO observation is the problem the user has normalized and no longer notices.

## Pitfalls
- Intervening during the observation: the moment you help, you've invalidated the observation of the struggle. Counter: brief the participant in advance that you will not be able to help, and brief yourself to stay silent even when it's uncomfortable.
- Observing only the 'best' users: organizations often route DILO researchers to their most proficient users ('you'll want to shadow Sarah — she's our best'). Counter: explicitly recruit the median user, not the champion user.
- Forgetting to photograph artifacts: the most revealing data in a DILO session is often physical or on-screen — the spreadsheet the user maintains because the system doesn't support what they need, the sticky note on the monitor with the workaround steps. Counter: photograph everything with permission.

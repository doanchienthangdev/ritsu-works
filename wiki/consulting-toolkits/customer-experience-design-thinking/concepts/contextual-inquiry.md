# Contextual Inquiry

*Category: research · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A structured field research method that combines observation and interview in the user's natural environment — the researcher observes the user performing a task, then intermittently asks questions about specific actions observed, creating a master-apprentice dialogue that surfaces the reasoning behind behavior.

**Origin:** Developed by Hugh Beyer and Karen Holtzblatt at Digital Equipment Corporation (1988); formalized in their book 'Contextual Design' (1997). A foundational method in the UX research canon.

## Why it works
Contextual inquiry is more powerful than either observation or interviews alone because it captures both the behavior and the mental model behind it, in context. The master-apprentice model (researcher as apprentice, user as master of their own domain) creates a dynamic where users naturally reveal their expertise, workarounds, and implicit knowledge that they would not think to mention in a traditional interview.

## When to use
Use for design challenges involving complex professional workflows (enterprise software, healthcare, financial services). Especially valuable when the gap between how users say they work and how they actually work is likely to be large.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Brief the participant: 'I'd like to watch you do [task] and ask questions as you go. Think of me as a student who is trying to understand how you work.'
2. 2. Ask the participant to perform a real task (not a simulated one). Observe without intervening.
3. 3. Interrupt with questions only when you observe something you don't understand: 'I noticed you just [action] — can you tell me why?' or 'You hesitated there — what was going through your mind?'
4. 4. Follow the participant's work flow, not a script. The session goes wherever the task goes.
5. 5. At the end, run a 15-minute debrief: 'What was the most challenging part of what you just did?' and 'Is there anything about how you do this that would surprise someone who doesn't know this work?'
6. 6. Write your observer notes within 30 minutes of the session.

## Real-life example — SAP
SAP UX teams use contextual inquiry as the primary research method for enterprise software redesign. In a redesign of SAP's inventory management module, contextual inquiry sessions with warehouse managers revealed that managers had created an elaborate system of paper notes alongside the software because the system could not show cross-location inventory in one view. This workaround — invisible in any survey — became the design brief for the 'Unified Inventory View' feature that reduced manual reconciliation by 70%.

**So what:** Contextual inquiry surfaces the workarounds that users have normalized and will never volunteer in an interview.

## Pitfalls
- Following a pre-planned script instead of the task: contextual inquiry sessions that stick to a question list miss the dynamic nature of real work. Counter: the only questions in the guide are the warm-up and debrief — all other questions arise from observations.
- Contaminating the task: asking 'Was that hard?' plants a suggestion. Counter: use neutral probes: 'Tell me about what just happened' rather than evaluative questions.

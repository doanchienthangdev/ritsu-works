# How Might We (HMW) Questions

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A facilitation technique that reframes pain points and problem statements as open, optimistic questions beginning with 'How might we...' — transforming barriers into invitations for creative ideation and anchoring brainstorming to real user needs.

**Origin:** Originated at Procter & Gamble in the 1970s; popularized by IDEO and the Stanford d.school as the standard bridge between the Define and Ideate phases of design thinking.

## Why it works
The phrasing 'How might we' does three things simultaneously: 'How' assumes a solution exists (it's not 'Can we' or 'Should we'); 'might' implies there are multiple possible answers (it's not 'should' or 'must'); 'we' establishes collective ownership. This framing prevents two failure modes: ideation anchored to a solution the team already has in mind, and ideation so broad it's useless.

## When to use
Use as the mandatory bridge between Define and Ideate — the last output of the Define phase and the first input to the Ideate phase. Also use when an ideation session is drifting or producing solutions rather than exploring the problem space.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Gather all pain points and insight statements from the Affinity Map and Empathy Map.
2. 2. For each pain point, rewrite it as a HMW question. Pain point: 'Users feel abandoned after signing up because no one follows up.' HMW: 'How might we make users feel supported and guided in their first 48 hours without requiring manual intervention?'
3. 3. Aim for 1–3 HMW questions per pain point, varying the scope: one narrow (specific feature), one medium (interaction), one broad (relationship).
4. 4. Check each question against three tests: (a) Is it too narrow? ('How might we add a welcome email?' — too close to a solution.) (b) Is it too broad? ('How might we transform our business model?' — too open.) (c) Does it encode the user's perspective, not the company's?
5. 5. Post all HMW questions on the wall before the ideation session begins. Have participants spend 5 minutes reading all questions silently.
6. 6. Ask participants to vote on the 3–5 HMW questions they find most inspiring or most important (2 votes each).
7. 7. Run ideation (Brainstorming, Crazy 8s, Mind Mapping) anchored to the top-voted HMW questions, one at a time.

## Real-life example — IDEO / Douglas County School System
When IDEO redesigned the school experience for Douglas County in Colorado, the research phase revealed a critical pain point: 'Students disengage from learning after repeated failure.' The team generated 12 HMW questions, including: 'How might we make failure feel like progress?'; 'How might we help teachers notice disengagement before it becomes dropout?'; 'How might we give students agency over how they demonstrate mastery?' The HMW question 'How might we make failure feel like progress?' led to a game-design-inspired feedback system where students earned 'learning badges' for attempting challenges rather than for correct answers — an insight that would not have emerged from a problem statement focused on reducing failure rates.

**So what:** The HMW question you choose determines the solution space. A well-chosen HMW unlocks solutions that data analysis alone cannot reach.

## Pitfalls
- HMW questions that are really solutions: 'How might we build a chatbot?' is not a HMW question — it's a solution. Counter: reject any HMW question that names a technology, channel, or feature.
- Too many HMW questions: 30 HMW questions overwhelm the ideation session. Counter: vote to narrow to 3–5 before ideation begins.
- HMW questions from the company's perspective: 'How might we reduce support costs?' is a company KPI, not a user need. Counter: reframe every HMW from the user's perspective first.

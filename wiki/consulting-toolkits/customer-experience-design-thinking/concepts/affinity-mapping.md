# Affinity Mapping

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A collaborative synthesis technique that organizes large volumes of qualitative research data (observations, quotes, facts) into meaningful thematic clusters — transforming raw field notes into actionable insights through bottom-up pattern recognition.

**Origin:** Developed by Jiro Kawakita in the 1960s (also called the 'KJ Method'); adapted into design thinking practice by IDEO and the Stanford d.school.

## Why it works
After qualitative research, teams face a wall of raw data that resists linear analysis. Affinity Mapping uses human pattern-recognition to cluster data points by similarity, then names each cluster with a higher-level insight statement. The act of physical (or digital) sorting also forces team members to engage deeply with the data rather than delegating synthesis to one analyst.

## When to use
Use immediately after completing qualitative research (user interviews, DILO sessions, ethnography). Use whenever the team is overwhelmed by the volume of qualitative data and needs to find patterns quickly.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Write each research observation, quote, or fact on a separate sticky note (one idea per note). Aim for 100–300 notes from 8+ research sessions.
2. 2. Post all sticky notes randomly on the wall — do not organize them yet.
3. 3. In silence, team members begin moving similar notes together — do not discuss or debate during the silent sort phase (5–10 minutes).
4. 4. Once initial clusters form, begin talking — discuss why notes belong together, combine, and split clusters as needed (20–30 minutes).
5. 5. Name each cluster with an insight statement — not a topic label. A topic label is 'Pain Points in Onboarding.' An insight statement is 'Users feel abandoned in the first 48 hours because no one checks if they've set up their account correctly.'
6. 6. Identify the 5–10 most important clusters based on: frequency (large clusters = common experience), emotional intensity (high-emotion quotes), and strategic relevance (maps to a Moment of Truth).
7. 7. Photograph or digitize the final affinity map. Convert each insight statement into a 'How Might We' question for ideation.

## Real-life example — IBM Design
IBM's Design Thinking practice uses Affinity Mapping as a standard post-research ritual called 'Big Room Thinking.' In a redesign of their Cloud console, a team of 15 created 450 sticky notes from 20 enterprise user interviews. The affinity map revealed that 30% of all notes clustered around a single theme: 'Users don't know which product to start with when onboarding.' This single insight drove the redesign of the IBM Cloud homepage from a feature catalogue to a role-based onboarding wizard — a change that improved activation rate by 35%.

**So what:** The cluster that surprises you — the theme you didn't expect — is often the most valuable finding. Affinity mapping surfaces it.

## Pitfalls
- Topic labels instead of insight statements: naming clusters 'Pain Points' or 'Navigation Issues' produces categories, not insights. Counter: force every cluster name to be a sentence that includes who feels what and why.
- One person driving the synthesis: the power of affinity mapping is collective sense-making. Counter: enforce the silent sort phase and require all team members to place at least 20 notes before anyone starts talking.
- Too many clusters: ending with 40 clusters is as useful as the raw data. Counter: after naming all clusters, merge any with fewer than 3 notes into adjacent clusters or label them 'outliers.'

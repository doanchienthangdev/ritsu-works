# Value Proposition Canvas

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A two-sided diagnostic and design tool that maps the alignment between a customer's Jobs-to-be-done, Pains, and Gains (customer side) and a company's Products & Services, Pain Relievers, and Gain Creators (company side) — revealing where the fit exists and where it breaks down.

**Origin:** Developed by Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur, and colleagues at Strategyzer (2014); an extension of the Business Model Canvas that zooms into the value creation mechanism.

## Why it works
Most value propositions are written from the company's perspective ('our product has feature X') rather than the customer's perspective ('this feature resolves the specific frustration of X'). The Value Proposition Canvas forces exact alignment: every product/service must map to a specific job, every pain reliever must eliminate a specific pain, every gain creator must deliver a specific gain. This creates a diagnostic: wherever there is no line connecting the company side to the customer side, there is a value gap.

## When to use
Use in the Define phase after user research is complete, to identify value gaps that become the design brief. Use again in the Prototype phase to verify that the proposed solution closes the previously identified gaps.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Complete the Customer Profile first (right side). This must be grounded in user research, not assumption. Document: Jobs (functional, social, emotional jobs the customer is trying to get done); Pains (what frustrates them before, during, and after the job — rank by severity); Gains (the outcomes they wish for — rank by relevance).
2. 2. Complete the Value Map second (left side). List all current Products & Services. For each, list the Pain Relievers it provides (how it reduces each pain on the right side) and the Gain Creators it delivers (how it creates each gain on the right side).
3. 3. Draw lines from each Pain Reliever to the specific Pain it addresses. Draw lines from each Gain Creator to the specific Gain it delivers.
4. 4. Identify unaddressed items: Pains on the right with no line to a Pain Reliever on the left are value gaps. Gains with no line are missed gain opportunities.
5. 5. Evaluate 'fit': strong fit = ≥80% of critical Pains (severity 4–5) have Pain Relievers; weak fit = critical Pains are unaddressed.
6. 6. Use the identified gaps to generate 'How Might We' questions for the Ideate phase.
7. 7. After ideation, re-complete the Value Map to show how the proposed solution addresses the previously identified gaps.

## Real-life example — Airbnb
Airbnb's Value Proposition Canvas for the host segment revealed a critical gap: Hosts' top Pain was 'I'm not sure my property will be safe with a stranger' (severity 5). Airbnb's existing Pain Relievers (payment guarantee, user reviews) only partially addressed this — they covered financial safety but not physical safety of property. This gap drove the creation of the Airbnb Host Guarantee (covering property damage up to $1M) and the introduction of identity verification for guests. These features, directly traceable to a value gap on the Canvas, were cited by Airbnb as key factors in accelerating host supply growth from 10,000 to 100,000 listings.

**So what:** The value gap — where no line connects customer pain to company solution — is the innovation opportunity.

## Pitfalls
- Building the Value Map before the Customer Profile: teams that start with 'what we offer' then try to fit it to customer needs produce a self-serving canvas. Counter: impose the strict sequencing: Customer Profile first, Value Map second, lines last.
- Job confusion — jobs vs. activities: listing activities ('uses the app') instead of jobs ('achieves budget control without constant checking'). Counter: every job statement must be in the format 'Verb + Object + Context.'
- Claiming fit without evidence: teams draw lines based on assumption. Counter: require that each Pain Reliever → Pain connection cites evidence from user research (a quote, an observation, or a data point).

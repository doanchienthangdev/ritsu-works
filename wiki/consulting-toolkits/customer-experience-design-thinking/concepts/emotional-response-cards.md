# Emotional Response Cards

*Category: research · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A facilitation tool consisting of a deck of 50+ emotion descriptor cards (illustrated words like 'anxious,' 'satisfied,' 'confused,' 'delighted') used in research synthesis sessions to help team members accurately and consistently label the emotional states observed in user research — making the emotional dimension of CX data precise and discussable.

**Origin:** Adapted from psychological affect research (the PANAS scale, Watson et al., 1988) and design research practice; used by IDEO and the Stanford d.school as a standard empathy synthesis tool.

## Why it works
Emotions are the primary driver of CX satisfaction — NPS and CSAT are fundamentally measures of emotional response. Yet most research synthesis sessions avoid emotional vocabulary because it feels imprecise. Emotional Response Cards provide a shared vocabulary that makes emotional labeling fast, consistent, and concrete.

## When to use
Use in every Empathy synthesis session, as part of Empathy Map construction. Also use in journey mapping workshops to populate the emotion curve with precise, evidence-based labels.

## Visual
`none`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. After each user research session, lay out the full deck of 50 cards on a table.
2. 2. Ask each observer (independently) to select the 5 cards that best describe the user's primary emotional state during the session.
3. 3. Reveal selections and discuss divergences: where two observers selected very different emotions for the same moment, probe the data to resolve.
4. 4. Build a 'emotional profile' for each user: the 5–8 most frequently selected cards across observers.
5. 5. In the Empathy Map synthesis, use the emotional profiles to populate the FEEL quadrant with precise, evidence-based emotion descriptors.
6. 6. Across all research participants, identify the 3–5 most common emotions — these are the emotional signature of the current experience.

## Real-life example — Philips Healthcare
When Philips redesigned the patient experience for MRI procedures, the research team used Emotional Response Cards after shadowing 10 MRI patients. The most selected cards across all 10 observations were 'anxious,' 'isolated,' 'confused,' and 'relieved' (only at the end). This emotional signature — anxiety→isolation→confusion→relief — became the design brief: 'How might we replace anxiety with calm, isolation with connection, and confusion with clarity throughout the MRI experience?' The redesign introduced audio narration during the procedure, visual cues visible through a mirror, and a pre-procedure 'practice run' — all directly traceable to the emotional profile from the card sort.

**So what:** When the emotional signature of an experience can be named precisely, the design brief writes itself.

## Pitfalls
- Selecting emotions the team feels comfortable with (positive bias): observers consistently select fewer negative emotion cards than are warranted by the observation data. Counter: require a minimum of 3 negative emotion cards in every selection of 5.
- Using emotional labels without evidence: labeling a user 'frustrated' without citing a specific observed behavior or quote is an assumption. Counter: require that each emotion card selected in the Empathy Map cites at least one behavioral observation as evidence.

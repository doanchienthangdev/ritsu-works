# Initiative Prioritization Matrix (Impact vs. Effort)

*Category: portfolio · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A 2x2 prioritization tool that plots all candidate CX initiatives on two axes — Impact (the expected improvement in Outcome KPIs: NPS, revenue, cost reduction) and Effort (the required investment: budget, FTE, time, complexity) — to identify Quick Wins, Major Projects, Incremental improvements, and initiatives to Deprioritize.

**Origin:** A variant of the classic McKinsey-style effort-impact matrix used in strategy and transformation programs; also related to the MoSCoW and RICE prioritization frameworks used in product management. Applied to CX initiative portfolios by Bain & Company and McKinsey's CX practices.

## Why it works
Without explicit prioritization, CX improvement programs suffer from two failure modes: (1) investing in high-impact but high-effort initiatives that never get built because they require organizational capability the company doesn't yet have; (2) building many low-impact quick wins that never move the business metrics. The 2x2 format forces a simultaneous assessment of both axes and creates a portfolio view that sequences the right investment at the right time.

## When to use
Use in Phase 4 (CX Strategy: Initiatives & Roadmap) to sequence the initiative portfolio. Also use in Design Thinking Phase 7 (Ideate) to select which ideas to prototype. Revisit quarterly as part of the CX strategy governance cycle.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. List all candidate initiatives from the CX ideation workshop (typically 15–25 after screening).
2. 2. Score each initiative on Impact (1–10): combine NPS uplift potential + revenue uplift + cost reduction (use McKinsey benchmarks: 10–20% CSAT, 10–15% conversion, 20–50% cost-to-serve as reference ranges for scoring).
3. 3. Score each initiative on Effort (1–10): combine investment required (budget in $), time to implement (months), FTE required, and organizational complexity (1=simple, 10=requires cross-business-unit change).
4. 4. Plot each initiative on the 2x2 as a dot. Initiatives in the top-left quadrant are Quick Wins (Wave 1: 0–6 months); top-right are Major Projects (Waves 2–3: 6–24 months); bottom-left are Incremental; bottom-right are Deprioritize.
5. 5. Challenge the Quick Wins: ask 'Is there a reason we haven't already done this?' — sometimes something appears low-effort because its difficulty has been underestimated.
6. 6. Challenge the Major Projects: ask 'Can we prototype this cheaply in Wave 1 (Design Thinking) to validate the impact estimate before committing to the full investment?'
7. 7. Build the 24-month roadmap from the matrix: assign initiative owners, wave timing, budget, and KPI targets to each selected initiative.
8. 8. Review the matrix quarterly and replot as new data (business cases, prototype results) changes the impact or effort estimates.

## Real-life example — T-Mobile
When T-Mobile launched its 'Un-carrier' strategy in 2013, the CX team used an impact-effort matrix to sequence its customer experience initiatives. The first three releases were Quick Wins: eliminate annual service contracts (High Impact: 8/10 — directly addressed #1 pain point in research; Low Effort: 5/10 — required a pricing policy change but no technology investment), eliminate overage charges (High Impact: 9/10; Low Effort: 4/10), and introduce unlimited music streaming (High Impact: 7/10; Low Effort: 6/10). The Major Project — building a new customer service AI to eliminate hold times — was sequenced for Wave 3 after the Quick Wins had rebuilt customer trust. NPS increased 40 points in 18 months, driven primarily by the Wave 1 Quick Wins.

**So what:** Quick Wins in a CX transformation are not 'nice to haves' — they are the trust-builders that create the organizational mandate to invest in Major Projects.

## Pitfalls
- Scoring Impact optimistically without evidence: teams systematically overestimate the impact of their favorite initiatives. Counter: require every Impact score ≥7 to cite a benchmark or analogous case (McKinsey industry average, competitor result, or business case calculation).
- Ignoring the Incremental quadrant entirely: not every initiative needs to be high-impact — some Low Impact / Low Effort initiatives can be done during Wave 1 with minimal additional resource. Counter: assess Incremental initiatives against 'Can this be done in a sprint without displacing Wave 1 priorities?'
- Static matrix: the Impact and Effort scores for initiatives change as more information becomes available (prototype test results, technology feasibility studies). Counter: mandate a quarterly matrix review and require scores to be updated when new evidence is available.

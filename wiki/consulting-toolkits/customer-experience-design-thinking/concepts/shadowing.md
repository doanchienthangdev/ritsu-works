# Shadowing (Contextual Observation)

*Category: research · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A non-participatory observation technique in which the researcher follows a user through a specific activity or time period, observing behavior, interactions, and environmental factors without interfering — generating rich contextual insight into real (not self-reported) behavior.

**Origin:** Adapted from ethnographic fieldwork methods (Bronisław Malinowski, 1920s); formalized for design research by IDEO and the Stanford d.school. Distinct from DILO in scope: shadowing focuses on a specific activity or workflow, while DILO covers an entire day.

## Why it works
Self-reported behavior and observed behavior diverge significantly — particularly for habitual or routine activities that users have stopped consciously noticing. Shadowing captures the unconscious workarounds, the social dynamics that shape behavior, and the environmental factors (noise, interruptions, physical constraints) that self-report cannot surface.

## When to use
Use in the Empathize phase for any design challenge involving a specific, repeatable workflow or activity. Pair with user interviews to triangulate self-reported and observed behavior.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the specific activity or workflow to shadow (e.g., 'a nurse completing a patient handoff at shift change').
2. 2. Brief the participant: explain that you will observe but not participate, and that you are interested in understanding their experience, not evaluating their performance.
3. 3. Take position where you can observe clearly without being in the way. Use a time-stamped observation log.
4. 4. Record every action, every tool used, every verbal interaction, and every visible emotional response. Photograph artifacts with permission.
5. 5. Do not intervene — even if the participant makes an error. Note the error as an observation.
6. 6. Debrief the participant immediately after the activity: 'Walk me through what just happened. What was the most difficult moment?'

## Real-life example — IDEO / Kaiser Permanente
IDEO shadowed nurses during shift-change handoffs at Kaiser Permanente hospitals. Observation revealed that nurses consistently skipped reading the written handoff notes because they were too long and poorly formatted — instead they relied on verbal briefings that frequently missed critical information. This observation (which nurses did not mention in interviews because they normalized the workaround) directly shaped the structured handoff protocol redesign.

**So what:** The behavior that participants don't mention in interviews because they've normalized it is often the most valuable observation in a shadowing session.

## Pitfalls
- Observer effect: participants modify their behavior when observed. Counter: allow a 15-minute warm-up period before formal observation begins — most people normalize the observer's presence quickly.
- Observing only the easy part of the activity: researchers sometimes avoid observing the most stressful or private parts of a workflow. Counter: explicitly plan to observe the complete activity cycle, including high-stress moments.

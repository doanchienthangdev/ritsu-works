# Card Sorting

*Category: testing · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A technique in which participants sort labeled cards (representing information items, concepts, or ideas) into groups that make sense to them — used in design thinking to validate whether a Problem Statement is 'clearly solvable' and in information architecture to reveal users' mental models of how content should be organized.

**Origin:** Established as a UX research method by Jakob Nielsen and others in the 1990s; adapted into Design Thinking problem-validation practice by the Stanford d.school.

## Why it works
In the Design Thinking context, the Card Sort is used as a rapid validation test for the Problem Statement: give 10 team members the statement on a card and ask them to sort it as 'clearly solvable', 'too broad', or 'too narrow'. A Problem Statement that fewer than 80% of reviewers classify as 'clearly solvable' needs revision before ideation begins.

## When to use
Use at the end of the Define phase, immediately after drafting the Problem Statement. Do not proceed to ideation until the Card Sort passes.

`none`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Write the Problem Statement on 10 identical cards (one per reviewer).
2. 2. Give one card to each of 10 team members (or external reviewers who are familiar with the problem domain but not involved in its creation).
3. 3. Ask each reviewer to read the statement independently and place the card in one of three zones: 'clearly solvable' (I could generate solution ideas for this right now), 'too broad' (I wouldn't know where to start), 'too narrow' (there is only one obvious solution).
4. 4. Count the distribution. Target: ≥80% 'clearly solvable.'
5. 5. If < 80% clearly solvable: 'too broad' responses → split the problem statement into sub-problems and test again; 'too narrow' responses → loosen the constraint in the 'needs' or 'because' clause.
6. 6. Iterate until ≥80% threshold is reached. Typical: 2–3 iterations required.

## Real-life example — General Electric (GE Healthcare)
When GE Healthcare's design team was crafting a Problem Statement for patient experience in radiology departments, their first draft was: 'Radiology patients need to feel less anxious.' The Card Sort result: 30% clearly solvable, 70% too broad. Revised: 'First-time radiology patients (age 60+) need to understand what will happen during their scan, in plain language, before they enter the scanner, because today's written consent forms use medical jargon that creates rather than reduces anxiety — we'll know we've solved this when pre-scan anxiety scores (1–10 scale) drop by at least 3 points.' Card Sort 2: 90% clearly solvable. The redesign of the pre-scan briefing process reduced patient anxiety by an average of 3.8 points.

**So what:** The Card Sort test is a 20-minute investment that prevents weeks of ideation pointed at the wrong problem.

## Pitfalls
- Testing with team members who wrote the statement: they will classify it as clearly solvable because they understand it. Counter: include at least 5 reviewers who did not participate in writing the statement.
- Skipping the Card Sort: teams that skip validation proceed to ideation with a problem statement that is too broad to anchor the session. Counter: the Card Sort is a 20-minute exercise — it is always worth doing.

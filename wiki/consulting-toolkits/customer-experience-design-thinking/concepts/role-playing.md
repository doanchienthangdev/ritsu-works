# Role-Playing (Service Role-Play)

*Category: prototyping · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A live, embodied prototype in which team members physically act out a service interaction — one person playing the customer, another playing the service provider or system — using the storyboard as a script, to identify gaps, awkward moments, and emotional disconnects in the proposed experience before building anything digital.

**Origin:** Adapted from improvisational theater (Viola Spolin, 1963) and the service design discipline; used by IDEO and Engine Service Design as a standard early-stage prototyping tool for service experiences.

## Why it works
Digital prototypes can prototype screen interactions but cannot prototype the human, social, and emotional dimensions of a service experience. Role-playing makes these dimensions concrete — the team immediately feels where the proposed service flow is awkward, unrealistic, or emotionally off-key — in a way that storyboard review cannot.

## When to use
Use in Phase 8 (Prototype) immediately after low-fi sketching and before digital prototyping — especially for service design challenges that involve human-to-human interactions.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Assign roles: one team member as the user (using the persona profile as their character brief), one as the service provider / system, and one as the facilitator (who introduces each storyboard panel and keeps the scene moving).
2. 2. Brief all actors: 'Stay in character. Don't correct the script — if something feels awkward, keep going and note it. The awkwardness is the data.'
3. 3. Run through the storyboard panel by panel, with actors improvising realistic dialogue based on the panel's scenario.
4. 4. Observers take notes on the Feedback Capture Grid: what felt natural? What felt forced? Where did the interaction break down? What was missing?
5. 5. After the full run-through, debrief as a group: 'What moments were most awkward?' 'What did the user actor feel that wasn't in the storyboard?' 'What does the service provider need that the design doesn't give them?'
6. 6. Revise the storyboard or design based on the debrief. Run the role-play again if significant changes are made.

## Real-life example — Bank of America / IDEO
When IDEO designed the 'Keep the Change' savings feature for Bank of America, the team ran a role-play of the branch conversation where a banker explains the feature to a customer for the first time. In the role-play, the team member playing the customer immediately asked: 'So wait — does the round-up happen for every purchase? What if I don't want it to?' The storyboard had not addressed this question. The role-play revealed a critical gap: the feature needed an opt-out mechanism and a way to show customers their accumulated savings in real time. Both features were added to the design before any digital prototype was built.

**So what:** Role-playing reveals the questions users will ask that the design team forgot to answer.

## Pitfalls
- Breaking character to 'fix' the script: actors who step out of character to explain 'that's not how it would really work' invalidate the prototype. Counter: the facilitator enforces staying in character — 'Write it down and keep going.'
- Role-playing only the happy path: teams run the ideal scenario and skip edge cases. Counter: run the role-play twice: once for the ideal scenario, once for the most common failure scenario.

# CX Initiative Business Case

*Category: financial · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A structured one-page financial and strategic case for a specific CX initiative that quantifies expected impact on Outcome KPIs, estimates investment required, and calculates a break-even timeline — giving finance and the executive team the information needed to approve or prioritize the initiative.

**Origin:** Standard management consulting deliverable (McKinsey, Bain, BCG business case format); adapted for CX initiatives by using customer-centric impact metrics (NPS uplift, conversion improvement, cost-to-serve reduction) rather than purely financial metrics.

## Why it works
CX investments that cannot be connected to a financial return will always lose budget debates to initiatives with clearer ROI. The CX Business Case forces the team to quantify impact — even roughly — and to make explicit the assumptions behind the estimate. A business case with transparent assumptions is more credible than a precise calculation with hidden assumptions.

## When to use
Use in Phase 4 (Initiatives & Roadmap) for all initiatives advancing from the screening process to the prioritization matrix. One business case per initiative.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Write the Problem Statement (1 sentence): which Moment of Truth does this initiative address, and what is the current performance on the relevant KPI?
2. 2. Describe the Proposed Solution (2 sentences): what will be built or changed, and how will it address the problem?
3. 3. Estimate Expected Impact: use McKinsey CX benchmarks as reference ranges — 10–20% CSAT improvement, 10–15% conversion uplift, 20–50% cost-to-serve reduction. Apply the range to the current baseline to calculate absolute improvement (e.g., NPS +8 to +12 points).
4. 4. Estimate Investment: headcount (FTEs × months × blended rate), technology (license + implementation), and change management (training + communications).
5. 5. Calculate Financial Return: convert the expected impact into revenue or cost terms (e.g., +8 NPS points × historical relationship between NPS and CLV = estimated revenue uplift).
6. 6. Calculate break-even timeline: investment / monthly benefit = months to break even.
7. 7. Document the Top 3 Assumptions: the critical assumptions that, if wrong, would significantly change the business case. Include a sensitivity analysis for the most uncertain assumption.

## Real-life example — Comcast
Comcast's CX team built a business case for a proactive service outage notification system. Problem: 40% of customer support contacts during outages were 'I didn't know there was an outage — is my service working?' Proposed Solution: real-time SMS/push notification when an outage affecting the customer's area is detected. Expected Impact: 35% reduction in outage-related contacts (industry benchmark from AT&T's similar implementation). Investment: $2.4M engineering + $800K annual support tool cost. Financial Return: 35% × 8M annual outage contacts × $7 average call cost = $19.6M annual savings. Break-even: 16 months. The business case was approved, and Comcast's 'My Account' app outage notification feature launched in 2018.

**So what:** A well-constructed CX business case converts 'this will make customers happier' into 'this will generate $19.6M in savings' — a fundamentally different conversation with the CFO.

## Pitfalls
- Single-point estimates: business cases with no scenario analysis create false precision. Counter: always provide conservative/base/stretch scenarios for the Expected Impact.
- Forgetting change management costs: CX initiatives that change frontline behavior require training and communications investment that teams consistently underestimate. Counter: add 15–25% of the technology cost as a change management allowance.

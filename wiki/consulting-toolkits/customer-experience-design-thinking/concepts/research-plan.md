# Research Plan

*Category: research · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A structured planning document that maps each design research question to a specific research method, sample size, recruiting criteria, guide/protocol, and expected output — ensuring the research investment is focused, sufficient, and methodologically rigorous.

**Origin:** Standard UX research methodology; formalized by the IDEO HCD (Human-Centered Design) toolkit (2009) and the Nielsen Norman Group research planning framework.

## Why it works
Without a plan, research teams default to the methods they find most comfortable (usually interviews) regardless of whether those methods are best suited to the research questions. The Research Plan forces explicit matching of method to question — some questions need observation (DILO), some need self-report (diary study), some need naturalistic behavior (contextual inquiry).

## When to use
Use at the start of the Empathize phase, immediately after the Project Canvas is approved. The Research Plan is the project plan for the research phase.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. List the 3–5 core research questions that must be answered before the team can define the problem. Start with 'What don't we know that we must know?'
2. 2. For each question, select the best-fit method: Why questions → interviews; How questions → DILO/observation; How often questions → diary study; What do they do in context → contextual inquiry.
3. 3. Determine sample size per method: interviews need 8–12; DILO needs 3–5 full-day sessions; diary studies need 10–15 participants over 7–14 days.
4. 4. Write recruiting criteria: specific attributes (role, experience level, frequency of the relevant behavior) that qualify a participant.
5. 5. Assign a lead researcher and timeline for each method.
6. 6. Get the research plan reviewed by the Design Thinking Lead and Sponsor before recruiting begins.

## Real-life example — IDEO / Shimano (cycling)
When IDEO redesigned the cycling experience for Shimano's casual cyclist market, the research plan included: (1) 12 user interviews (Why don't casual adults cycle?); (2) 5 DILO sessions with existing casual cyclists (How do they experience the cycling journey?); (3) a 7-day diary study with 10 participants (When and why do they decide to cycle or not?). The multi-method plan revealed that the barrier was not gear complexity (what focus groups had suggested) but fear of traffic and embarrassment about not knowing traffic laws — an insight that DILO alone would have missed.

**So what:** A multi-method research plan triangulates findings and catches the insights that any single method would miss.

## Pitfalls
- One method for all questions: using interviews for everything regardless of the research question type. Counter: mandate at least 2 different research methods per research plan to triangulate findings.
- Recruiting for convenience rather than fit: using whoever is available (internal staff, willing contacts) rather than actual target users. Counter: the recruiting criteria must be written before any recruiting begins.

# Storyboarding

*Category: prototyping · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A visual narrative technique borrowed from film production that depicts a user's journey through a proposed solution as a sequence of 6–10 hand-drawn panels — each showing a key moment in the interaction, with a caption — making abstract solution concepts concrete and testable before any digital prototyping begins.

**Origin:** Invented at Walt Disney Studios in the 1930s for film pre-production; adopted into interaction design and service design practice by IDEO and Google Ventures. A standard output of the Ideate-to-Prototype transition in the Stanford d.school Design Thinking model.

## Why it works
A storyboard forces the design team to think through the full user experience of a proposed solution — not just the central interaction, but the context that precedes it (why is the user doing this?), the key moment (what does the solution actually do?), and the resolution (what is the user able to do now that they couldn't before?). This narrative structure reveals gaps in solution logic that abstract descriptions miss, and creates a tangible artifact for user testing without requiring any code.

## When to use
Use at the start of the Prototype phase, before any digital prototyping begins, to validate the solution concept at its coarsest level. Also use to communicate solution concepts to stakeholders who are not present in the design process.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Start from the Idea Evaluation Canvas for the concept you are storyboarding: know the target user, the key assumption, and the expected benefit before you draw a single panel.
2. 2. Write the 8-panel sequence in words first (one sentence per panel) before drawing: Panel 1: setup (user's current context and frustration); Panels 2–7: the core interaction (the solution in use); Panel 8: resolution (what the user can now do or feel).
3. 3. Draw the panels in sequence using simple visuals: stick figures for people, rectangles for screens or objects, speech bubbles for dialogue, arrows for actions. Do not spend more than 5 minutes per panel — this is a communication tool, not art.
4. 4. Add a caption below each panel (1–2 sentences) that describes what is happening and what the user is thinking or feeling at that moment.
5. 5. Review the storyboard with the full team: 'Does each panel follow logically from the previous one? Is there a missing step? Are we skipping over the hardest part of the interaction (the 'magic happens here' panel)?'
6. 6. Use the storyboard as the script for a Role-Play session: one team member plays the user, another plays the service/system, and they act through each panel.
7. 7. Present the storyboard to 3–5 users in a rapid concept test: ask 'Walk me through what you think is happening here' for each panel. Where users misunderstand a panel, the design needs more clarity.

## Real-life example — Google
Google's design team used storyboarding extensively in the early development of Google Glass. The storyboard for the 'hands-free navigation' use case showed: (1) User in a city, lost; (2) User raises Glass with voice command; (3) Turn-by-turn directions appear in the field of view; (4) User walks without looking at phone; (5) User arrives at destination; (6) User completes other tasks with free hands. The storyboard revealed a critical gap in Panel 3: the design team had drawn arrows overlaid on the real world, but in testing, users couldn't understand the arrows without a scale reference. This led to the design of the 'minimap' overlay — a design decision traceable directly to a storyboard review session.

**So what:** The panel where the story 'jumps' — where the logic is not obvious — is the design problem you haven't solved yet.

## Template
Write one sentence per panel before drawing. Draw simple visuals. Add captions. Do not spend more than 30 minutes on the full storyboard.

- [ ] Panel 1 (Setup — user's current frustration): Caption: ___ | Sketch: [describe]
- [ ] Panel 2 (Trigger — user encounters or seeks the solution): Caption: ___ | Sketch: ___
- [ ] Panel 3 (Core interaction step 1): Caption: ___ | Sketch: ___

- [ ] User test: Misunderstood panels: ___

## Pitfalls
- 8-panel storyboard of 2 panels: teams often produce 2–3 panels that describe the core interaction but skip the setup and resolution. Counter: enforce the structure: Panel 1 must show the user's current frustration; Panel 8 must show the resolution after using the solution.
- The 'magic happens here' panel: teams storyboard around the hard part of the interaction by replacing it with a panel that just says 'the solution does X.' Counter: explicitly identify the most technically or logistically complex step and require it to have its own panel.
- High-fidelity drawings: team members who can draw well spend 2 hours on a storyboard that should take 30 minutes. Counter: explicitly say at the start 'Anyone who can draw a stick figure can do this. Artistic quality is irrelevant.'

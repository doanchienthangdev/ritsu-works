# Mind Mapping

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A radial visual ideation tool that starts with a central concept (the problem statement or HMW question) and expands outward through associative branching — mapping sub-problems, related concepts, potential solutions, and analogies in a non-linear structure that mirrors associative thinking.

**Origin:** Popularized by Tony Buzan in 'Use Your Head' (1974); adopted into design thinking and consulting ideation practice as a complement to linear brainstorming.

## Why it works
Linear brainstorming can miss connections between domains. Mind mapping creates a spatial representation of idea territory, revealing clusters and relationships between sub-problems that list-based ideation misses. It also enables individual and group work simultaneously — one person can build a map alone, or a group can co-create a large-format digital map.

## When to use
Use in Phase 7 (Ideate) as a complement to linear brainstorming — particularly useful for complex, multi-dimensional problems where the relationships between sub-problems are as important as the sub-problems themselves.

## Visual
`tree`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Write the HMW question or Problem Statement in the center of a large canvas or Miro board.
2. 2. Draw 4–6 first-level branches representing the major dimensions of the problem (e.g., for a checkout experience: 'information', 'trust', 'speed', 'recovery').
3. 3. For each first-level branch, add second-level sub-branches representing specific aspects of that dimension or solution directions.
4. 4. For each second-level branch, add third-level specific ideas (potential features, behaviors, or interventions).
5. 5. After the map is built (20–30 minutes), identify the areas of the map with the most branching activity — these are the richest solution spaces.
6. 6. Identify cross-branch connections: draw links between nodes in different branches that are related. These connections often reveal the most innovative ideas.

## Real-life example — Apple
Steve Jobs reportedly used a form of mind mapping in early product planning sessions, mapping the central concept ('a computer in your pocket') outward to connectivity, entertainment, communication, and computing branches. Each branch independently generated dozens of ideas that, at their intersections, produced the iPhone's core feature set (touchscreen from the accessibility branch × music player from entertainment × internet browser from connectivity).

**So what:** The connections between branches of a mind map often produce the most valuable ideas.

## Template
Build the map from the center outward. Add branches rapidly; refine after the initial generation is complete. Identify cross-branch connections before moving to evaluation.

- [ ] Central concept: ___
- [ ] Branch 1: ___ | Second-level: ___ | Third-level ideas: ___
- [ ] Branch 2: ___ | Second-level: ___ | Third-level ideas: ___
- [ ] Branch 3: ___ | [repeat]
- [ ] Branch 4: ___ | [repeat]
- [ ] Cross-branch connections identified: ___
- [ ] Richest solution areas (most branching): ___

## Pitfalls
- Maps with only 2 levels of branching: stopping at sub-problems without generating specific ideas produces a problem map, not an ideation map. Counter: require at least 3 levels of branching before the exercise ends.
- Building the map as a solo activity: mind mapping generates its greatest value through unexpected connections that different team members see differently. Counter: run mind mapping as a group activity with a shared digital canvas.

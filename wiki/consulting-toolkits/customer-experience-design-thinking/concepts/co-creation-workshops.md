# Co-Creation Workshops

*Category: prototyping · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
Structured collaborative sessions in which 6–12 real users participate alongside the design team to co-design specific elements of the proposed solution — making users active contributors to the design process rather than passive research subjects.

**Origin:** Rooted in participatory design (Scandinavian tradition, 1970s–80s); formalized as a service design methodology by Engine, IDEO, and academic practitioners (Elizabeth Sanders, 1999).

## Why it works
Users possess contextual expertise about their own experience that designers cannot fully acquire through observation or interviewing alone. Co-creation workshops leverage this expertise by inviting users to directly generate and evaluate design alternatives — producing solutions that are simultaneously better (more contextually accurate) and more likely to be adopted (users are invested in their own creation).

## When to use
Use in Phase 8 (Prototype) for design decisions where user mental models and contextual expertise are most likely to produce better solutions than team-led design. Especially valuable for healthcare, financial services, and enterprise software design.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the design decisions where user input is most valuable: typically, the 3–5 elements where the design team has highest uncertainty or where user mental models are most likely to differ from team assumptions.
2. 2. Recruit 6–12 users who represent the primary persona. Brief them: 'You are the experts on your own experience. We need your help designing something that actually works for you.'
3. 3. Prepare workshop materials: printed hi-fi screens of the current prototype, sticky notes, markers, and blank design templates for each co-design activity.
4. 4. Structure the session: (1) 15-min intro and context-setting; (2) 20-min user evaluation of the existing prototype (what works? what doesn't?); (3) 45-min co-design activity (users redesign specific screens using printed components + sticky notes); (4) 15-min gallery walk and group discussion of co-designed outputs; (5) 15-min debrief.
5. 5. During the co-design activity, observe without directing: let users make design decisions, even when they make choices the team disagrees with. Note the reasoning behind the choices.
6. 6. After the workshop, synthesize the co-designed outputs and identify the 3–5 design changes most consistently requested or generated across participants.

## Real-life example — NHS (UK National Health Service)
The NHS Design team ran co-creation workshops with 8 cancer patients to redesign the treatment appointment reminder system. Patients were given printed mockups of the current letter (dense text, medical jargon) and asked to redesign it using scissors, sticky notes, and printed components. Every participant independently moved the appointment date and time to the top of the letter (it was buried in paragraph 3), added a map, and replaced the medical department name with 'Where to go.' These three changes — generated in a co-creation workshop, not by designers — were implemented unchanged and reduced missed appointments by 31%.

**So what:** Users know what they need better than any designer. Co-creation workshops give them the tools to show you.

## Pitfalls
- Recruiting users who are experts in design: co-creation workshops should include typical users, not design-literate users who will produce technically sophisticated but unrepresentative outputs. Counter: screen for design expertise in the recruiting screener and exclude it.
- Directing users during the co-design activity: design team members who suggest designs during the co-creation activity are invalidating the method. Counter: design team members observe and take notes during co-design activities; they speak only during the debrief.

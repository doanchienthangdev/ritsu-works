# NABC (Need, Approach, Benefit, Competition)

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Customer Experience Strategy & Design Thinking*

## What it is
A structured idea evaluation framework that scores solution concepts against four criteria — Need (does it solve the validated problem?), Approach (is it technically and organizationally feasible?), Benefit (does it deliver the expected KPI impact?), Competition (is it meaningfully better than alternatives?) — to distinguish the best ideas from the merely interesting ones.

**Origin:** Developed at SRI International (formerly Stanford Research Institute) by Gorenberg & Curtis in the 1990s as a business communication and pitch evaluation tool; adopted into design thinking practice as an idea evaluation method.

## Why it works
Divergent ideation produces many ideas of wildly varying quality. Without a structured evaluation method, the most confident person or the most senior person in the room determines which ideas proceed — a recipe for selection bias. NABC provides a democratic, criteria-based scoring method that separates the quality of an idea from the status of its advocate.

## When to use
Use in the Ideate phase as the second-round evaluation method (after dot voting) to select ideas for prototyping. Use whenever a team has more ideas than it can prototype and needs a structured way to make the selection decision.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. After the ideation session, select the 10–15 most interesting ideas (via dot voting — 5 votes per person, ideas with ≥3 votes advance).
2. 2. For each surviving idea, prepare a 2-minute verbal pitch: problem it addresses, proposed solution, and expected impact.
3. 3. Assemble an evaluation panel of 4–6 people: the Design Thinking Lead, the Sponsor, a technical expert, and a user (or user proxy from research).
4. 4. For each idea, score it independently on each NABC criterion (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent) before discussing. Collect scores silently (on paper or digital forms).
5. 5. N — Need: Does this idea address the validated root cause in the Problem Statement, and does it solve it for the primary persona? If no, score ≤2.
6. 6. A — Approach: Is the implementation technically feasible within the project constraints (budget, timeline, regulatory)? Score the confidence that the team can actually build it.
7. 7. B — Benefit: How confident are you that this idea will achieve the targeted KPI improvement (e.g., +10 NPS points, -20% cost-to-serve)? Use evidence from analogous solutions for calibration.
8. 8. C — Competition: Compared to the current solution and known alternatives, how meaningfully better is this idea? 'Better' means users would noticeably prefer it, not just that it has more features.
9. 9. Average the panel's scores for each criterion. Ideas scoring ≥16/20 proceed to prototyping; 12–15: discuss; <12: park.
10. 10. Complete the Idea Evaluation Canvas for the top 3–5 selected ideas.

## Real-life example — Google Ventures
The Google Ventures Design Sprint (a formalized version of design thinking compressed into 5 days) uses a version of NABC in its 'Decide' phase. During a sprint for a healthcare startup redesigning its patient intake process, 22 ideas were generated on Monday. By Friday's evaluation, NABC scoring surfaced a surprise winner: a simple paper-based check-in form redesign scored 18/20 (N:5, A:5, B:4, C:4), beating a digital kiosk (14/20) and an app-based solution (12/20). The winning idea was 3x more feasible and addressed the user's primary anxiety (complex forms) more directly. Without NABC, the digital solution would have been selected based on team enthusiasm for technology.

**So what:** NABC prevents the team's enthusiasm for sophisticated solutions from overriding the evidence for simple ones.

## Pitfalls
- Scoring as a group before individual scoring: anchoring bias causes the first person to speak to dominate the evaluation. Counter: require all panel members to score silently and independently before any discussion.
- Ignoring the Approach criterion: teams with high enthusiasm for an idea consistently overscore Approach. Counter: the technical expert on the panel must provide evidence for their Approach score — not an opinion.
- Over-weighting Benefit at the expense of Need: an idea can deliver impressive results for the wrong problem. Counter: any idea scoring ≤2 on Need is automatically disqualified, regardless of its Benefit score.

# Three Horizons of Growth

*Category: growth · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A portfolio-of-initiatives framework that organizes investment across three time horizons: defending and extending the current core (H1), building emerging businesses (H2), and creating genuinely new options (H3).

**Origin:** Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley, and David White, McKinsey & Company, published in 'The Alchemy of Growth' (1999).

## Why it works
Companies that only invest in H1 (optimizing the current business) stagnate and eventually decline. Companies that only invest in H3 (moonshots) burn cash without near-term returns. Sustainable growth requires simultaneous investment across all three horizons — the H1 cash flows fund H2 growth, which funds H3 exploration. The three horizons must be managed differently: H1 by improving execution, H2 by building scale, H3 by experimenting rapidly and cheaply.

## When to use
Long-range strategic planning; investment allocation between core and growth; innovation portfolio governance; preparing a growth strategy for a new CEO or board.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Classify all current and planned strategic initiatives into one of the three horizons based on time-to-significant-cash-flow: H1 (<2 years), H2 (2–5 years), H3 (5+ years).
2. Apply the '70-20-10 investment rule' as a starting point: 70% of investment in H1, 20% in H2, 10% in H3. Adjust based on the maturity of your core business — if H1 is structurally declining, increase H2/H3 investment.
3. Assess the pipeline health: do you have enough H2 initiatives that will become H1 businesses in 2–5 years? Do you have enough H3 experiments that could become H2 businesses in 5+ years?
4. Establish separate governance for each horizon: H1 uses operational review cadences (monthly); H2 uses strategic review cadences (quarterly); H3 uses venture-style stage-gate reviews.
5. Set different success metrics for each horizon: H1 metrics are financial (EBIT, ROIC); H2 metrics are commercial traction (revenue growth, customer acquisition); H3 metrics are learning (hypotheses tested, pivot rate, optionality created).

## Real-life example — Amazon Web Services
In 2003, AWS was an H3 experiment — an internal technology project exploring whether Amazon could rent computing infrastructure to external developers. It consumed resources with no clear near-term revenue. By 2006 (public launch), it was H2 — growing rapidly but requiring heavy investment in data centers with long payback periods. By 2012, AWS was H1 — generating consistent, high-margin revenue that funded Amazon's next H3 experiments (Alexa/Echo, Amazon Go). The three-horizons lens explains why Amazon was willing to invest in AWS for 3 years before it had commercial revenue — it was managed as an H3 bet, not evaluated on H1 financial metrics.

**So what:** The critical discipline in Three Horizons is resisting the urge to apply H1 financial metrics to H3 initiatives — doing so kills every genuine innovation before it can mature.

## Pitfalls
- Under-investing in H2 and H3 to maximize near-term H1 performance — the most common corporate failure mode; it produces great 3-year results and a growth cliff at year 4.
- Applying the same governance and financial metrics to all three horizons: H3 ventures killed by H1 IRR hurdle rates is a structural innovation failure.
- Treating H3 as purely speculative: the best H3 initiatives have a clear hypothesis about how they will create value and a fast experimentation cadence to test it.

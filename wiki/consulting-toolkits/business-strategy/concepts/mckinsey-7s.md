# McKinsey 7S Framework

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
An organizational analysis framework that assesses the alignment of seven interdependent internal elements required for effective strategy execution: Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Style, Staff, and Skills.

**Origin:** Tom Peters and Robert Waterman, McKinsey & Company, 1980; published in 'In Search of Excellence' (1982). Built to explain why structural reorganizations alone fail to improve performance.

## Why it works
Organizations fail at strategy execution not because the strategy is wrong but because the seven organizational elements are misaligned. The framework's power is in its web structure: every element affects every other. A new strategy (hard S) that is incompatible with the current Shared Values (soft S) will be silently rejected by the organization. Alignment analysis prevents this.

## When to use
Before implementing a new strategy (to identify organizational blockers); during post-merger integration; when execution has consistently fallen short of strategic intentions.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Map each of the seven elements for the current organization: Strategy (what is the current plan?), Structure (how is the org organized?), Systems (what processes and IT govern work?), Shared Values (what is the culture / what behaviors are rewarded?), Style (how does leadership manage?), Staff (who is here, with what capabilities?), Skills (what is the organization good at?).
2. For each element, write a 'required state' — what the element needs to look like for the new strategy to execute successfully.
3. For each element, identify the gap between current and required state. Classify each gap as a major misalignment (will block strategy), moderate misalignment (will slow strategy), or minor (manageable).
4. Identify the most critical interdependencies: which misalignments compound each other? (e.g., wrong Structure → wrong Systems → wrong Skills cascade).
5. Build an organizational change roadmap that addresses major misalignments in priority order, with owners and timelines.

## Real-life example — IBM (2000s transformation)
When IBM shifted strategy from hardware products to services and software under Lou Gerstner and then Sam Palmisano, the 7S analysis revealed massive misalignments: the Structure (product divisions) did not support integrated solutions selling; Systems (incentives rewarded hardware revenue, not solutions revenue); Shared Values (engineers valued products, not client service); Skills (workforce lacked consulting capabilities). IBM's transformation program had to address all seven elements simultaneously — restructuring into industry-facing service units, retraining 100,000+ employees, and rewriting the incentive system.

**So what:** IBM's transformation took a decade precisely because every element of the 7S web required realignment. Gerstner's insight was that changing Strategy without changing Systems, Skills, and Shared Values would produce nothing.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the 7S as a static snapshot: alignment erodes over time as the environment changes; the analysis should be refreshed annually or after major strategic shifts.
- Focusing only on the hard Ss (Strategy, Structure, Systems) which are easier to see and change, while ignoring the soft Ss (Style, Shared Values) which are harder to change and more likely to derail execution.
- Running 7S without linking it to action: the output must be an organizational change roadmap, not a descriptive report.

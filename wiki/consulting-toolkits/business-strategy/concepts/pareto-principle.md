# Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A heuristic principle holding that roughly 80% of outcomes come from 20% of causes, used in strategy to focus analytical and operational effort on the vital few drivers.

**Origin:** Vilfredo Pareto, Italian economist, 1896 — observed that 80% of Italy's land was owned by 20% of the population. Joseph Juran coined the '80/20 rule' term in quality management in the 1940s and applied it to industrial defects.

## Why it works
In any complex system, inputs and outputs are not uniformly distributed — a small subset of factors drives a disproportionate share of results. By identifying the vital 20%, decision-makers can concentrate scarce resources (time, capital, management attention) where they generate maximum impact rather than spreading them evenly.

## When to use
Resource allocation; cost reduction; quality improvement; customer profitability analysis; IT troubleshooting; any situation where scarce management attention must be concentrated.

## Visual
`chart`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the outcome you want to explain (revenue, profit, defects, support tickets, etc.).
2. List all categories contributing to that outcome (products, customers, SKUs, cost centers, failure modes).
3. Sort categories in descending order by their contribution to the total outcome.
4. Calculate cumulative percentage and draw the Pareto chart. Identify the point where the cumulative line crosses 80% — that marks the 'vital 20%'.
5. Focus all deep analytical effort and operational intervention on the vital 20% identified. Confirm that these items receive disproportionate management attention and resource allocation.
6. Revisit the analysis quarterly: the Pareto distribution shifts over time as products, customers, and markets evolve.

## Real-life example — Amazon Seller Services
Amazon's marketplace team applied the Pareto principle to seller support: analysis showed that 18% of seller account types generated 81% of total support escalations. Rather than building a uniform support improvement program, the team built a dedicated specialist team for those 18% of account types, reducing escalation volume by 67% while improving the resolution rate for the remaining 82% of less-complex tickets through automation. The Pareto lens prevented the mistake of 'fix everything equally'.

**So what:** The Pareto principle is most powerful as an attention-allocation tool: it tells you where NOT to focus before telling you where to focus.

## Pitfalls
- Treating 80/20 as a precise law rather than an empirical tendency: the actual split may be 75/25 or 90/10; the pattern matters, not the exact numbers.
- Ignoring the 'trivial many': in safety-critical contexts, the 80% of minor issues can compound into major failures — Pareto focus must be context-appropriate.
- One-time analysis: the vital 20% shifts as markets evolve; static Pareto analysis becomes misleading over time.

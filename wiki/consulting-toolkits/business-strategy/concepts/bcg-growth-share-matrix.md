# BCG Growth-Share Matrix

*Category: portfolio · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A 2×2 portfolio management matrix that classifies business units or products by relative market share (competitive strength) and market growth rate (industry attractiveness) to guide investment decisions.

**Origin:** Bruce Henderson, Boston Consulting Group, 1968. Built on the empirical observation that market leadership confers sustainable cost advantage via the experience curve.

## Why it works
Cash generation and cash consumption patterns differ systematically by quadrant: Cash Cows (high share, low growth) generate surplus cash; Stars (high share, high growth) consume cash to maintain position but will become Cash Cows; Question Marks (low share, high growth) require investment to potentially become Stars; Dogs (low share, low growth) destroy value. A balanced portfolio has Cash Cows funding Stars and selective Question Marks.

## When to use
Portfolio review for multi-BU companies; resource allocation discussions; M&A target screening; annual strategy refresh.

## Visual
`matrix-2x2`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define 'relative market share' as your BU's market share divided by the largest competitor's market share (not your absolute share). This is the correct BCG x-axis metric.
2. Define 'market growth rate' as the relevant market's annual volume or revenue growth rate over the past 3 years. Use a threshold (commonly 10%) to distinguish high-growth from low-growth markets.
3. Plot each BU or major product as a bubble on the 2×2, with bubble size proportional to revenue or profit.
4. Classify each BU into the four quadrants and assign a recommended investment stance: Stars → invest to maintain/grow share; Cash Cows → extract cash, minimal incremental investment; Question Marks → make a binary call (invest to become a Star, or exit); Dogs → divest or harvest remaining value.
5. Stress-test the portfolio balance: an all-Stars portfolio is unsustainable (cash-consumptive); an all-Cash-Cows portfolio is stagnating. Aim for a portfolio with 1–2 Stars funded by 2–3 Cash Cows and deliberate Question Mark bets.
6. Triangulate with the GE-McKinsey 9-box matrix for any BU where the BCG verdict is ambiguous — the two frameworks measure slightly different things.

## Real-life example — Procter & Gamble
P&G's 2012–2014 portfolio rationalization was driven in part by BCG-style analysis. CEO A.G. Lafley identified ~70 brands that were Dogs or low-ROI Question Marks (e.g., Pringles, which had low relative share in snacks, a non-core category). P&G divested 100+ brands between 2012 and 2016, retaining only the 65–70 brands where it had high relative market share (Cash Cows and Stars: Tide, Pampers, Gillette). Result: focused portfolio, higher ROIC, and shareholder returns that outperformed the consumer goods sector.

**So what:** BCG matrix discipline turned P&G's 'bigger is better' portfolio logic on its head — relative market share, not revenue size, determined which brands deserved investment.

## Pitfalls
- Using absolute market share instead of relative market share — the correct x-axis is your share divided by the leader's share, not your percentage of the total market.
- Treating the matrix as a mandate: a Dog in a stable niche can be highly profitable; the matrix informs the question, it doesn't answer it.
- Ignoring competitive dynamics: a Question Mark with a credible path to Star status may justify investment even if the matrix says 'exit'.

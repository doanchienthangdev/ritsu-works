# Product Life Cycle

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A model that describes the stages a product or business moves through from market introduction to decline, each with characteristic growth rates, competitive intensity, and optimal strategic responses.

**Origin:** Theodore Levitt, 'Exploit the Product Life Cycle', Harvard Business Review, 1965. Grounded in the S-curve theory of diffusion by Everett Rogers (1962).

## Why it works
Products follow predictable patterns of adoption and maturity. Each stage demands a different strategy: Introduction requires market creation investment; Growth requires scale investment to capture share before the market matures; Maturity requires efficiency and differentiation to defend margins; Decline requires cash extraction or strategic exit. Mismatching strategy to stage destroys value.

## When to use
Portfolio planning; investment allocation decisions; responding to sales slowdowns; new product launch timing; competitive positioning.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Identify where each of your major products/BUs currently sits on the curve: Introduction (market awareness <20%, rapid sales growth still ahead), Growth (sales growing >15–20% p.a., market share battles intensifying), Maturity (sales growth <5%, price competition dominant), or Decline (sales falling).
2. For each stage, define the appropriate strategic response: Introduction → invest in market education and distribution; Growth → invest aggressively in capacity and brand to build share before maturity; Maturity → focus on cost efficiency, product differentiation, and customer retention; Decline → decide between managed harvest, niche refocus, or exit.
3. Identify the key inflection points: when is the product likely to move from Growth to Maturity? What signals will tell you (price erosion, new competitor entry, slowing category growth)? Set KPI tripwires.
4. Apply the PLC analysis across the portfolio: ensure you have products in Introduction/Growth stages to replace those moving to Maturity/Decline.
5. Cross-reference with the BCG matrix: PLC stage should inform the BCG classification — a product in the Growth stage of its PLC is a candidate Star even if current market share is low.

## Real-life example — Nintendo Switch
Nintendo's 2017 launch of the Switch followed a classic Introduction → Growth → Maturity arc. In Introduction (2017), Nintendo invested heavily in marketing and developer partnerships to build the game library (ecosystem investment). In Growth (2018–2021), global supply could not meet demand; Nintendo invested in manufacturing scale. By 2022, signs of Maturity appeared: unit growth slowed, Nintendo began releasing price-reduced 'Lite' variants and bundling to sustain volume. The strategy explicitly shifted from market-building to margin defense and installed-base monetization.

**So what:** Nintendo's success was in recognizing the Growth-to-Maturity transition and proactively shifting strategy before sales momentum visibly stalled — a common failure mode for companies that miss the inflection.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the PLC as deterministic — companies can extend the maturity stage through innovation (e.g., Apple revitalizing the Mac repeatedly). The PLC is a tendency, not a destiny.
- Not monitoring the pipeline: if all products are in Maturity/Decline, the company faces a growth cliff — Introduction/Growth products must be actively developed.
- Applying maturity-stage strategies to a growth-stage product (under-investing to preserve margins when the market is still building) destroys long-term share.

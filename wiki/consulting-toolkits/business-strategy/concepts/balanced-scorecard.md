# Balanced Scorecard

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A strategic performance management framework that translates strategy into a set of objectives, KPIs, targets, and initiatives organized across four perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Process, and Learning & Growth.

**Origin:** Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton, Harvard Business School, 1992 — 'The Balanced Scorecard: Measures That Drive Performance', Harvard Business Review. Extended to a strategy management system in 'The Strategy-Focused Organization' (2001).

## Why it works
Financial measures alone are lag indicators that tell you what happened, not why or what to do. The Balanced Scorecard adds three non-financial perspectives that are lead indicators: customer outcomes drive financial outcomes; internal process excellence drives customer outcomes; employee learning and growth drives process excellence. The causal chain connects operational levers to financial results.

## When to use
Annual strategy deployment; making strategy measurable and actionable; aligning the organization around strategic priorities; management reporting reform.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Build the Strategy Map first: draw the causal chain of strategic objectives across the four perspectives. Each objective in one perspective should causally link to an objective in the perspective above it.
2. For each strategic objective on the map, define 1–2 KPIs that genuinely measure whether the objective is being achieved. Avoid vanity metrics (inputs masquerading as outcomes).
3. Set baselines (current performance) and targets (end-of-year or end-of-period goals) for each KPI. Targets should be ambitious but achievable.
4. Assign a data owner and data source for each KPI. If the data does not currently exist, building the measurement system is itself a strategic initiative.
5. Build the scorecard table: perspectives as rows, then objectives, KPIs, baseline, target, and actual for each review period.
6. Run a balanced scorecard review monthly: update actuals, identify Red and Amber items, determine root causes, and agree corrective actions.

## Real-life example — Mobil North America Marketing & Refining (1990s)
Mobil NAMR used the Balanced Scorecard under Brian Baker to transform from the least profitable to the most profitable division in Mobil within 3 years. The scorecard revealed that Financial KPIs (volume growth, ROCE) were not moving despite operational improvements. Drilling into the Learning & Growth perspective, the analysis found that dealer incentive programs were rewarding volume, not profitability (a Customer/Internal Process KPI misalignment). Realigning the dealer scorecard to reward non-gasoline revenue (car washes, convenience, food) changed dealer behavior, drove 20% increase in non-gasoline revenue, and improved ROCE by 8 percentage points.

**So what:** The Balanced Scorecard is most powerful when it reveals a misalignment between what you are measuring (and thus incentivizing) and what drives strategic outcomes — Mobil's insight was about incentive architecture, not operational capability.

## Pitfalls
- Creating too many KPIs: a scorecard with 40+ KPIs is not a scorecard, it is a spreadsheet. The discipline is in limitation — 20–25 KPIs for a full organization scorecard.
- Failing to build the Strategy Map first: without the causal chain, the scorecard becomes a disconnected list of metrics rather than a strategic management tool.
- Treating the scorecard as a reporting tool rather than a management tool: it must be reviewed in a leadership forum with authority to make decisions, not just updated in a spreadsheet.

# GE-McKinsey 9-Box Matrix

*Category: portfolio · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A 3×3 portfolio management matrix that assesses business units on two composite dimensions: industry attractiveness (3 levels) and competitive strength (3 levels), providing a more nuanced investment guide than the BCG 2×2.

**Origin:** Developed jointly by General Electric and McKinsey & Company in the early 1970s as GE sought a more sophisticated tool to manage its 150+ business units. Published more widely through academic adoption in the late 1970s.

## Why it works
BCG uses only two single-variable proxies; GE-McKinsey uses composite scores on each axis, incorporating multiple weighted criteria. This richer assessment reduces the risk of misclassifying a business that scores well on one dimension but looks different on the other criteria that actually drive long-term value.

## When to use
Multi-BU portfolio resource allocation; strategic planning retreats; M&A target evaluation; annual business review.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the industry attractiveness criteria and their weights — typical criteria: market size, market growth rate, industry profitability, competitive intensity (Porter's Five Forces score), technological requirements, environmental / regulatory factors. Each criterion gets a weight that sums to 100%.
2. Rate each BU's industry on each attractiveness criterion (1=Low, 3=Medium, 5=High). Calculate weighted score. Place on the 3-level y-axis (1.0–2.33 = Low, 2.34–3.67 = Medium, 3.68–5.0 = High).
3. Define the competitive strength criteria and their weights — typical criteria: relative market share, brand strength, production capacity, profit margins, technological capability, management quality. Sum of weights = 100%.
4. Rate each BU on each competitive strength criterion. Calculate weighted score. Place on the 3-level x-axis using the same scaling.
5. Plot each BU as a bubble (proportional to revenue) and identify the investment zone (Invest/Grow, Selective, Harvest/Divest).
6. For BUs on zone boundaries, hold a structured dialogue: what would need to be true for this BU to move to the Invest zone? Is that achievable and worth the investment?

## Real-life example — General Electric (1970s)
GE used the matrix to rationalize its portfolio of 150+ business units under CEO Reginald Jones and later Jack Welch. Welch's famous 'be #1 or #2 in every market or we'll fix, sell, or close it' strategy was operationalized via GE-McKinsey scores — any BU in the Harvest/Divest zone that could not articulate a credible path to the Invest zone was exited. Between 1981 and 2001, GE divested hundreds of businesses and reinvested in high-attractiveness, high-strength BUs (jet engines, medical imaging, financial services).

**So what:** The GE-McKinsey matrix gave Welch an objective framework to make and defend portfolio decisions that would otherwise have been politically impossible in a company with entrenched business unit owners.

## Pitfalls
- Garbage-in: if criteria weights and ratings are not evidence-based, the 'objectivity' of the matrix is illusory — make the scoring debates explicit and audit the assumptions.
- Using the matrix without a clear action implication: each zone must map to a concrete investment decision, not just a label.
- Ignoring interdependencies: some Dogs cross-subsidize Stars (e.g., a high-volume but low-margin BU creates manufacturing scale that benefits a high-margin BU).

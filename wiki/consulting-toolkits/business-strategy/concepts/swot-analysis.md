# SWOT Analysis + TOWS Matrix

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A structured synthesis tool that consolidates internal analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses) and external analysis (Opportunities, Threats) into four quadrants, and then generates strategic options via the TOWS cross-matrix.

**Origin:** Attributed to Albert Humphrey at Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s–70s; SWOT became standard strategic planning currency by the 1980s. The TOWS cross-matrix extension was formalized by Heinz Weihrich in 1982.

## Why it works
Strategy is fundamentally about achieving fit between internal capabilities and external context. SWOT forces that fit question into a two-by-two structure; TOWS operationalizes it by systematically crossing each pair of quadrants to generate strategy options that are internally grounded rather than analytically arbitrary.

## When to use
As the synthesis step at the end of Phase 1 analysis, and as the starting point for Phase 2 strategy design. Best used when all internal and external analyses are complete — not as a substitute for them.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Complete the SWOT inputs first: internal analysis provides Strengths and Weaknesses; external analysis (Porter's Five Forces, PESTLE, competitor analysis) provides Opportunities and Threats.
2. For each quadrant, list factors ranked by strategic significance (most important first). Limit to 5–7 items per quadrant to avoid dilution — not every observation belongs in a SWOT.
3. Build the TOWS matrix by drawing a 2×2 grid with S/W on one axis and O/T on the other. In each cell, generate 1–3 strategic options that genuinely combine both inputs (not just restate either side).
4. Evaluate TOWS options against three criteria: strategic fit with mission/vision, distinctiveness (does it create competitive advantage?), and feasibility (can we execute it?). Score and rank options.
5. Feed the top TOWS options directly into Phase 2 strategy tool analysis as the candidate strategies to explore with BCG, Ansoff, and Porter's Generic Strategies.

## Real-life example — Apple (2001 iPod launch)
Apple's pre-iPod SWOT showed Strengths in design and software (OS X), a Weakness of sub-5% PC market share, Opportunities in the nascent digital music market (Napster had created demand, legal solutions were absent), and a Threat of commoditization in the PC market. The SO option — use design strength + software capability to capture the digital music opportunity — produced the iPod strategy. The WT option — avoid competing head-on in commoditizing PCs — reinforced the pivot.

**So what:** SWOT/TOWS is most powerful when it generates strategy options that are non-obvious — the iPod was an SO option that used Apple's design strength in a completely new category, not an extension of the existing business.

## Pitfalls
- Listing generic factors not specific to your company (e.g., 'Strength: good people') — every item must be specific and evidence-backed.
- Stopping at SWOT without building the TOWS cross-matrix: SWOT without TOWS is diagnosis without prescription.
- Allowing the SWOT to be a political document where weaknesses are soft-pedaled — rigor requires honesty about genuine weaknesses.

# Porter's Generic Competitive Strategies

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A framework that defines three defensible competitive positions for achieving above-average industry returns: Cost Leadership, Differentiation, and Focus (cost focus or differentiation focus).

**Origin:** Michael E. Porter, 'Competitive Strategy', The Free Press, 1980.

## Why it works
Sustainable competitive advantage requires a clear and consistent source of advantage — either lower cost than rivals, or a unique offering that commands a premium, applied either broadly or to a focused niche. Being 'stuck in the middle' (pursuing neither clearly) destroys above-average returns because no advantage is actually created or defended.

## When to use
When setting or refreshing competitive strategy; when margins are eroding; when a new competitor enters the market and forces a strategic response; when evaluating a business unit's competitive position.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Determine your competitive scope: do you target the full addressable market (broad) or a specific segment (narrow)?
2. Assess your cost position vs. rivals: do you have structural cost advantages (scale, experience curve, proprietary process)? Can you maintain the lowest total cost in the market?
3. Assess your differentiation capability: can you offer attributes customers value enough to pay a premium for — and can competitors not easily replicate them within 2–3 years?
4. Select one primary generic strategy and commit to it. If your cost position AND differentiation are both mediocre relative to competitors, you are stuck in the middle — this is the most dangerous position.
5. Validate the choice against the Product Life Cycle stage: Cost Leadership is more defensible in mature/declining markets; Differentiation is more defensible in growth/introduction stages; Focus strategies suit fragmented markets with distinct underserved niches.

## Real-life example — Southwest Airlines (Cost Leadership) vs. Singapore Airlines (Differentiation)
Southwest chose Cost Leadership (broad): standardized fleet (Boeing 737 only), point-to-point routes, no assigned seats, no meals — structural cost advantages producing the lowest cost per seat mile in US aviation. Singapore Airlines chose Differentiation (broad): first-class suites, world-class service, premium brand — commanding ticket prices 30–50% above economy competitors. Both strategies earned above-average returns in the same industry because each is internally consistent and non-imitative. A hybrid carrier trying to do both (e.g., legacy US carriers in the 2000s) faced structural margin compression.

**So what:** The power of Porter's framework is in the discipline of choice — once the strategy is selected, every investment, process, and culture decision should reinforce it, not dilute it.

## Pitfalls
- Believing that a company can successfully pursue Cost Leadership and Differentiation simultaneously in the same market — Porter argued this is structurally unstable; rare exceptions (Toyota, Apple) require unique capabilities that cannot be assumed.
- Selecting a generic strategy based on aspiration rather than evidence of actual advantage — 'we want to be premium' is not the same as 'customers will pay a premium and we can sustain the differentiation'.
- Neglecting the Focus option: for a smaller company without the scale for cost leadership or the resources for broad differentiation, a well-chosen niche focus is often the highest-return strategy.

# Value Chain Analysis

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A framework that disaggregates a company's activities into primary (value-creating) and support activities to identify where competitive advantage is actually created or destroyed.

**Origin:** Michael E. Porter, 'Competitive Advantage', The Free Press, 1985.

## Why it works
Competitive advantage does not reside in a company as a whole — it resides in specific activities. By mapping and costing every activity in the value chain, leadership can identify which activities are sources of differentiation or cost advantage, and which activities consume cost without creating proportional value.

## When to use
Identifying sources of competitive advantage; cost reduction programs; make-or-buy decisions; capability investment prioritization; post-M&A synergy identification.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Map all primary activities for your business: what are the specific sub-activities within inbound logistics (receiving, inventory, distribution to operations), operations (manufacturing, assembly, testing), outbound logistics (order processing, distribution), marketing & sales (brand, channels, pricing), and service (installation, repair, after-sales)?
2. Map all support activities: Firm Infrastructure (finance, legal, general management); HRM (recruiting, training, compensation); Technology Development (R&D, IT, process automation); Procurement (supplier selection, contract management).
3. For each activity, assign a cost (as % of total cost) and a differentiation potential score (1–5: how much does excellence here contribute to a customer-perceived unique value?).
4. Identify activities where you have cost advantages vs. competitors and activities where your differentiation score exceeds competitors. These are the sources of competitive advantage.
5. Identify activities where cost exceeds the value created — candidates for outsourcing, automation, or elimination.
6. Link the value chain analysis to the 7S framework: the activities where advantage is created must be supported by aligned Structure, Systems, and Skills.

## Real-life example — Zara (Inditex)
Zara's competitive advantage was revealed by a value chain analysis: unlike traditional fashion retailers (Operations: outsourced to Asia, Marketing: heavy brand advertising), Zara's unique advantages lay in two activities — Operations (own factories in Spain/Portugal/Morocco enabling 2-week design-to-shelf vs. 6 months for competitors) and Technology Development (store managers feed daily sales data directly to designers, closing the customer feedback loop). These two activities created a fundamentally different value chain that H&M, Gap, and Zara's rivals could not replicate without abandoning their entire cost model.

**So what:** Value chain analysis reveals that Zara's competitive advantage is not in a single activity but in the linkage between Technology Development (real-time sales data) and Operations (near-shore speed) — linkages are often more defensible than individual activity advantages.

## Pitfalls
- Mapping activities at too high a level (e.g., 'marketing') without disaggregating into specific activities where advantage actually resides.
- Analyzing the value chain without benchmarking against competitors — the analysis is only meaningful relative to alternatives.
- Ignoring activity linkages: competitive advantage often lies in how activities are connected, not in individual activities in isolation.

# Market Sizing (TAM / SAM / SOM)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A structured framework for estimating the total addressable market (TAM), serviceable addressable market (SAM), and serviceable obtainable market (SOM) to size market opportunities and validate growth strategies.

**Origin:** Standard in venture capital and strategy practice since the 1990s; the TAM/SAM/SOM framework was popularized through Silicon Valley startup due diligence and McKinsey/BCG market entry analyses.

## Why it works
Strategy requires knowing the size of the prize. Without market sizing, companies either over-invest in niche opportunities or under-invest in large ones. The three-level framework creates intellectual honesty: TAM is the theoretical maximum; SAM is what is genuinely addressable given your product and channels; SOM is a realistic capture estimate given competition and execution capacity.

## When to use
Market entry decisions; investment pitch preparation; product launch sizing; acquisition target evaluation; annual strategic planning.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the TAM using two independent methods and triangulate: (1) Top-Down: total industry revenue from an authoritative source × the share your product category represents; (2) Bottom-Up: total number of potential customers × average revenue per customer per year.
2. Define the SAM: apply filters to TAM for the constraints of your specific business model — geography served, product-capability limits, channel reach, and customer segment focus. SAM = TAM × relevant filters.
3. Define the SOM: estimate realistic market capture in a 3–5 year horizon given your current competitive position, go-to-market capacity, and the competitive intensity. SOM = SAM × achievable market share.
4. Sensitivity-test the sizing: document the 3 most important assumptions and show the impact of changing each by ±20% on the SOM estimate.
5. Validate with bottom-up customer data: identify 5–10 potential customers; estimate how much each would spend; extrapolate to the addressable universe. This ground-truth check often reveals errors in the top-down methodology.

## Real-life example — Zoom Video Communications (2014 market sizing)
Zoom's early market sizing: TAM = global video conferencing market ~$6B (top-down from IDC research) + SMB collaboration software ~$14B adjacent = $20B total. SAM = subset addressable via cloud-first SaaS model (excluding on-premise enterprise only) ≈ $8B. SOM = realistic 3-year target assuming 3–5% penetration of SAM ≈ $240–400M ARR. The sizing validated sufficient opportunity to justify venture investment while setting grounded revenue targets. By 2020 (COVID acceleration), Zoom exceeded its 3-year SOM in a single quarter — illustrating that good sizing quantifies the base case, not the upside scenario.

**So what:** Market sizing is most valuable not for its precision (all estimates are wrong) but for its discipline — it forces explicit assumptions about who will buy, at what price, and with what conversion rate, all of which are testable.

## Pitfalls
- Using only the top-down method: 'We're in a $50B market and we only need 1% share' is the most abused slide in startup pitches. Ground the estimate in a bottom-up customer count.
- Confusing TAM with SAM: TAM is not the opportunity; SAM is. A $50B TAM with a $100M SAM is a niche business.
- Not segmenting the SOM over time: a single SOM estimate for '3–5 years' is not useful for planning. Build a year-by-year capture curve.

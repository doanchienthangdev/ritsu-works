# Strategic Group Mapping

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A visualization technique that plots industry competitors on two strategic dimensions to reveal clusters of competitors pursuing similar strategies (strategic groups) and white spaces representing underserved market positions.

**Origin:** Michael E. Porter, 'Competitive Strategy', 1980. Extended by Kim and Mauborgne in 'Blue Ocean Strategy' as the Strategy Canvas (2005).

## Why it works
Competitors within the same strategic group face similar competitive pressures and are each other's most direct rivals. Competitors in different groups compete less directly. White spaces (no competitors) on the map represent either an unattractive position (competitors tried and failed) or an unexploited opportunity (no one has tried). The map reveals where the competitive battles are raging and where they are not.

## When to use
Market entry analysis; competitive positioning; Blue Ocean Strategy development; annual competitive intelligence review.

## Visual
`chart`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Select two axes that best differentiate strategic positions in your industry. Good axis candidates: price level, geographic scope, product/service breadth, vertical integration degree, innovation intensity, customer segment focus.
2. Plot each significant competitor at the position that best describes their current strategic position on both axes. Size the bubble proportional to market share or revenue.
3. Identify strategic groups: clusters of 2–5 competitors occupying similar positions on the map.
4. Identify white spaces: areas of the map with no competitors. For each white space, assess: why is it empty? (unattractive, or unexplored?) If unexplored, does it represent an opportunity consistent with our strategy?
5. Assess mobility barriers: what would prevent a competitor from moving from one strategic group to another? High mobility barriers protect your position; low barriers mean a competitor could rapidly enter your strategic group.
6. Plot your own position on the map. Are you in a crowded strategic group (Red Ocean) or a white space (Blue Ocean potential)?

## Real-life example — US Airline Industry
A strategic group map of the US airline industry in 2015 using axes of Price Level (Low → Premium) and Route Network (Point-to-Point → Hub-and-Spoke) shows three distinct clusters: (1) Ultra-Low-Cost Carriers (Spirit, Frontier) — low price, point-to-point; (2) Full-Service Legacy Carriers (Delta, American, United) — premium price, hub-and-spoke; (3) Southwest — unique middle position: low price but not ultra-low (more amenities), point-to-point but at scale. Southwest's strategic group of one provided a protected competitive position for decades — it was too large for ULCCs to imitate and too cost-efficient for legacy carriers to compete with.

**So what:** The strategic group map revealed that Southwest's competitive advantage came from occupying a strategic position that was internally consistent but structurally difficult for competitors to reach without dismantling their own business models.

## Pitfalls
- Choosing axes that are correlated with each other (e.g., price and quality, which often move together) — this collapses all competitors onto a diagonal and produces no insight.
- Not including potential entrants on the map: a technology company that has not yet entered your market but has all the capabilities to do so belongs on the map.
- Treating the map as static: strategic groups evolve; re-draw the map annually.

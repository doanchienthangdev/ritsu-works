# Objectives and Key Results (OKRs)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A goal-setting and performance management framework that cascades aspirational objectives with 2–4 measurable key results per objective, reviewed quarterly to maintain organizational focus and alignment.

**Origin:** Andy Grove, Intel, 1970s — described in 'High Output Management' (1983). Popularized at Google by John Doerr (who learned it from Grove), described in 'Measure What Matters' (2018).

## Why it works
Most organizations fail not from lack of strategy but from loss of focus: too many priorities dilute execution energy. OKRs force two disciplines simultaneously: (1) saying no to everything except the vital few objectives; (2) making success measurable so progress is unambiguous. The quarterly cadence creates organizational agility — the system adapts every 90 days rather than waiting for the annual planning cycle.

## When to use
Quarterly strategy execution management; aligning cross-functional teams around shared outcomes; scaling a startup through a growth phase; replacing MBO (Management by Objectives) with a more agile framework.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Set 3–5 company-level Objectives per quarter. Each Objective is a qualitative, inspiring direction (not a KPI): 'Become the #1 customer-rated service in our market' is an Objective; 'Increase NPS to 60' is a Key Result.
2. For each Objective, define 2–4 Key Results that collectively define what achieving the Objective looks like. Key Results must be quantitative, binary, or otherwise objectively verifiable at quarter-end.
3. Cascade: ask each team to set their own 3–5 team-level OKRs that contribute to the company-level OKRs. The connection should be explicit and traceable.
4. Set OKRs to be aspirational: if you are achieving 100% of your Key Results every quarter, your targets are too conservative. The 70% achievement rate is often cited as healthy for stretch OKRs.
5. At quarter-end, score each Key Result (0.0–1.0) and hold a public debrief: what was achieved? What was not? What will change in the next quarter? Use this to inform Q+1 OKR setting — not as a performance appraisal tool.

## Real-life example — Google (2000s)
Larry Page's first company-level OKR at Google: Objective — '10× the number of users experiencing high-quality search results'. Key Results: (1) 'Reduce average latency on search to <100ms', (2) 'Increase daily active users by 30%', (3) 'Launch in 3 new international languages'. The Objective was directional and inspiring; the Key Results were unambiguous. Google later credited OKRs with enabling the company to maintain focus on search quality even as it grew to thousands of employees across dozens of products.

**So what:** OKRs succeeded at Google not because of the framework but because of two disciplines: radical transparency (all OKRs visible to everyone) and separating OKR achievement from performance reviews (removing the incentive to sandbagging targets).

## Pitfalls
- Linking OKR scores to compensation: this incentivizes sandbagging (setting easy targets) and kills the aspiration discipline. OKRs should be separated from performance reviews.
- Setting too many OKRs: 3–5 company Objectives is the ceiling. More than 5 means everything is a priority, which means nothing is.
- Creating Key Results that are outputs (tasks completed) rather than outcomes (results achieved): 'Conduct 10 customer interviews' is a task; 'Identify 3 validated customer pain points' is an outcome.

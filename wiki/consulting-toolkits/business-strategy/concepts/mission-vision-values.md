# Mission, Vision, and Values

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
The foundational statements that define an organization's purpose (mission), desired future state (vision), and the principles that guide behavior and trade-off decisions (values).

**Origin:** Formalized in corporate planning literature through Peter Drucker's concept of organizational purpose (1954, 'The Practice of Management'); vision and values statements became standard strategic planning practice through the 1980s–1990s.

## Why it works
Strategy changes; purpose does not — or at least changes more slowly. Mission and vision create the stable frame within which strategic choices are made. Values are operationally critical: they resolve trade-offs that rules and procedures cannot anticipate. An organization with internalized values makes better distributed decisions faster than one governed purely by procedures.

## When to use
Founding or refounding an organization; new CEO taking over; post-merger integration (aligning two cultures); major strategic pivot; when the organization has lost strategic clarity.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the Mission: complete the sentence 'We exist to [verb] [what outcome] for [whom].' The mission should be specific enough to exclude some things — if the mission could describe any company, it needs to be sharpened.
2. Define the Vision: write the most ambitious, specific, time-bound description of success you can defend. Avoid generic statements ('be the leader in our industry'). A good vision passes the 'vivid picture' test — a new employee can see exactly what success looks like.
3. Define 3–5 Values: for each candidate value, apply the 'fire test' — would we actually terminate a relationship (client, employee) over violating this? If not, it is an aspiration, not a value. Write each value as an action statement ('We speak up even when it is uncomfortable') not a noun ('Courage').
4. Test mission/vision/values for alignment: does the mission connect logically to the vision? Do the values resolve the real trade-offs the organization faces (not decorative ones)?
5. Publish widely and use in decision-making: mission/vision/values only create value if they are actively referenced in strategic decisions, hiring, and performance management.

## Real-life example — Patagonia
Patagonia's mission: 'We're in business to save our home planet.' This is decision-useful: it has led the company to encourage customers to repair rather than buy new clothes ('Don't Buy This Jacket' campaign), to donate 1% of sales to environmental causes, and ultimately to transfer company ownership to a trust in 2022 to ensure profits fund climate work. Every major strategic decision — product line, pricing, partnerships, marketing — is tested against this mission. The result is exceptional brand loyalty among a specific customer segment willing to pay premium prices for alignment with the mission.

**So what:** A mission is powerful when it creates competitive disadvantage in the short run but structural advantage in the long run — Patagonia's mission excludes fast-fashion customers but secures deep loyalty from the customers it most wants.

## Pitfalls
- Writing values by committee vote: the resulting list becomes a collection of everyone's favorite words ('Integrity, Innovation, Excellence') that no one will actually invoke in a difficult decision.
- Separating the mission/vision exercise from the strategy: purpose and strategy must be co-developed — a mission developed by the marketing team without the strategy team produces a statement that inspires but does not guide.
- Never revisiting: a mission/vision appropriate for a $10M startup may be wrong for a $500M company; these statements need periodic review, especially after major strategy shifts.

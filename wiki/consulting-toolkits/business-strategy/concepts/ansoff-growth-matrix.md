# Ansoff Growth Matrix

*Category: growth · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A 2×2 strategic planning matrix that maps four growth strategies based on whether they involve existing or new products and existing or new markets.

**Origin:** Igor Ansoff, 1957, published in 'Strategies for Diversification', Harvard Business Review. One of the first formal tools for corporate growth strategy.

## Why it works
Growth always comes from one of four combinations of product and market newness, each with a different risk profile. Market penetration carries the least risk (known customers, known products); diversification carries the most (unknown customers, unknown products). The matrix makes the implicit risk of a growth ambition explicit and forces a conscious choice.

## When to use
Annual strategic planning; growth ambition-setting; investment allocation decisions; new CEO mandate to accelerate growth.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Map your current business precisely: which products, in which markets? This baseline is the starting point — all growth options are defined relative to it.
2. Generate candidate strategies in all four quadrants: for Market Penetration, ask 'how could we increase share in existing markets?'; for Market Development, ask 'which new segments or geographies could we enter with existing products?'; for Product Development, ask 'what adjacent products do our existing customers need?'; for Diversification, ask 'are there related or unrelated categories where our capabilities could win?'
3. Score each candidate on three dimensions: strategic fit (alignment with strengths and competitive position), market attractiveness (size, growth, margins), and execution risk (capability gap, capital requirement, time-to-payback).
4. Select a primary growth direction for the planning period and secondary options. Note that most successful companies pursue 70–20–10: 70% of growth investment in market penetration, 20% in adjacent moves, 10% in diversification bets.
5. Cross-check with the 7 Degrees of Freedom for Growth to ensure the option set is exhaustive before committing.

## Real-life example — Starbucks
Starbucks has executed all four Ansoff quadrants systematically. Market Penetration: loyalty program and mobile ordering increased purchase frequency among existing US customers. Market Development: aggressive international expansion (China, India) took the existing coffee-shop format to new markets. Product Development: food, merchandise, and RTD (ready-to-drink) grocery products sold to existing Starbucks customers. Diversification: Starbucks Reserve Roasteries and the Olive Way premium concept explore new premium segments with new format products.

**So what:** Starbucks' sustained growth comes from disciplined Ansoff execution — most investment in the proven lower-risk quadrants, with selective diversification bets that leverage the core brand.

## Pitfalls
- Choosing diversification because it feels most 'strategic' — it carries the highest failure rate. Most value destruction in corporate history comes from unrelated diversification.
- Treating the four quadrants as mutually exclusive: companies can and should pursue multiple strategies simultaneously, with appropriate investment weighting.
- Not defining 'market' precisely: geographic expansion and new-segment entry are both 'market development' but have very different risk and capability profiles.

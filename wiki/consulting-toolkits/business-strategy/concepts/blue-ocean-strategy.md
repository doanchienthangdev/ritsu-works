# Blue Ocean Strategy

*Category: growth · Toolkit: Business Strategy & Strategic Planning*

## What it is
A strategy framework that advocates creating uncontested market space (blue oceans) rather than competing in overcrowded existing markets (red oceans), using the Four Actions Framework (Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create) and Strategy Canvas.

**Origin:** W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, INSEAD, 2005 — 'Blue Ocean Strategy', Harvard Business Review Press. Based on a study of 150 strategic moves across 30 industries over 100 years.

## Why it works
In red oceans (existing markets), rivals fight over a fixed demand pie, and competition is zero-sum. Blue oceans are created by redefining industry boundaries through value innovation — simultaneously pursuing differentiation and low cost by eliminating factors the industry takes for granted and creating factors never offered before. This breaks the value-cost trade-off.

## When to use
When growth in the existing market is structurally limited; when competing in a commoditized market with deteriorating margins; when a new executive mandate calls for a step-change in competitive position.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Draw the current Strategy Canvas: list all the key competing factors in your industry (price, service speed, product range, brand, etc.) on the x-axis; plot the current offering curve of you and 2–3 key rivals on the y-axis (1–5 scale). This shows where you are converging with competitors.
2. Apply the Four Actions Framework: for each factor, ask — should we Eliminate (remove entirely), Reduce (lower below industry standard), Raise (increase above industry standard), or Create (add a new factor)? Each action in one quadrant should be balanced by actions in the opposite quadrant to control cost.
3. Construct a new value curve on the Strategy Canvas based on the E-R-R-C actions. Test for three criteria: focus (a few things dramatically different, not incremental improvement on everything), divergence (the curve looks distinctly different from rivals), and a compelling tagline.
4. Quantify the demand potential of the reconstructed market: identify non-customers in three tiers (about-to-leave customers, conscious non-users, and unexplored non-users) and estimate the addressable pool.
5. Test for business model viability: does the Blue Ocean offering deliver a price accessible to the mass of target buyers while achieving a sufficient cost structure to earn target margins?

## Real-life example — Cirque du Soleil
The traditional circus industry competed on animal acts, star performers, and multiple show arenas. Cirque du Soleil applied the Four Actions Framework: ELIMINATED animals and star performers (high-cost, declining popularity); REDUCED thrill-and-danger elements; RAISED unique venue and production quality; CREATED a theatrical narrative storyline and artistic music. Result: a completely new Strategy Canvas aimed at adult corporate entertainment buyers (not children) who would pay 5× the ticket price of a traditional circus. Cirque created a $1B+ industry category with no direct competitors for two decades.

**So what:** Blue Ocean Strategy is not about being cheaper or better than rivals — it is about making the competition irrelevant by redefining the product's entire value curve.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing Blue Ocean with 'premium' strategy: blue oceans must create new demand, not just charge more for the same product.
- The ERRC framework without the Strategy Canvas: the visual comparison with rivals is essential to verify you have genuinely diverged, not just improved incrementally.
- Underestimating the organizational change required: blue ocean strategies typically require new capabilities and business model changes that existing organizations resist.

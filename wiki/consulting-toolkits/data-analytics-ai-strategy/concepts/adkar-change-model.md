# ADKAR Change Management Model

*Category: change · Toolkit: Data Analytics & AI Strategy*

## What it is
A five-element individual change framework (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement) that maps each stakeholder group to their change readiness and prescribes targeted interventions for each gap — preventing the generic-training failure mode.

**Origin:** Developed by Jeff Hiatt, founder of Prosci; published in 'ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and our Community' (2006). The most widely-used individual change framework in enterprise transformation, used by 80% of Fortune 100 companies (Prosci, 2022).

## Why it works
Most data programmes fail not because the technology is wrong but because people do not change their behaviour. ADKAR addresses the root cause: change fails when the programme provides Knowledge and Ability (training) to people who lack Desire (motivation) or Awareness (why the change is needed). Diagnosing which ADKAR element is the barrier enables targeted interventions rather than generic training that addresses the wrong problem.

## When to use
In Phase 6 (change management) from the programme start. Apply throughout all phases as the change diagnosis and intervention-design tool.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Map every major stakeholder group to their primary ADKAR barrier. Common patterns for data transformations: senior leaders (have Awareness, often lack Desire if data challenges intuition), frontline managers (have Desire, lack Knowledge and Ability), analysts (have Knowledge and Ability, need Reinforcement their work will be used).
2. Design targeted interventions per ADKAR gap: Awareness → executive briefings with competitive data; Desire → co-design sessions (WIIFM conversations at a personal level); Knowledge → role-specific training; Ability → practice with real data from the person's own domain; Reinforcement → integrate data use into performance reviews, celebrate public wins.
3. Run an ADKAR assessment at programme start (survey + structured interviews per stakeholder group) and quarterly thereafter.
4. Identify the lowest-scoring ADKAR element per group — this is the constraint. Adding Knowledge training to someone who lacks Desire wastes budget and generates resentment.
5. For senior leadership: Desire is the most critical and most often skipped element. Address by showing cases where data revealed something intuition missed — framing data as augmenting their judgment, not replacing it.
6. Define reinforcement mechanisms before the programme closes: what management routine changes, incentive adjustments, or performance-review criteria will sustain behaviour after the programme team leaves?

## Real-life example — Unilever
Unilever's data transformation used ADKAR to segment 5,000 brand managers into three cohorts: low Awareness (senior brand directors — intervention: quarterly 'data-driven competitor' briefings), low Desire (mid-level managers who saw analytics as IT's job — intervention: co-design sessions where managers built their own first dashboard), and low Ability (analysts with Knowledge from training but no live project experience — intervention: 8-week analytics apprenticeship with a real business problem). Brand decision-making speed improved 35% within 18 months.

**So what:** Generic change management training fails because it addresses Knowledge and Ability for people stuck at Awareness and Desire. Diagnosing the ADKAR barrier first makes every change investment 3× more effective.

## Pitfalls
- Applying Knowledge (training) as the default for all change resistance: counter: diagnose the ADKAR barrier first — if the person lacks Desire, training will not help and may increase resistance.
- Ignoring senior leaders: counter: a CDO who presents data and is overruled by an executive's intuition in the same meeting sends the most powerful anti-change signal possible.
- Treating Reinforcement as optional: counter: without reinforcement mechanisms, behaviour reverts to the pre-change default within 6 months of programme close.

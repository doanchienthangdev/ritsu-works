# 3×3 Risk Heat Map (Executive)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A 3×3 matrix plotting all risks by Likelihood (Low/Medium/High) against Consequence (Low/Medium/High), colour-coded Red/Amber/Green, designed for Board and executive reporting where simplicity and immediate comprehension are paramount.

**Origin:** Adapted from the 5×5 engineering risk matrix for Board-level use in the 1990s by consulting firms (McKinsey, Deloitte). The 3×3 variant became the standard for Board risk reporting in UK corporate governance practice following the Turnbull Guidance (1999) and its revisions.

## Why it works
The Board needs to understand the risk portfolio at a glance — not manage it at the operational level. The 3×3 matrix sacrifices granularity for clarity: 9 cells rather than 25 makes the heat map readable in a 30-second Board paper scan. The 3-zone colour scheme (Red = act immediately, Amber = monitor, Green = accept) maps directly to Board-level decision categories. For organizations with fewer than 30 material risks, the 3×3 provides sufficient differentiation without the false precision of a 5×5.

## When to use
Phase 4 (Risk Prioritization), for Board, Audit Committee, and investor reporting. Produce quarterly. Use the 5×5 variant for operational risk committees.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Convert the 5×5 residual risk scores to the 3-band scale: Scores 1–7 → Green (Low); 8–14 → Amber (Medium); 15–25 → Red (High). Or use the 3-band likelihood/consequence scoring directly if a 5×5 assessment was not run.
2. Draw the 3×3 grid with Likelihood on the horizontal axis and Consequence on the vertical. Label each axis with Low/Medium/High and the corresponding probability anchors.
3. Colour each cell: Top-right 2×2 area (High Consequence + any Likelihood; High Likelihood + Medium Consequence) = Red. Bottom-left 2×2 area (Low Consequence + any Likelihood; Low Likelihood) = Green. Diagonal = Amber. Validate zone boundaries match the Risk Appetite Statement.
4. Plot each risk: use the Risk ID label (e.g., OPS-007) positioned in the appropriate cell. If multiple risks land in the same cell, stack the labels vertically within the cell.
5. Add a legend: Red = Mandatory mitigation within 30 days; Amber = Plan within 90 days, quarterly monitoring; Green = Annual review, accepted.
6. Include a 'Change since last period' column or arrows on each risk label (↑ deteriorating, → stable, ↓ improving) to show Board the trend, not just the current state.
7. Pair the heat map with a narrative 'Top 5 Risks' commentary (one paragraph per risk) to give context to the visual. The heat map shows where; the narrative explains why and what's being done.

## Real-life example — Rolls-Royce plc
Rolls-Royce presents a 3×3 risk heat map in its Annual Report, reporting to shareholders the position of its principal risks across the strategic, financial, operational, and compliance categories. In the 2020–2021 period, the 'Commercial Aerospace demand recovery' risk occupied the top-right Red cell (High Likelihood of continued demand suppression, High Consequence for revenue). The heat map was accompanied by a narrative explaining the COVID-19 driver and the mitigation actions (cost reduction program, liquidity management). The 3×3 format allowed investors with no risk management background to immediately grasp that this was the dominant risk and that the mitigation was active.

**So what:** Public companies use the 3×3 heat map as a communication tool to stakeholders who are not risk specialists. Its value is in accessibility — a regulator, investor, or journalist can understand the organization's risk profile from one page without reading the full risk register.

## Pitfalls
- Using the 3×3 as the sole risk management tool — the 3×3 is a Board communication tool, not an operational management tool. Counter: always maintain the full 5×5 operational register alongside the 3×3 Board heat map.
- Populating the 3×3 with too many risks — a heat map with 40 risk labels is unreadable. Counter: limit the 3×3 to the top 15–20 risks by residual score. All risks are in the 5×5 or operational register.
- Zone boundaries not aligned to the Risk Appetite Statement — if the Board approved 'High = 15+ on the 5×5 scale' but the 3×3 heat map colours the 'High Likelihood × High Consequence' cell differently, the Board heat map is misleading. Counter: derive zone boundaries mathematically from the RAS and document the derivation.

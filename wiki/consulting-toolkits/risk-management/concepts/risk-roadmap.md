# Risk Management Implementation Roadmap

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A 12–24 month phased implementation plan that sequences the activities required to build the risk management capability from its current maturity state to its target state, with defined milestones, budget estimates, and owners for each phase.

**Origin:** Phased implementation roadmaps are standard in management consulting delivery (McKinsey, Deloitte, BCG use them to structure any large-scale capability-building engagement). The specific application to risk management implementation follows the structure recommended by ISO 31000 implementation guides and consulting best practice for ERM program design.

## Why it works
The risk management strategy is only valuable if it is implemented. A roadmap converts the strategy (what we will achieve) into an operational plan (what we will do, when, and with what resources). Phasing the implementation prevents the common failure mode of attempting to implement all 10 strategy components simultaneously — which produces nothing to completion. Three phases (Foundation → Embed → Optimize) align to the ISO 31000 improvement cycle and allow early wins (Phase 1) to build organizational credibility before more ambitious capability building (Phase 3).

## When to use
Phase 1 (Risk Management Strategy), Step 6. Built after the maturity gap analysis and before team/budget design. Revised annually as part of the risk management strategy review.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start with the maturity gap analysis from Phase 1, Step 3: the roadmap should close the identified gaps in priority order.
2. Define three implementation phases with clear names and milestone outcomes. Phases should be sequential but overlapping — Phase 2 activities can begin before all Phase 1 milestones are complete.
3. For each phase, list the specific activities (not 'improve risk management' but 'design and roll out risk identification workshop facilitation guide for all 6 business units').
4. Estimate the budget for each phase: FTE time (at fully-loaded cost), technology (GRC platform if applicable), training, external advisory support. Present as a range, not a false precision.
5. Assign a phase owner (typically the CRO for Phase 1, a program manager for Phases 2–3).
6. Identify the 3 most critical dependencies: what must be true for each phase to begin? (e.g., 'Phase 2 requires the risk register to be live and the Board to have approved the RAS')
7. Present the roadmap to the Executive Risk Committee and obtain explicit approval, including budget sign-off. The roadmap without budget approval is a wish list.

## Real-life example — Allianz SE
Allianz's Group Risk function implemented a 3-phase risk management capability improvement program from 2015–2018 following Solvency II implementation. Phase 1 (2015, 12 months) established the ORSA (Own Risk and Solvency Assessment) process, the Group risk taxonomy, and the first consolidated Group risk dashboard. Phase 2 (2016–2017, 18 months) embedded quarterly risk review cadences across all business units and launched a Group-wide risk awareness training program. Phase 3 (2018, ongoing) introduced quantitative risk modeling (scenario analysis, stress testing) and a Risk Culture Index measured annually across 150,000 employees. The phased approach allowed Allianz to meet its Solvency II regulatory deadline (Phase 1) while building sustainable capability over 3 years rather than attempting a big-bang implementation.

**So what:** Allianz's phased approach solved the classic tension between regulatory deadline (requiring Phase 1 within 12 months) and sustainable capability (requiring 3 years). Phasing let them say 'compliant now, excellent by Year 3' — a sequencing that works for any organization facing both immediate compliance and long-term capability goals.

## Pitfalls
- Roadmap without budget — a roadmap that doesn't include budget estimates is not a plan; it is a narrative. Without explicit budget approval, every Phase 2 and Phase 3 activity will be cancelled when business pressures arise. Counter: always present the roadmap with budget estimates in the same document.
- Underestimating Phase 1 — organizations consistently underestimate the time and effort required to establish the basic infrastructure (register, governance, first report). A Phase 1 that takes 3 months in the plan but 9 months in reality destroys the Phase 2 and 3 timeline. Counter: add 50% buffer to Phase 1 estimates from the first draft.

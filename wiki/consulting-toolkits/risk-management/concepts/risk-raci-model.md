# Risk Management RACI Matrix

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A Responsible-Accountable-Consulted-Informed matrix that defines the roles and decision rights for every major risk management activity across the Three Lines of Defence, eliminating accountability ambiguity and defining who does what at each stage of the risk cycle.

**Origin:** RACI as a project management and organizational design tool was formalized by PMI and is widely used in consulting engagements (McKinsey, Deloitte, Accenture) to define organizational decision rights. Its application to risk management roles was standardized by the IIA Three Lines Model and ISO 31000 implementation guidance.

## Why it works
Risk management processes that lack a RACI produce the most common failure mode in organizational design: activities are either done by everyone (duplication, inconsistency) or by no one (gaps). In risk management specifically, the RACI is the mechanism that operationalizes the Three Lines of Defence: Line 1 is 'Accountable' for identifying and treating risks in their area; Line 2 is 'Responsible' for the framework, tools, and monitoring; Line 3 is 'Consulted' (not responsible or accountable) for assurance activities. Without the RACI, these distinctions remain theoretical.

## When to use
Phase 1 (Risk Management Strategy), Step 7. Built alongside the team structure and budget design. Also use when a major organizational restructuring changes the distribution of risk management responsibilities.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List all major risk management activities across the full risk cycle: identification, assessment, prioritization, mitigation design, mitigation monitoring, closure, issue escalation, reporting, appetite setting, framework review.
2. For each activity, assign the four RACI roles: Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome and is answerable if it fails — only one per activity), Consulted (provides input before the work is done), Informed (receives output after the work is done).
3. Validate that each activity has exactly ONE Accountable. Multiple Accountable entries mean no one is actually accountable. No Accountable entry means the activity has no governance.
4. Validate against the Three Lines of Defence: Line 1 should be Accountable for identifying and treating risks in their area; Line 2 should be Responsible for the framework tools; Line 3 should be Consulted (not Responsible or Accountable for operational risk activities).
5. Share the draft RACI with each organizational role listed in the columns. Ask: 'Is there anything you are listed as Accountable for that you don't have the authority to deliver?' This reveals authority mismatches.
6. Publish the RACI in the Risk Management Policy and use it as the foundation for the risk team's role descriptions and performance objectives.
7. Review annually — RACI roles change when the organizational structure changes.

## Real-life example — Banco Santander
Following its rapid international expansion (UK, US, Latin America), Santander implemented a Group Risk RACI matrix to resolve accountability confusion across its multi-jurisdictional risk management function. The critical design decision: Group Risk Function (Line 2) is 'Responsible' for the risk framework and methodology; Country CROs (Line 1) are 'Accountable' for risk register maintenance in their jurisdiction. This distinction resolved the prior ambiguity where Group would 'take over' country risk registers when quality was poor, creating a cycle of dependency. After the RACI, Group's role was explicitly to challenge and coach, not to do. Country risk register quality improved by 40% in 18 months because country CROs were now unambiguously accountable.

**So what:** Santander's RACI revealed that the Group's prior practice of 'taking over' poor-quality country risk registers was creating moral hazard — countries that knew Group would fix it for them had no incentive to fix it themselves. The RACI made country accountability explicit and non-negotiable.

## Pitfalls
- Multiple Accountable entries per row — this is the most common RACI design error and defeats the purpose of the tool. Counter: enforce the rule 'exactly one Accountable per activity.' If two people claim Accountability, this is a governance conflict that must be resolved before the RACI is published.
- RACI at too high a level of abstraction — 'Risk Management: R=Risk Function, A=CRO, C=BU, I=Board' is not useful. Counter: decompose each risk management phase into its individual activities (10–15 activities minimum) and apply RACI at the activity level.

# Risk Management Guiding Principles

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A set of 6–8 non-negotiable operating principles that govern how the risk management function behaves and makes decisions — resolving ambiguity when the policy doesn't cover a situation and reinforcing the risk culture the function is trying to build.

**Origin:** Guiding principles as an organizational design tool originate in systems thinking and values-based management (Collins & Porras, 'Built to Last', 1994). Their application to risk management was formalized in ISO 31000:2018, which defines 8 risk management principles as the foundational layer of the framework (Integrated, Structured, Customized, Inclusive, Dynamic, Best available information, Human and cultural factors, Continual improvement).

## Why it works
Policies cover defined situations; principles cover undefined ones. Every risk management situation that is not explicitly covered by policy (and most real situations are not) requires a judgment call. Principles provide the decision rule for those judgment calls. A principle like 'We report what we find, not what people want to hear' covers an infinite number of specific situations where the risk function faces pressure to soften or omit unflattering risk findings. Without this principle, the decision is made differently each time depending on who is applying pressure and how much.

## When to use
Phase 1 (Risk Management Strategy), Step 9. Built after the Mission/Vision/Values and before the final governance charter is approved. Principles should be referenced in every governance document and every risk committee meeting.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start with the ISO 31000:2018 eight principles as a reference list. Select 6–8 that are most relevant to the organization's current risk maturity failures — the principles that address the most common current failure modes.
2. For each selected principle, write a behavioral statement that is specific to the organization's context: not 'We are transparent' but 'We include all High and Medium risks in the Board risk report, even when the business unit responsible for that risk objects.'
3. Test each principle against a real past situation where it was violated (a time when the risk function 'didn't follow this principle'). The principle is most credible when it has a concrete counter-example that everyone recognizes.
4. Limit the list to 6–8 principles — a list of 15 principles is a policy document, not a guiding principles statement. Fewer is more memorable; memorable is what makes them effective.
5. Obtain Board approval and publish in the Risk Management Policy, the risk team's onboarding materials, and the wall of the risk function's workspace.
6. Reference the principles explicitly in risk committee meetings when a behavioral issue arises: 'The principle of Transparency requires that we include this risk in the Board report, despite the BU leader's objection.' This is how principles become operational, not just decorative.

## Real-life example — McKinsey & Company
McKinsey's internal risk management function (which manages client engagement, reputational, and operational risk for the firm) operates on a principle that has been publicly articulated by senior partners: 'The reputation of the firm is the most important asset we manage. No engagement, regardless of revenue, is worth a reputational risk that we cannot mitigate.' This principle operationalizes a Terminate response option for any engagement that meets the reputational risk threshold — regardless of the financial opportunity. It has been cited as the principle underlying McKinsey's withdrawal from a number of highly lucrative but reputationally problematic client relationships (notably the Purdue Pharma opioid work, which the firm later acknowledged should have been declined under this principle).

**So what:** McKinsey's reputational risk principle demonstrates the value of a principle that is specific enough to drive concrete business decisions ('decline this engagement') rather than general enough to be ignored ('we care about reputation'). The Purdue Pharma case, where the principle was applied too late, is equally instructive: principles are only effective when they are applied consistently.

## Pitfalls
- Generic principles that apply to any function — 'Excellence', 'Integrity', 'Innovation' are not risk management principles; they are corporate values. Counter: every principle should be specific to the risk management function's unique challenges and failure modes. Test each principle: could it be violated specifically in the context of risk management work? If not, it's too generic.
- Principles that are aspirational rather than operational — 'We aspire to embed risk culture throughout the organization' describes an aspiration, not a principle. Counter: write principles as present-tense behavioral commitments: 'Risk management is embedded in every operational decision.' The present tense creates accountability.

# Stakeholder Power-Interest Analysis

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A structured framework for mapping all stakeholders who affect or are affected by the organization's risk management function onto a Power-Interest grid, determining engagement priority and strategy for each stakeholder group.

**Origin:** The power-interest matrix was developed by Mendelow (1991) at the Second International Conference on Information Systems. It became the dominant stakeholder prioritization tool in management consulting and project management (PMI PMBOK) through the 1990s–2000s. In risk management, it is applied specifically to determine who needs what level of risk information and engagement.

## Why it works
Not all stakeholders need the same risk information in the same format at the same frequency. The Board needs a strategic risk overview quarterly; the operational risk team needs the full register daily. The power-interest grid makes these differences explicit by categorizing stakeholders on two dimensions: Power (their ability to influence the risk management function's effectiveness and credibility) and Interest (the degree to which they have a stake in the organization's risk outcomes). The four quadrants produce four unambiguous engagement strategies: Manage Closely (high/high) — give them maximum attention and involvement; Keep Satisfied (high/low) — keep them informed without overwhelming them; Keep Informed (low/high) — ensure they receive relevant information; Monitor (low/low) — lightweight engagement, sufficient to prevent surprises.

## When to use
Phase 7 (Stakeholder Engagement), Step 1. Also use at the start of any risk management program design, before major risk reporting redesigns, and after leadership changes that alter the stakeholder landscape.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Identify all stakeholders: list every individual, group, or organization that has a material relationship with the risk management function. Include internal (Board, executive, business units, functions) and external (regulators, auditors, investors, key suppliers, customers).
2. Score each stakeholder on Power (1–5: 1=no influence; 5=can shut down or transform the risk function by their decisions) and Interest (1–5: 1=no stake in risk outcomes; 5=outcomes directly affect their financial or operational position).
3. Plot each stakeholder on the 2×2 grid using the power and interest scores. Use midpoints (2.5) as the quadrant boundaries.
4. Assign the engagement strategy based on quadrant: Manage Closely / Keep Satisfied / Keep Informed / Monitor.
5. For each 'Manage Closely' stakeholder: define the specific engagement objective (what do you need them to do?), the channel, frequency, and the key risk message they should receive.
6. Identify the stakeholder most likely to resist or undermine the risk management function (the 'blocker') and design a specific engagement strategy to address their concerns.
7. Review the stakeholder map whenever there is a material change in the organizational or external environment: new Board members, leadership changes, new regulators, M&A activity, or a major risk incident that changes who cares about risk.

## Real-life example — Johnson & Johnson
J&J's stakeholder analysis for its enterprise risk management function identifies: the Board Risk Committee (high power, high interest — Manage Closely) as requiring quarterly risk reports with heat map and top-10 narrative; the FDA (high power, low interest in ERM process but high interest in product safety outcomes — Keep Satisfied) as requiring proactive notification of safety-related risk events; patient advocacy groups (low power, high interest — Keep Informed) as requiring annual transparency reports on product risk management; and general media (low power, low interest in ERM — Monitor). J&J's Credo — which places patients first — operationally means that patient advocacy groups receive more engagement than their formal 'low power' classification would suggest, demonstrating that ethical considerations can override the grid's strategic logic.

**So what:** J&J's experience illustrates an important nuance: the power-interest grid determines the minimum engagement required; organizational values may determine that certain stakeholders deserve more engagement than the grid prescribes. The grid is a prioritization tool, not an absolute rule.

## Pitfalls
- Classifying stakeholders based on formal authority rather than actual influence — a major investor with a 3% stake may have low formal power but extremely high actual influence if they are vocal in the media. Counter: assess actual influence patterns, not just formal hierarchical power.
- Treating the stakeholder map as a one-time deliverable — stakeholder power and interest changes as the external environment, leadership, and organizational position change. Counter: review the map at every annual risk management strategy review.
- Using the grid to justify minimal engagement with 'Monitor' stakeholders — a 'Monitor' stakeholder who becomes concerned and moves to 'Keep Informed' without being noticed can become a disruptive critic. Counter: set a 'power/interest change trigger' threshold and schedule an annual minimum touchpoint for all Monitor stakeholders.

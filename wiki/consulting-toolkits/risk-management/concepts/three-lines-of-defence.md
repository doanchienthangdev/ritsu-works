# Three Lines of Defence (Three Lines Model)

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A governance framework that defines clear roles and responsibilities for risk management across three organizational layers: Line 1 (business units own and manage risks), Line 2 (risk and compliance functions set policy and monitor), and Line 3 (internal audit independently assures). Updated to the 'Three Lines Model' by the IIA in 2020.

**Origin:** Originally formalized by the Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA) in 2013 as the 'Three Lines of Defence.' Updated in 2020 to the 'Three Lines Model' to reflect a more collaborative (rather than purely defensive) framing. Widely adopted by financial regulators (Basel Committee, PRA, FCA), insurance regulators (Solvency II), and corporate governance codes globally.

## Why it works
Risk management fails when everyone thinks someone else owns it. The Three Lines Model solves this by creating explicit, non-overlapping accountability: Line 1 owns the risk (it is in their business process); Line 2 provides the framework, tools, and monitoring that Line 1 uses; Line 3 independently assesses whether Lines 1 and 2 are actually working as designed. The model prevents two failure modes: (a) Line 2 shadow-managing risks that Line 1 should own (creating accountability diffusion); (b) Line 3 (internal audit) identifying risks rather than assuring that Lines 1 and 2 are identifying them (scope confusion). The 2020 update added the 'Governing Body' as an overarching fourth element, recognizing that the Board must actively oversee the model, not just receive assurance from it.

## When to use
Use when establishing or reforming the risk governance structure (Phase 1, Step 8). Essential for regulated industries (financial services, healthcare, utilities). Also use when a risk incident reveals accountability confusion — 'who owns this risk?' is a Three Lines problem.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the Three Lines for your organization: identify which teams and roles belong to Line 1, which to Line 2, and which to Line 3. Some functions (e.g., Finance) have components in both Lines 1 and 2 — resolve these overlaps explicitly.
2. Draft a RACI matrix for all risk activities (identification, assessment, monitoring, reporting, escalation, assurance) with one column per Line. Confirm that Line 1 is 'Accountable' for identification and treatment; Line 2 is 'Responsible' for framework and monitoring; Line 3 is 'Consulted' (receives, not performs, operational risk activities).
3. Design the committee structure that connects the three lines: an Operational Risk Forum (Line 1 inputs → Line 2 aggregates), an Executive Risk Committee (Line 2 presents → Line 1 executives respond), a Board Risk Committee (Line 2 reports → Line 3 attests).
4. Establish the information flows: what data does Line 2 need from Line 1 (risk register updates, incident reports, control self-assessments)? What does Line 3 need from Line 2 (risk framework documentation, prior audit findings, heat maps)?
5. Codify the model in a Risk Management Policy, approved by the Board. This document is the 'constitution' of the risk governance structure.
6. Run an annual 'Three Lines health check': interview representatives from each line and ask 'Do you feel clear about your risk responsibilities?' and 'Do you know what the other lines are doing?' Gaps in awareness signal structural or communication failures.
7. For regulated industries: validate the Three Lines structure against regulator expectations (Basel Committee SR 11-7, PRA Supervisory Statement SS1/13, FCA guidance) before finalizing.

## Real-life example — HSBC
Following the US Senate's 2012 findings that HSBC's anti-money-laundering (AML) controls had failed, enabling billions in drug cartel transactions, HSBC undertook a wholesale rebuild of its Three Lines of Defence for financial crime risk. Line 1 (business relationship managers) were given mandatory financial crime risk training and AML risk checklists embedded in the client onboarding process. Line 2 (Global Standards function, a new Line 2 body reporting to the Board) was created with 4,000+ FTE and explicit authority to block transactions that Line 1 approved. Line 3 (Internal Audit and the external Compliance Monitor appointed by the US Department of Justice) independently attested the effectiveness of Lines 1 and 2 quarterly. The rebuild took 5 years and $2.5B in compliance investment.

**So what:** HSBC's pre-2012 failure was a Three Lines breakdown: Line 1 owned the risk but lacked the tools and incentives to manage it; Line 2 lacked the authority to override Line 1; Line 3 was not auditing financial crime risk as a priority. The model's value is only realized when all three lines are resourced, empowered, and actually functioning independently.

## Pitfalls
- Line 2 doing Line 1's job — when the risk function writes the risk register entries that business units should be writing, Line 1 develops no risk ownership. Counter: Line 2 provides the tool and the process; Line 1 populates it and owns the content.
- Line 3 identifying operational risks rather than assuring the system — internal audit should not be a risk identification function. Counter: clarify in the Audit Charter that Internal Audit assures the design and operating effectiveness of Lines 1 and 2.
- The Three Lines as a siloed structure rather than a collaborative system — the 2020 IIA update explicitly rejected the 'defence' metaphor because it implied Lines are adversarial. Counter: create joint forums where all three lines share information and align on priorities.

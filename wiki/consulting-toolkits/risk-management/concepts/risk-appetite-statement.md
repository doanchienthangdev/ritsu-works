# Risk Appetite Statement (RAS)

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A board-approved document that defines the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategic objectives. It provides the decision rule that makes risk prioritization and mitigation choices defensible and consistent.

**Origin:** Developed as a governance best practice by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (2013 Principles for an Effective Risk Appetite Framework) and subsequently adopted as standard practice across industries via ISO 31000, COSO ERM, and the UK Corporate Governance Code.

## Why it works
Without a documented appetite, every risk prioritization decision is a judgment call that different people will make differently. The RAS converts the Board's risk tolerance — which is inherently qualitative ('we are conservative on regulatory risk') — into an operational decision rule ('any risk with >5% probability of causing a regulatory fine >€1M must be escalated to the Board within 48 hours'). The two-tier structure (qualitative + quantitative) is essential: the qualitative tier communicates culture; the quantitative tier enables enforcement.

## When to use

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Begin with a Board workshop: present 3–5 anonymized risk scenarios that test where the Board's appetite boundary lies. Use a hypothetical: 'If there is a 20% probability of losing €5M on a new market entry, would you proceed?' Iterate to calibrate appetite.
2. Define the risk categories relevant to the organization (typically 6–10). Use the organization's risk taxonomy from Phase 2 as the category list.
3. For each category, write a qualitative appetite band using a consistent scale: None (zero tolerance) / Low (very limited; avoid where possible) / Moderate (willing to accept some; requires controls) / High (actively willing to accept for appropriate reward).
4. Convert each qualitative band to a quantitative threshold. For financial risks: express as €/$ absolute amount or % of EBITDA. For operational: express as hours of downtime, error rate, SLA breach rate. For reputational: express as NPS movement or media incident count. If the Board cannot agree on a number, that is a governance gap — escalate.
5. Define the escalation trigger for each category: what event or threshold triggers automatic escalation to the risk committee or Board?
6. Obtain formal Board Risk Committee approval. The RAS is not an executive document — it must be signed by the Board.
7. Review annually (at minimum) and whenever the strategy changes materially. The RAS is a living document, not a one-time deliverable.

## Real-life example — Lloyds Banking Group
Following the 2008 financial crisis and the Halifax Bank of Scotland acquisition, Lloyds Banking Group rebuilt its Risk Appetite Framework with an explicit Risk Appetite Statement approved by the Board Risk Committee. The RAS set quantitative thresholds for: maximum loan-to-deposit ratio (100%), maximum single-counterparty credit concentration (10% of Tier 1 capital), and maximum annual conduct risk losses (£500M). These thresholds became operational decision rules that halted several proposed lending programs that would have breached the appetite. The RAS was publicly disclosed in Lloyds' Annual Report as part of its Pillar 3 regulatory disclosure — making it a credibility signal to investors as well as an internal governance tool.

**So what:** The quantitative thresholds in Lloyds' RAS made abstract Board risk preferences into stopping criteria that front-line decision-makers could apply without Board involvement. This is the difference between a governance document and an operational tool.

## Pitfalls
- Qualitative-only RAS — without quantitative thresholds, the RAS cannot be used as a prioritization or escalation decision rule. Counter: always define the number that triggers action, even if it requires a Board discussion to agree on it.
- Static RAS that does not change with strategy — if the company enters a new market or acquires a business, the appetite thresholds need re-calibration. Counter: link the RAS review to the annual strategy cycle, not just the compliance calendar.
- Appetite bands that are too broad — 'Moderate appetite for financial risk' covers everything. Counter: decompose financial risk into sub-categories (liquidity, credit, market, FX) and set separate thresholds for each.

# Risk Management Mission, Vision and Values

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A one-page statement that defines the risk management function's purpose (mission), its 3-year aspiration (vision), and the behavioral principles (values) that govern how it operates — aligning the function to the organization's culture and preventing it from becoming a compliance silo.

**Origin:** Mission, vision, and values framing as an organizational design tool was popularized by Jim Collins and Jerry Porras in 'Built to Last' (1994) and has been standard in management consulting engagements since. Its application to functional departments (including risk management) reflects the recognition that functions with a clear identity and purpose outperform those defined purely by their reporting line and policy mandate.

## Why it works
Risk management functions without a clear identity are captured by one of two failure modes: they become 'the police' (adversarial, compliance-focused, avoided by business units) or 'the bureaucracy' (process-heavy, irrelevant, ignored). A well-designed Mission/Vision/Values statement prevents both failure modes by creating a shared identity that is aligned to business value creation. The mission connects risk management to the organization's purpose; the vision sets a measurable aspirational state; the values define the behavioral norms that determine how the function engages with the rest of the organization.

## When to use
Phase 1 (Risk Management Strategy), Step 4. Should be completed after the maturity assessment and before the KPI scorecard, because the vision informs what the KPIs should measure.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Facilitate a 2-hour workshop with the risk team and 2–3 senior business unit leaders. Use the question 'What would an excellent risk management function do for this organization that ours doesn't do today?' as the starting point.
2. Draft the Mission in plain language, not corporate jargon. Test it with the question: 'Would a front-line employee understand why this function exists from this sentence?' If not, simplify.
3. Define the Vision as 3–5 specific, measurable attributes of success 3 years from now. Each attribute should connect to the maturity model's target state. Avoid abstract aspirations ('best-in-class risk culture') without operationalizing them.
4. Select 4–6 Values that are genuinely differentiating — values that describe how this function behaves differently from a compliance-only function. 'Integrity' is not a differentiating value for a risk function; 'Enabling smart risk-taking' is.
5. Validate the Mission/Vision/Values with the CEO and CRO before publishing. If the CEO cannot recall one of the values after reading the document, simplify.
6. Publish in the Risk Management Policy, the Risk Team onboarding pack, and display in the risk function's workspace. Reference the values explicitly in risk committee meetings when a behavioral issue arises.

## Real-life example — Microsoft Corporation
Microsoft's Enterprise Risk Management function operates with the mission 'to enable Microsoft to achieve its mission to empower every person and every organization on the planet to achieve more — by ensuring that risks are understood, managed, and used as a source of competitive insight.' The 'used as a source of competitive insight' clause is the differentiating element: it positions risk management as a strategic intelligence function, not a control function. This framing was credited by Microsoft's former CRO as the primary reason business units actively engaged with the risk function rather than treating it as a compliance obligation.

**So what:** Microsoft's risk function mission reframed 'risk management' as 'competitive intelligence' — a positioning that made the function attractive to business units rather than threatening. The mission statement was a deliberate internal marketing decision that changed how the function was perceived and used.

## Template
Complete this template in a 2-hour workshop with the risk team and senior business leaders. Validate with CEO and CRO. Publish in the Risk Management Policy.

- [ ] Mission (1 sentence: why does the risk function exist? Connect to business objectives, not compliance)
- [ ] Vision — 3 years from now, list 3–5 specific measurable attributes of success
- [ ] Values — list 4–6 behavioral principles; for each: value name (2–3 words) + behavioral description (1–2 sentences describing what this looks like in practice)
- [ ] Alignment check: for each value, cite one specific example of behavior that would demonstrate this value in a real situation the risk team faces
- [ ] Board approval (Y/N) and date
- [ ] Publication channels (Risk Management Policy / Team onboarding / Risk Committee charter / Website)

## Pitfalls
- Generic values that apply to every function in every organization — 'Integrity, Excellence, Innovation' describe nothing specific about a risk function. Counter: test each value with 'Would the IT department, the marketing department, and the finance department also claim this value?' If yes for all three, the value is not differentiating.
- Mission that is internally focused rather than business-focused — 'To build a world-class risk management function' is not a mission; it is a self-improvement goal. Counter: always connect the mission to the organization's strategic objectives and the value delivered to the business.

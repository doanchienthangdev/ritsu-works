# Risk Management Capability Maturity Model

*Category: diagnosis · Toolkit: Risk Management*

## What it is
A structured assessment framework that evaluates an organization's risk management capability across 5 domains (Governance, Process, People, Systems, Culture) on a 1–5 maturity scale, producing a radar chart baseline and a prioritized improvement roadmap.

**Origin:** Adapted from the Software Engineering Institute's Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI, 1991) and subsequently applied to enterprise risk management by major consulting firms (McKinsey, Deloitte, PwC) and regulators. The 5-domain risk-specific adaptation is widely used in ISO 31000 implementation projects.

## Why it works
Risk management effectiveness is not binary — organizations do not simply 'have' or 'not have' risk management. Capability matures along a predictable progression: from ad-hoc individual responses (Level 1) through defined processes (Level 2), embedded practices (Level 3), managed with metrics (Level 4), to continuous optimization (Level 5). The maturity model makes the current state visible, creates a shared language for improvement, and allows benchmarking against industry peers. The 5-domain structure ensures the assessment covers the full system: governance (decision rights), process (the risk cycle), people (skills and accountability), systems (tools and data), and culture (the behavioral norms that determine whether the process is actually followed).

## When to use
At the start of any risk management improvement program (Phase 1, Step 3) to baseline current capability. Repeat annually. Also use when a new CRO wants to assess the function they inherited, or when a regulator or Board requests evidence of risk management maturity.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the 5 assessment domains and the 5 maturity levels with organization-specific descriptors (adapt the generic 1–5 scale to your sector's language).
2. Design a 20–30 question survey covering 4–6 questions per domain. Use behavioral anchors: 'Rarely / Sometimes / Usually / Always / Best-in-class' rather than numerical self-assessment, to reduce optimism bias.
3. Administer the survey to 15–30 stakeholders across all organizational levels: Board member, CRO, business unit leaders, operational managers, front-line staff. The distribution across levels is critical — senior leaders systematically overstate maturity.
4. Calculate the average score per domain. Plot the current-state polygon on the radar chart.
5. Identify the 2–3 domains with the largest gap between current and industry benchmark (or target state). These are the priority improvement areas.
6. For each priority domain, identify the specific capability gap (e.g., 'Process = Level 2 because we have no documented risk identification procedure') and a time-bound improvement action.
7. Set the target state (typically 12–24 months) for each domain based on strategic ambition and resource constraints. Plot the target polygon on the same radar chart.
8. Re-run the assessment annually to track progress. The radar chart trend across multiple years is the most compelling evidence of risk function improvement for the Board.

## Real-life example — National Australia Bank (NAB)
Following a series of operational risk incidents (foreign exchange trading losses in 2004, and subsequent regulatory scrutiny), NAB deployed a Risk Management Capability Maturity Model assessment across its retail banking, institutional banking, and wealth management divisions. The baseline assessment revealed Culture at Level 1 ('risk incidents are blamed, not investigated') and Systems at Level 2 ('risk data in spreadsheets with no aggregation capability'). NAB's Board prioritized Culture as the #1 improvement area, commissioning a three-year culture change program anchored on the principle 'no risk owner is penalized for escalating a risk.' The maturity model was re-run annually, and by Year 3 Culture had reached Level 3 — with measurable increase in near-miss reporting (a leading indicator of risk culture).

**So what:** Culture is consistently the hardest domain to mature and the most consequential — organizations with high Process and Systems maturity but low Culture maturity see their risk frameworks bypassed in practice. The radar chart makes this visible to the Board.

## Pitfalls
- Self-assessment by senior leaders only — leaders systematically overstate maturity by 0.5–1.5 levels. Counter: always include front-line staff in the survey; the gap between senior and front-line scores is itself a finding.
- Using maturity improvement as the KPI rather than risk outcomes — a Level 4 maturity organization can still have poor risk outcomes if the process is bureaucratic. Counter: always pair maturity metrics with outcome metrics (residual risk trend, issue recurrence rate).
- One-time assessment rather than annual tracking — the value of the model is the trend, not the snapshot. Counter: commit to annual re-assessment before the first one is complete.

# Active Listening — RASA Model

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A four-step active listening protocol — Receive, Appreciate, Summarise, Ask — that transforms passive hearing into engaged, empathic listening and signals genuine attention to the speaker.

**Origin:** The RASA acronym was developed by Julian Treasure, author and acoustic consultant, and introduced in his widely-viewed 2012 TED Talk 'How to speak so that people want to listen.' The underlying active listening concept draws from Carl Rogers' person-centred therapy (1940s-50s) and Thomas Gordon's Teacher Effectiveness Training (1974).

## Why it works
Most people listen at 25% efficiency — hearing words but not meaning. The most common listening failure is formulating a response while the other person is still speaking. RASA structures listening as a four-stage protocol that keeps the listener's attention on the speaker rather than on their own internal monologue. The 'Appreciate' step (acknowledging, not agreeing) removes the pressure to evaluate, enabling genuinely receptive listening.

## When to use
In every 1:1 meeting, coaching conversation, and feedback session. Critical in any high-stakes conversation where the other person's perspective matters: performance conversations, stakeholder meetings, customer interviews, conflict resolution.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Set up the physical environment for listening: phone on silent, face-down. If you are at a desk, turn away from your screen. If possible, sit at the same level as the speaker rather than behind a desk.
2. When the person begins speaking, focus entirely on them. Your single goal is to understand what they are saying and what they mean — not to prepare your response.
3. Use Appreciate cues continuously: a small nod, 'I understand', 'Go on', 'Mmm' — these keep the speaker talking and signal engagement. Do not confuse appreciation with agreement.
4. When the person pauses or completes a point, wait 3 seconds before responding. This pause has two purposes: (a) it signals you are genuinely processing what was said, not just waiting to speak; (b) it often prompts the speaker to add something important they had held back.
5. Summarise before responding: 'So what I'm hearing is [paraphrase of key points]. Is that right?' This allows the speaker to correct any misunderstanding before you respond to a point they didn't actually make.
6. Ask one genuine question that opens the conversation further: 'What is making that particularly difficult for you?' or 'What have you already tried?' Avoid multiple questions at once — one question is more powerful than three.

## Real-life example — Pixar Animation Studios — Ed Catmull's leadership listening practice (documented in 'Creativity, Inc.', 2014)
Ed Catmull, Pixar co-founder and president, documents in 'Creativity, Inc.' how the cultural health of Pixar depended on senior leaders genuinely listening to every level of the organisation. In one specific practice, Catmull institutionalised 'notes sessions' after every rough-cut screening — where he would listen to every team member's feedback using structured active listening before any leader spoke. The rule: leaders could not respond to a note until they had summarised and confirmed they understood it. This practice surfaced creative problems months earlier than traditional review processes, because junior animators felt safe enough to speak.

**So what:** Active listening is a leadership performance multiplier: the leader who listens receives information the non-listener never gets, builds trust that generates loyalty, and surfaces problems before they become crises.

## Pitfalls
- Listening to respond rather than to understand: The most common listening failure. If you find yourself formulating your answer while the speaker is mid-sentence, you have stopped listening. Counter: commit to writing down your response after the speaker finishes rather than preparing it in your head.
- Confusing appreciation cues with agreement: Nodding and saying 'I see' are acknowledgements of reception, not agreement. Speakers sometimes interpret these as agreement. Counter: after the Summarise step, make your position clear: 'I hear what you're saying — and my view is different.'
- Skipping the pause: Most leaders move directly from hearing to speaking with no pause. The 3-second pause is where the quality of listening becomes visible to the speaker. Practice it deliberately — it will feel awkward at first.

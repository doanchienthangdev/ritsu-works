# Executive Presence Model

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A multi-dimensional framework describing the behaviours, physical attributes, and communication habits that signal credibility, authority, and trustworthiness to senior audiences — enabling leaders to project confidence and inspire confidence in others.

**Origin:** The concept of executive presence has been studied and codified by leadership development researchers including Sylvia Ann Hewlett (Center for Talent Innovation, 2012 research, 'Executive Presence: The Missing Link Between Merit and Success', 2014) and various management consulting firms. The three-pillar model (Gravitas, Communication, Appearance) emerged from Hewlett's survey of 4,000 senior executives.

## Why it works
Leaders are evaluated not only on what they decide but on how they make others feel when they are in the room. Executive presence is the package of signals — physical, vocal, and behavioural — that communicates 'This person belongs in the room and knows what they are doing.' Research shows that promotion decisions for leadership roles are influenced significantly by perceived presence, sometimes more than by technical performance. Presence can be learned and deliberately practised.

## When to use
When preparing for a promotion or step-change role. When receiving feedback that 'gravitas' or 'presence' is a gap. Before any high-stakes presentation, board meeting, or client pitch. For leaders who are technically excellent but not advancing due to perception gaps.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Assess your current presence baseline: record yourself in a high-stakes presentation or meeting (video). Watch it once for content, once for non-verbal presence. Evaluate: eye contact frequency, speech pace (target 130-150 WPM), use of filler words ('um', 'uh', 'like'), posture, gestures.
2. Build physical grounding: before any high-stakes meeting, take 2 minutes to ground yourself — stand with feet shoulder-width apart, breathe slowly and deeply (4 counts in, hold 4, 4 out), roll your shoulders back and down, lift your chin to horizontal. This physical posture change directly affects cortisol and testosterone levels (Amy Cuddy, Harvard, 2010).
3. Master verbal economy: identify your top-3 filler words from your recording. In the next 2 weeks, whenever you feel the impulse to use a filler word, substitute a pause (silence). Pauses read as authority; fillers read as uncertainty.
4. Practice deliberate eye contact: in presentations, identify 3-5 individuals in different parts of the room. Hold eye contact with each for one complete thought (2-3 seconds) before moving to the next. Avoid the 'lighthouse sweep' — panning continuously without landing.
5. Develop vocal variety: vary your pace (slow for emphasis), pitch (lower for authority), and volume (controlled dynamics, not flat monotone). Record and listen: identify your natural vocal default and practise expanding your range.
6. Dress as a conscious signal: research your audience's dress code before any high-stakes presentation. Dress one level above the audience as a presence signal. Ensure your physical presence is not a distraction (no distracting accessories, wrinkled clothing, or unkempt presentation).

## Real-life example — PepsiCo — Indra Nooyi's deliberate presence cultivation (documented in 'My Life in Full', 2021)
Indra Nooyi, who became CEO of PepsiCo in 2006, describes in her memoir the deliberate work she invested in developing executive presence as a woman of colour in a predominantly white male corporate culture. She worked with a speech coach to modulate her speaking pace and accent; she studied and adopted the dress norms of PepsiCo's board (conservative, authoritative); she practised deliberate pausing before answering difficult questions in board meetings to project thoughtfulness rather than reactivity. She also invested heavily in gravitas — becoming known for a 26-year strategic vision that she communicated consistently. The result: she was rated by her peers as having among the highest executive presence of any Fortune 100 CEO.

**So what:** Executive presence is a skill, not a personality trait. It can be observed, analysed, practised, and improved. The leaders who invest in it deliberately are those who break through the last mile of promotion barriers.

## Pitfalls
- Presence as performance: Faking executive presence is detectable and counterproductive. The goal is to align your genuine confidence and authority with how you present physically — not to perform a character. Start with building real confidence (competence, preparation) before addressing surface signals.
- Over-focusing on appearance: Hewlett's research shows appearance accounts for only 5% of the executive presence assessment. Many leaders over-invest in clothing and grooming while under-investing in gravitas (decision-making quality, emotional intelligence) and communication (clarity, authority).
- Neglecting the audience read: Presence is relational — it depends on reading the room correctly. A style that projects authority in a board meeting will project arrogance in a startup team stand-up. Context-appropriate presence is the goal.

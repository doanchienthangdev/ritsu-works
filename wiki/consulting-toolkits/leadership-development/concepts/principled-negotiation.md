# Principled Negotiation (Getting to Yes Framework)

*Category: negotiation · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A four-principle negotiation method that separates people from problems, focuses on underlying interests rather than stated positions, generates options for mutual gain, and anchors agreements on objective criteria.

**Origin:** Developed by Roger Fisher and William Ury at the Harvard Negotiation Project and published as 'Getting to Yes' in 1981 (updated with Bruce Patton, 1991). It is the most widely taught negotiation framework in business schools and consulting firms globally.

## Why it works
Most negotiations fail because parties argue over positions (what they say they want) rather than interests (why they want it). Two people disputing over the last orange might both agree to cut it in half — when in reality one wants the juice and the other wants the peel. By revealing underlying interests, principled negotiation creates value that positional bargaining leaves on the table, and by using objective criteria, it removes personal ego from the outcome.

## When to use
For any negotiation where the relationship matters and where there is potential for mutual gain — salary negotiations, supplier contracts, partnership terms, internal resource negotiations. Less appropriate for pure zero-sum, one-shot transactions where the relationship is irrelevant.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Before the negotiation, complete the BATNA worksheet: write your best alternative if no agreement is reached. This is your real walk-away point — not your stated opening position.
2. At the start of the negotiation, invest 10–15 minutes in rapport and framing before discussing substance. State the ground rules: 'I'd like this to be a productive conversation where we both walk away with a good outcome.'
3. When the other party states a position, respond with curiosity not counter-position: 'Help me understand — why is that important to you?' Use 'why' questions until you uncover the underlying interest.
4. Once both parties have shared their underlying interests, shift to option generation: 'Let me put aside what each of us has said we want and brainstorm what solutions might work for both of us.' Generate at least 5 options before evaluating any.
5. Evaluate options using objective criteria: industry benchmarks, precedent deals, third-party assessments, legal standards. Frame as 'The market rate for this service is X — can we anchor to that?'
6. Use the 'if-then' trade structure to move to agreement: 'If you can extend the payment terms to 90 days, then I can agree to the volume discount you've requested.'
7. Close by summarising every agreed point out loud and confirming explicitly: 'So we've agreed: [point 1], [point 2], [point 3]. Is that your understanding?' Follow up with written summary within 24 hours.

## Real-life example — Camp David Accords — Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty (1978)
The Camp David Accords are often cited as the textbook example of principled negotiation. Egypt's stated position: return of the Sinai Peninsula (sovereignty). Israel's stated position: retention of the Sinai (security). These positions were irreconcilable. Jimmy Carter's mediation team applied principled negotiation: Egypt's underlying interest was national sovereignty (not the land per se); Israel's underlying interest was security (not the land per se). The solution: Egypt regained full sovereignty (addressing their interest); Israel received a demilitarised Sinai with international monitoring (addressing their interest). Both parties' positions were 'defeated' but both underlying interests were satisfied.

**So what:** Positions are what you say you want; interests are why you want it. The distance between two opposing positions is often much smaller than it appears when you focus on underlying interests.

## Template
Complete this worksheet before any significant negotiation. Review it again immediately before entering the room.

- [ ] My stated opening position: _________
- [ ] My underlying interest (why I want this — the real need): _________
- [ ] My BATNA (best alternative if no deal): _________
- [ ] My walk-away point (minimum acceptable outcome): _________
- [ ] Their likely stated position: _________
- [ ] Their likely underlying interest (why they want it): _________
- [ ] Their likely BATNA: _________
- [ ] Areas where we value things differently (value-creation zones): _________
- [ ] Objective criteria I will use to anchor the deal: _________
- [ ] 5 potential options for mutual gain (generated without evaluation): 1. __ 2. __ 3. __ 4. __ 5. __
- [ ] My if-then trade offers: If they do [X], I can offer [Y]. If they do [A], I can offer [B].

## Pitfalls
- Confusing your BATNA with your opening position: Your BATNA is what you do if no deal is reached — it is not what you open with. A strong BATNA gives you negotiating power; a weak one (no real alternative) weakens your position and should prompt you to improve your BATNA before entering the room.
- Option generation in the room: Generating options and evaluating them simultaneously kills creativity. Separate the brainstorm from the evaluation — 'let's generate 5 options before we judge any of them.'
- Neglecting the relationship: Principled negotiation treats people and problems separately, but in business, the relationship often matters more than the deal. A 'win' that damages the relationship may be a long-term loss. Always ask: 'Would I be comfortable if this counterpart saw exactly what I'm doing and why?'

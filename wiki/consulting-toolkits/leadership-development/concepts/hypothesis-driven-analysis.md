# Hypothesis-Driven Analysis

*Category: problem-solving · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
An analytical approach in which the analyst begins with a testable hypothesis (a directional prediction about what the answer will be) and uses data to confirm or refute it — rather than collecting all available data and then synthesising a conclusion.

**Origin:** Hypothesis-driven analysis is the foundational methodology of management consulting, taught universally at McKinsey, BCG, and Bain. It was described in 'The McKinsey Way' (Ethan Rasiel, 1999) and 'The McKinsey Engagement' (Paul Friga, 2009). The underlying scientific method (Karl Popper's falsificationism, 1934) provides the philosophical basis.

## Why it works
Business data is infinite; time is finite. Without a hypothesis to test, analysts collect all available data and then — typically near a deadline — attempt to find patterns in it. This 'boil the ocean' approach is slow, expensive, and produces confusing outputs. By contrast, forming a hypothesis first (even a rough one) directs data collection to exactly the evidence needed to confirm or refute it, cutting analysis time by 60-80% while improving the quality of insights.

## When to use
In every analytical project, from simple business case to complex strategic analysis. Any time you have more potential analyses to run than time available. The more time-constrained the project, the more important the hypothesis-driven approach.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. After building and prioritising the issue tree, assign a hypothesis to each priority branch: 'I believe [this branch resolves] in the direction of [X] because [initial evidence or reasoning].' The hypothesis does not need to be correct — it just needs to be testable.
2. For each hypothesis, identify the single most decisive piece of evidence that would confirm or refute it. Ask: 'If I could only collect one piece of data for this hypothesis, what would it be?' This single-data-point discipline focuses the analysis.
3. Write the 'so-what if confirmed' and 'so-what if refuted' for each hypothesis before running the analysis. This ensures you know what you will do with each possible finding before you invest analysis effort.
4. Run the analysis. When the data arrives: if it confirms the hypothesis, write the so-what and add it to the Pyramid. If it refutes the hypothesis, update the issue tree (the branch is resolved as 'not a root cause') and redirect effort to the next priority branch.
5. Avoid the temptation to re-hypothesise until the data fits. If the data clearly refutes the hypothesis, accept the refutation and move on. The consulting value is in being right about the answer, not in defending the hypothesis.
6. At the end of the analysis phase, every priority branch in the issue tree should be resolved as either 'confirmed root cause' or 'not a root cause.' The synthesis step then assembles the confirmed branches into the Pyramid Principle recommendation.

## Real-life example — McKinsey & Company — pharmaceutical market analysis
In a market entry assessment for a pharmaceutical company, the team formed a Day-1 hypothesis: 'The market opportunity in Latin America is primarily in the private (out-of-pocket) segment, not government procurement, because government procurement cycles are 2+ years and the client's product patent window is 5 years.' This hypothesis directed data collection to three specific pieces of evidence: the size of the private segment (confirmed: 40% of market by value), the typical government procurement timeline (confirmed: 18-24 months), and the competitive intensity in the private segment (refuted: three competitors already well-established). The refutation of the third point modified the recommendation from 'enter private segment' to 'enter private segment via acquisition of [specific distributor] rather than organic.' Total analysis time: 4 days. Without the hypothesis, the team estimated it would have taken 3 weeks to reach the same conclusion.

**So what:** Hypothesis-driven analysis is the single most time-saving practice in management consulting. The hypothesis acts as a filter: it determines which data to collect and which to ignore. An analyst without a hypothesis has no filter.

## Pitfalls
- Hypothesis confirmation bias: Forming a hypothesis and then unconsciously seeking only confirming data. Counter: explicitly name the piece of data that would refute the hypothesis before running the analysis. Seek the refuting evidence first.
- Treating the hypothesis as a commitment: The hypothesis is a starting point, not a bet. Refusing to revise a hypothesis when the evidence refutes it is the most common analytical failure — and in consulting, the one most likely to lead to a wrong recommendation.
- Forming hypotheses too early (before issue tree): Jumping to hypotheses before building the MECE issue tree risks missing entire categories of root cause. Build the issue tree first; hypotheses are assigned to branches of the tree, not formed in place of it.

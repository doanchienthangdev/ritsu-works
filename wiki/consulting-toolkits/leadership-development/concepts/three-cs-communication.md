# The 3 Cs of Engaging Communication

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A three-criterion quality framework for any spoken segment of a presentation or conversation: Clear (one idea, plain language), Concise (under 30 seconds per point), and Compelling (opens with a hook that creates attention before delivering the message).

**Origin:** The 3 Cs framework is a synthesis of communication best practices from management consulting and public speaking, codified in various leadership development programmes. The underlying principles draw from George Orwell's 'Politics and the English Language' (1946) on plain language; research on human attention span in presentations; and rhetorical tradition (Aristotle's concepts of logos, ethos, pathos) on persuasion.

## Why it works
Most business presentations fail at one of three levels: they communicate too many ideas at once (breaking the 'one idea per unit' principle of clarity), they use too many words to make each point (cognitive overload), or they fail to create attention before delivering the content (the audience is not ready to receive the message). The 3 Cs is a self-editing and rehearsal tool that corrects all three failures simultaneously.

## When to use
For any significant presentation, client briefing, board update, or stakeholder communication. Also apply to important written communications — emails to senior stakeholders benefit from the same 3C discipline.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Write your intended talking points for a presentation or key conversation. For each point, apply the Clear test: can you state this in one complete sentence? If you need two sentences to state the core idea, you have two points — choose one.
2. Read each talking point aloud and time it. Any point that takes more than 30 seconds to explain needs editing. Cut: remove qualifications that don't change the core meaning, remove backstory unless it is essential, and remove the obvious.
3. Write a hook for each key message. Options: (a) Surprising statistic: 'Eighty percent of our customers churn before year two — but the ones who don't are worth 10x more.' (b) Brief story: 'Last month, one of our engineers missed a critical bug because they were too afraid to speak up.' (c) Provocative question: 'What if I told you our largest growth opportunity is the one we've explicitly decided not to pursue?'
4. Rehearse the entire communication with a timer. Adjust where you run over 30 seconds on any single point. The goal is not to speak for the minimum time — it is to make every second count.
5. Test with a practice audience: ask them to write down the one key message from each segment after you finish. If their notes don't match your intended key messages, something failed the Clear test.

## Real-life example — Apple — Steve Jobs' product launch presentations (1998-2011)
Steve Jobs' product launch presentations are studied as the gold standard of the 3 Cs. His introductions consistently used the three-criterion structure: Clear ('There is no second message in a Jobs keynote'), Concise (the famous 'One more thing' slide containing a single idea), and Compelling ('Today, Apple is going to reinvent the phone' — a hook that created immediate attention before a single feature was mentioned). His teams rehearsed each segment until it could be delivered in under 30 seconds and still be clearly understood. The result: product launches that generated global media coverage without a single press release — the communication did the marketing work.

**So what:** The 3 Cs discipline is not about dumbing down complex ideas — it is about respecting the audience's cognitive load. The clearer, more concise, and more compelling the communication, the more sophisticated the audience will perceive the speaker to be.

## Pitfalls
- Starting with context instead of the hook: Many presenters spend the first 5 minutes on 'background and context' before reaching the point. This is backwards — the hook goes first, the context (if needed at all) comes after the audience is engaged.
- Cutting content without cutting meaning: Conciseness requires cutting words, not cutting thinking. Identify which words carry meaning and which carry qualification or habit. 'It is somewhat possible that we might consider...' can be reduced to 'We could...' with no loss of meaning.
- Using the same hook type every time: Opening every presentation with a statistic becomes predictable and loses its attention-grabbing power. Rotate: story one time, question the next, counter-intuitive claim the third.

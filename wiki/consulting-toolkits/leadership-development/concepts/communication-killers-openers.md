# Communication Killers and Openers

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A two-part taxonomy of verbal behaviours that either shut down productive conversation (Killers) or encourage openness, trust, and continued dialogue (Openers) — giving leaders a practical vocabulary audit tool for transforming their real-time communication patterns.

**Origin:** The concept draws from Carl Rogers' person-centred communication research (1951) on the barriers to therapeutic communication, Thomas Gordon's work on 'roadblocks to communication' in Parent Effectiveness Training (1970), and management consulting communication training programmes. The specific 'killers and openers' framing is used in McKinsey and BCG leadership development curricula.

## Why it works
Communication breaks down not because of bad intent but because of habitual verbal patterns that the speaker finds natural but the listener finds deflating or threatening. The seven communication killers (interrupting, moralising, advising prematurely, minimising, distracting, comparing, interrogating) all share a common flaw: they make the conversation about the listener's response rather than the speaker's experience. Openers do the opposite — they signal genuine interest in the speaker's experience without evaluating it.

## When to use
In any 1:1 conversation where the other person is sharing a challenge, concern, or significant piece of information. In performance feedback conversations, coaching sessions, and any situation where genuine understanding is more important than rapid response. As the first intervention when team psychological safety is low.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Run a personal communication audit: over the next week, keep a tally in a notebook. Every time you notice yourself using one of the 7 killers in a conversation, make a tick. At the end of the week, identify your top-3 most frequent killers.
2. For each of your top-3 killers, identify the trigger: when do you most often use it? (E.g., 'I interrupt most often when I think I already know the answer.') Understanding the trigger allows you to build an anticipatory counter.
3. For each killer, practise the corresponding opener as a replacement. If your killer is 'advising prematurely', practise inserting 'Tell me more about what you've already tried' before any advice. If your killer is 'minimising', practise 'That sounds genuinely difficult — how are you holding up?'
4. Use the self-monitoring card technique: for one week, carry a card with your top-3 killers written on it. Before any significant conversation, glance at the card as a priming cue. The physical act of reading the killers before the conversation reduces their frequency by 40-60% in most training participants.
5. Practice in low-stakes conversations first: the morning coffee chat, the informal corridor conversation. Build the opener habit in low-stakes contexts before deploying it in high-stakes performance or coaching conversations.
6. Model the change publicly: in a team meeting, acknowledge one instance where you used a killer: 'I just interrupted you — I'm sorry. Please continue.' This signals to the team that communication quality is a shared responsibility and builds psychological safety.

## Real-life example — Google — Project Oxygen (2008-2013, People Operations research)
Google's Project Oxygen identified 10 key behaviours of highly effective managers at Google. Behaviour #4 was 'Is a good communicator who listens and shares information' and Behaviour #5 was 'Supports career development and discusses performance.' In analysing what differentiated high-scoring managers on these behaviours, Google's People Analytics team found that the single most consistent differentiator was not the managers' advice quality but their listening quality. High-scoring managers consistently used communication openers (paraphrasing, validation, curiosity) and avoided killers (premature advice, minimising). Low-scoring managers' 1:1 conversations typically included at least 2-3 killers per session, which correlated strongly with employee disengagement scores.

**So what:** The most impactful communication skill for a manager is not eloquence — it is elimination of the killers. The first step to becoming a better communicator is to become a better listener, which means removing the verbal habits that prevent genuine listening.

## Pitfalls
- Openers that are not genuine: Using opener language while mentally still in killer mode ('Tell me more' said with visible impatience or in a rushed tone) is worse than saying nothing. The opener must be genuine — actually intending to hear the answer.
- Eliminating all advice-giving: Premature advice is a killer; timely, invited advice is a legitimate management behaviour. The distinction is sequence: first understand fully (openers), then advise if appropriate. 'What have you already tried?' before 'Here is what I would do.'
- Self-monitoring as performance: Leaders who are performing the killers audit (consciously monitoring every word) can come across as stilted or unnatural. The goal is to habituate the openers through practice until they are automatic, not to maintain conscious vigilance in every conversation.

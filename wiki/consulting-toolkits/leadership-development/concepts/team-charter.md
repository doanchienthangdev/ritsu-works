# One-Page Team Charter

*Category: team-management · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A single-page document that codifies a team's collective agreements on mission, vision, values (with behavioural definitions), goals, roles, and ways of working — creating a shared operating contract that every member has helped create and explicitly endorsed.

**Origin:** Team charters draw from project management (PMI), agile team practices (Scrum team agreements), and organisational development literature. The 'one-page' format was popularised by management consulting firms as a practical tool for quickly aligning new teams. Patrick Lencioni's work on team clarity (The Five Dysfunctions, 2002) provides the theoretical underpinning.

## Why it works
Most team dysfunction traces to one of two root causes: ambiguity (no one knows the direction, roles, or expectations) or misalignment (people have different implicit assumptions about how the team operates). A co-created team charter addresses both: the process of creating it surfaces disagreements before they become conflicts, and the document provides an external reference point that depersonalises future disagreements ('Let's check our charter' rather than 'I disagree with you').

## When to use
When forming a new team. When an existing team is experiencing alignment problems or trust issues. At the start of a major new project or significant business challenge. When onboarding new team members (the charter is the first document they receive).

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Schedule a 2-hour team workshop. Pre-work: ask each member to write independently — 'Why does this team exist?' and 'What would the ideal version of this team look like in 3 years?' The pre-work prevents groupthink in the session.
2. Session Part 1 — Mission and Vision (40 min): Each member shares their pre-work. Cluster responses by theme on a whiteboard. Identify the 2-3 strongest themes. Draft a mission sentence together ('We exist to [do what] for [whom]'). Draft a vision statement together. Iterate until the group agrees.
3. Session Part 2 — Values (30 min): Ask each member to write their top 3 values for how this team should work. Cluster. Shortlist to 3-5 shared values. Critically: for each value, agree on a behavioural definition ('Integrity means we tell each other the hard truth in private and present a united front in public'). A value without a behavioural definition is a wall decoration.
4. Session Part 3 — Ways of Working (20 min): List 5-7 specific behavioural agreements. Examples: 'We start meetings on time and end on time.' 'We challenge each other's ideas in meetings and support each other's decisions outside.' 'We respond to messages within 4 hours during work hours.'
5. Post-session: Design lead consolidates the charter into a single A3 page. Share with team for final review. Incorporate edits. Hold a 15-minute sign-off session where each member signs the charter physically or digitally.
6. Maintenance: Review the charter quarterly. At the start of each quarter, ask: 'Are we living our values? Are the ways of working still relevant?' Update where needed.

## Real-life example — New Zealand All Blacks rugby team — 'Better People Make Better All Blacks' (2004-2015)
When Graham Henry took over as All Blacks coach in 2004, one of his first acts was to co-create a team charter with the players — not hand it down to them. The charter included explicit values (Humility, Excellence, Responsibility) with behavioural definitions. Most notably, the 'Sweep the Sheds' norm — senior players clean the dressing room after every game, regardless of status — became a defining cultural marker. The charter was reviewed and refreshed at the start of every major tournament. The All Blacks won the Rugby World Cup in 2011 and 2015 under this charter, becoming the highest-winning percentage team in any major team sport.

**So what:** The process of building the charter matters as much as the document. When high-status players sweep the sheds, it signals that the behavioural norms apply to everyone — which is the only way a team charter creates a real culture rather than a PR document.

## Template
Complete this template in a 2-hour team workshop. Every section must be co-created, not handed down. Sign and display when complete.

- [ ] Team Name: _________ | Date Created: _________ | Review Date: _________
- [ ] MISSION: We exist to [action] for [beneficiary] so that [outcome].
- [ ] VISION (3 years): In 3 years, our team will be known for _________ and will have achieved _________.
- [ ] VALUES (3-5): Value 1: _________ | Behavioural definition: _________

## Pitfalls
- Manager writes charter and presents it to the team: This produces compliance, not commitment. A charter the team co-creates is a living agreement; a charter handed down is a policy document. Always co-create.
- Values without behavioural definitions: 'Integrity', 'Respect', 'Excellence' mean different things to different people. Without a behavioural definition, the value cannot be used to adjudicate a real situation. Define what each value looks like in a typical Monday morning.
- Charter created and never revisited: The charter is only valuable if it is referenced in real decisions. Build in a quarterly charter review and use it in feedback conversations: 'The way this was handled — does it align with our value of [X]?'

# SBI Feedback Model (Situation-Behaviour-Impact)

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A three-part feedback structure that describes the specific Situation in which a behaviour occurred, the observable Behaviour itself (what the person did or said, not an interpretation), and the concrete Impact that behaviour had — enabling feedback that is specific, objective, and non-threatening.

**Origin:** Developed by the Center for Creative Leadership (CCL) in the 1980s as part of their leadership development research. The model emerged from research into why feedback so often fails: recipients become defensive when feedback is about their personality or character rather than observable behaviour. SBI shifts the conversation to facts, not judgements.

## Why it works
Feedback that attacks personality ('You are dismissive') triggers defensiveness and shuts down learning. Feedback that describes observable behaviour and its concrete impact ('In the Monday meeting, when you interrupted Sarah three times, it caused the team to stop contributing') gives the recipient something specific to act on without attacking their identity. The Impact element is particularly important: it connects the behaviour to consequences, making the feedback meaningful rather than just critical.

## When to use
For any developmental or positive feedback conversation with a direct report, peer, or manager. Use for positive reinforcement as well as developmental feedback — 'In [Situation], when you [Behaviour], the Impact was [positive outcome]' is equally powerful for reinforcing good behaviour.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Choose the right moment: give feedback within 48 hours of the observed behaviour, when both parties are calm and there is time for a proper conversation. Never give developmental feedback in public or in the heat of the moment.
2. Draft the SBI statement in writing before delivering it. This forces precision: write the Situation (where, when — specific), the Behaviour (observable only — no judgement), and the Impact (concrete effect on team, relationship, or work).
3. Deliver the feedback using the SBI structure out loud: 'I'd like to share some feedback. In [Situation], when you [Behaviour], I observed/felt [Impact].'
4. Stop after the Impact statement and invite the recipient's perspective: 'How does that land with you?' or 'What is your experience of that situation?' Listen with RASA (Receive, Appreciate, Summarise, Ask). Do not defend the feedback — just listen.
5. Agree a forward-looking action if appropriate: 'What do you think you might do differently in a similar situation?' This shifts from judgement to development and gives the recipient agency.
6. For receiving feedback using ANNA: Acknowledge (nod, thank them for the feedback), No defend (don't justify immediately — pause), Note it (write it down), Act on it (follow up on any agreed action within 1 week).

## Real-life example — Netflix — 'Radical Candour' feedback culture documented in Patty McCord's 'Powerful' (2018)
Netflix's 'Keeper Test' and feedback culture require managers to give SBI-quality feedback continuously, not annually. In one documented example, a senior engineer's repeated pattern of dismissing colleagues' ideas in architecture reviews was causing talent retention issues. The SBI feedback: S — 'In the last three architecture reviews.' B — 'You cut off each presenter before they finished explaining their design rationale, then reframed their idea as a problem.' I — 'Two engineers told me they feel their contributions aren't valued, and one has started job-searching.' The engineer responded not with defensiveness but with a genuine question: 'I didn't know I was doing that. Is there a specific moment I should watch for?' — a textbook ANNA response that led to behavioural change.

**So what:** The behaviour description must be so specific and observable that the recipient cannot argue with the facts — only with the interpretation. The more observable and specific the B, the less defensive the response.

## Template
Draft the SBI statement in writing before every developmental feedback conversation. Use the fields below. Deliver the statement in one breath, then pause.

- [ ] Recipient: _________ | Date of conversation: _________ | Date of observed behaviour: _________
- [ ] S — Situation: 'In [specific context: date, meeting, project, location]...'
- [ ] B — Behaviour: '...when you [specific, observable action or words — no interpretations]...'
- [ ] I — Impact: '...I observed/felt [concrete effect on me / team / work / relationship].'
- [ ] Opening question after delivery: 'How does that land with you?' or 'What is your experience of that?'
- [ ] Agreed forward action (if reached): _________
- [ ] Follow-up date to check in: _________

## Pitfalls
- Behaviour that is actually an interpretation: 'You seemed disengaged' is not a behaviour — it is an interpretation of body language. The observable behaviour might be 'You looked at your phone three times during the presentation.' Replace interpretations with observations.
- Skipping the Impact: Feedback without Impact sounds like a complaint or a criticism. The Impact is what makes the feedback meaningful — it connects the behaviour to consequences the recipient cares about.
- Giving feedback about personality: 'You are arrogant' is about character, not behaviour. Feedback about character produces defensiveness and shame. Feedback about behaviour ('In the Monday meeting, when you dismissed the junior team member's idea without engaging with it') produces learning.

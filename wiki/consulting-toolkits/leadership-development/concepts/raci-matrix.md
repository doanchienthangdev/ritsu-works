# RACI Matrix (Responsibility Assignment Matrix)

*Category: team-management · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A table that maps every key process, task, or decision to four roles — Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome), Consulted (input is required), and Informed (kept up to date) — eliminating role ambiguity and decision-making confusion.

**Origin:** The RACI concept emerged from project management practice in the 1950s-60s and was formalised in the 1970s by Eiji Ogawa and popularised through project management literature. It is now a standard tool in PMI (Project Management Institute) methodology and management consulting practice globally.

## Why it works
Ambiguity about who owns a decision or a deliverable is the single most common cause of project failure and team friction. RACI makes implicit agreements explicit: each cell in the matrix forces a conscious allocation of responsibility. The critical constraint is that every task must have exactly one Accountable party — two accountable parties means no one is truly accountable.

## When to use
When a new team is forming or a new project kicks off. When existing teams are experiencing role confusion, decision paralysis, or accountability gaps. When onboarding new team members who need to understand the team's operating model quickly.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List all key processes, decisions, and deliverables in the left-hand column. Aim for the 15-20 that matter most — avoid making the matrix too granular or it becomes unusable.
2. List all relevant roles (not people) across the top row. Use roles (e.g., 'Project Lead', 'Finance Manager') not names — the RACI defines the role, not the individual.
3. For each process/decision, assign R (Responsible), A (Accountable), C (Consulted), or I (Informed) to each role. Rules: (a) Every row must have at least one R and exactly one A. (b) If A and R are the same person, use 'R/A'. (c) Avoid giving too many people R on the same task — it diffuses responsibility.
4. Review the RACI with the team in a 60-minute working session. For each row, ask: 'Does everyone agree with who is Accountable? Does the Accountable person agree?' Flag any rows with conflict or confusion.
5. Identify and address RACI anti-patterns: (a) Multiple Accountables on one row — pick one. (b) 'R' assigned to everyone — diffuses responsibility. (c) Missing Consulteds on high-stakes decisions — risks stakeholder surprise.
6. Publish the finalised RACI in a shared location (team wiki, Confluence, SharePoint) and reference it in onboarding any new team member.

## Real-life example — Amazon — 'Single-Threaded Owner' model
Amazon's operational excellence is built partly on what Jeff Bezos calls the 'Single-Threaded Owner' (STO) model — every major initiative has exactly one person who owns it completely, is fully accountable, and has no other responsibilities. This is the 'A' in RACI taken to its logical extreme. When Amazon launched Amazon Prime, Jeff Wilke was the STO — he was the single Accountable party, cutting through the matrix of logistics, tech, and marketing accountability that would otherwise have fragmented ownership and slowed decision-making.

**So what:** The most important cell in any RACI is the 'A'. If two people claim the 'A' on the same row, there is no real accountability. Force the conversation: 'If this fails, whose performance review is it?'

## Pitfalls
- Too many Responsibles: When 4+ people are all marked 'R' on the same task, no individual feels accountable. Limit to 1-2 Responsibles per task; if more are needed, decompose the task.
- Forgetting to distinguish A from R: In many teams, the same person is both R (doing the work) and A (accountable for the outcome). This is fine — mark as 'R/A'. The problem arises when the Accountable party doesn't know they own the outcome because it was never made explicit.
- Building the RACI without the team: A RACI built by the manager and handed to the team breeds resentment and non-compliance. Always build it collaboratively — the conversation is as valuable as the document.

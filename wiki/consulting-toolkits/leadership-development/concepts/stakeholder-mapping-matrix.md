# Stakeholder Mapping Matrix (Power-Interest Grid)

*Category: stakeholder-management · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A 2x2 visual tool that plots every stakeholder along two axes — Power (their ability to influence or affect the outcome) and Interest (how much they care about the outcome) — producing four engagement strategies, one per quadrant.

**Origin:** The Power-Interest grid was developed by Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann in 'Making Strategy' (1998, SAGE Publications). It has since been adopted as a standard tool in project management (PMI PMBOK Guide), business analysis (BABOK), and management consulting stakeholder management frameworks globally.

## Why it works
Not all stakeholders deserve equal attention. The Power-Interest grid allocates engagement effort efficiently: high-power, high-interest stakeholders can make or break a project and must be actively managed; low-power, low-interest stakeholders waste both parties' time if over-engaged. The grid makes the implicit political landscape of any initiative visible and manageable.

## When to use
At the start of any project, initiative, or new role with significant stakeholder complexity. Also use when a project encounters unexpected resistance and you need to diagnose where it is coming from and how to address it.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Identify all stakeholders: brainstorm anyone who can influence, will be affected by, or has a material interest in your project or role. Cast wide — internal and external, formal and informal.
2. For each stakeholder, assess Power on a 1-5 scale: 1=no authority or influence, 5=can single-handedly approve, veto, or block. Assess Interest on a 1-5 scale: 1=unaware or indifferent, 5=highly engaged and invested in the outcome.
3. Plot each stakeholder on the 2x2 grid using their Power and Interest scores. Use initials or codes if confidential.
4. Assign the engagement strategy for each quadrant (Manage Closely, Keep Satisfied, Keep Informed, Monitor). For every 'Manage Closely' stakeholder, define: their key concerns, your desired relationship, and 3 specific actions.
5. Build a stakeholder engagement calendar: for 'Manage Closely', schedule monthly or bi-weekly touchpoints. For 'Keep Satisfied', schedule quarterly updates. Review and update the grid quarterly.
6. Watch for stakeholder migration: a 'Keep Informed' stakeholder who becomes a vocal critic can migrate to 'Manage Closely' overnight. Scan your grid after every major project event.

## Real-life example — Procter & Gamble — Tide Pods launch stakeholder management (2012)
When P&G launched Tide Pods in 2012, the product team mapped stakeholders across retail, regulatory, and media categories. The FDA and child safety advocacy groups were mapped as 'Manage Closely' (high power, high interest in product safety). Mass retailers (Walmart, Target) were 'Manage Closely' on the commercial side. Consumer media were 'Keep Informed.' The team's proactive engagement with child safety advocates in the design phase — installing child-resistant packaging and launching a safety communication campaign before retail launch — prevented what could have been a regulatory crisis from becoming one. Stakeholders who had been mapped as high-risk were converted into supporters.

**So what:** The Power-Interest grid is most valuable when used proactively: map stakeholders before they become problems, not after. A 'Manage Closely' stakeholder who feels ignored becomes a blocker; one who feels engaged becomes a sponsor.

## Pitfalls
- Static grid: Building the map once and never updating it. Stakeholder positions migrate constantly — a new CFO can change an entire quadrant. Build quarterly review of the grid into your calendar.
- Over-communicating to 'Monitor' stakeholders: Sending detailed updates to 'Monitor' quadrant stakeholders wastes their time and yours. Segment your communications — detailed briefings only for 'Manage Closely.'
- Focusing only on supporters: The most important stakeholders to map are often the potential blockers — those who could derail the project if not properly engaged. Actively seek out and address concerns of high-power sceptics before they mobilise.

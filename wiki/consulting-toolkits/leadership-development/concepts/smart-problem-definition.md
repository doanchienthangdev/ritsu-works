# SMART Problem Definition

*Category: problem-solving · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A framework for writing precise, actionable problem statements by ensuring they are Specific, Measurable, Agreed, Realistic, and Time-bound.

**Origin:** Attributed to George T. Doran (1981), who published the acronym in Management Review. Widely adopted in management consulting and project management as the entry point for any rigorous analytical process.

## Why it works
Most problem-solving failures begin with solving the wrong problem. By forcing explicit criteria across five dimensions, SMART prevents the most common consulting mistake — confusing a symptom with the root cause — and creates a shared contract between the analyst and the decision-maker about what 'done' looks like before any work begins.

## When to use
At the very start of any analytical project, strategic initiative, or performance improvement effort — before any data is collected, before any hypotheses are formed. Also use when a project stalls because team members disagree on what they are trying to achieve.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Draft a one-sentence problem description without using any SMART language yet — capture the raw issue as you understand it.
2. Apply the SMART filter: for each criterion, ask the corresponding question and rewrite or annotate your sentence until each criterion is satisfied.
3. Review the SMART statement with the key decision-maker in a 15-minute meeting; ask them 'Does this accurately capture the problem you want solved?' Revise until they confirm.
4. Document the agreed SMART problem statement at the top of your work plan and reference it at the start of every project meeting.
5. Test completeness: if you could hand this statement to a competent analyst who has never heard of this project, could they know what 'done' looks like? If not, it is not yet SMART.

## Real-life example — General Electric (GE) under Jack Welch — Six Sigma adoption 1995
When GE adopted Six Sigma in 1995, every improvement project was required to open with a precisely defined problem statement. One famous example from GE Lighting: 'The defect rate on Model A lamp bases is 3.4 per 1,000 units (vs. Six Sigma target of 3.4 per million), costing $4.2M annually in scrap and rework, and must be reduced to <100 per million by Q4 1997.' This SMART definition prevented scope creep and gave teams a clear finish line.

**So what:** A SMART problem statement is the most valuable 5 minutes of any project — it prevents weeks of wasted effort solving the wrong problem.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing a symptom with the problem: 'Customer complaints are up' describes a symptom; 'our returns process takes 14 days vs. a 3-day industry benchmark' describes the problem. Always ask 'why?' at least twice before finalising the statement.
- Skipping the 'Agreed' step: An analyst who defines the problem alone without validating it with the decision-maker risks spending 3 weeks solving the wrong problem. Non-negotiable: validate before structuring.
- Over-constraining in the statement: Do not bake in the solution ('We need to hire 10 more people to fix the response time issue'). The problem statement must describe the gap, not the fix.

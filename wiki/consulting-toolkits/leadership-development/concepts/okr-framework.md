# OKR Framework (Objectives and Key Results)

*Category: team-management · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A goal-setting system that pairs qualitative, inspiring Objectives (the direction) with quantitative, measurable Key Results (the evidence of progress) — creating ambitious yet trackable goals at every level of an organisation.

**Origin:** Developed by Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s (described in 'High Output Management', 1983). Popularised by John Doerr, who introduced OKRs to Google in 1999, and documented in 'Measure What Matters' (2018). Used by Google, Intel, LinkedIn, Twitter, and thousands of organisations globally.

## Why it works
OKRs solve two goal-setting failures simultaneously: vagueness (impossible to track) and excessive tactical focus (fails to inspire). Objectives are ambitious and qualitative; Key Results are specific and quantitative. The 0.7 target (70% completion = success) builds psychological safety for stretch goals, preventing sandbagging.

## When to use
Quarterly goal-setting for teams and individuals. When a team lacks clarity on priorities. When you want to align individual work with organisational strategy.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start from organisational strategy: identify the 2–3 most important things the team must accomplish this quarter.
2. Write each Objective as an inspiring, directional sentence — a destination that would energise the team.
3. For each Objective, write 3–5 Key Results that together prove the Objective is met. Each KR must be specific, measurable, and ambitious.
4. Test each KR against the sandbagging test: could the team achieve this without changing current behaviour? If yes, it is not ambitious enough.
5. Review OKR progress every two weeks. For each KR, report current status, confidence, and blockers.
6. At quarter end, score each KR 0.0–1.0. Retrospect: what did we learn? This informs the next cycle.

## Real-life example — Google — Q1 2000 (John Doerr's introduction of OKRs)
When John Doerr introduced OKRs to Google's 30-person team in 1999, the first OKR set: Objective — 'Get everyone on the internet using Google search.' KR1 — 10x query volume by year end. The OKRs were public, scored quarterly, and directly linked to resource allocation. By 2003, OKRs had scaled to thousands of employees with all OKRs visible company-wide.

**So what:** OKRs work because they make strategy operational (everyone knows what to work on) and create accountability through transparency.

## Template
Set 2–3 Objectives per quarter with 3–5 Key Results each. All OKRs on a single page, understood in 5 minutes.

- [ ] Quarter: _________ | Team: _________
- [ ] Objective 1 (inspiring direction, 1 sentence): _________
- [ ]   KR1.1: [Metric] will [increase/decrease] from [baseline] to [target] by [date].
- [ ]   KR1.2: _________
- [ ]   KR1.3: _________
- [ ] Objective 2: _________
- [ ]   KR2.1: _________ | KR2.2: _________ | KR2.3: _________
- [ ] End-of-quarter scores: O1: ___/___/___ | O2: ___/___/___
- [ ] Key learnings for next cycle: _________

## Pitfalls
- Setting too many OKRs: More than 3 Objectives and 5 KRs creates noise, not focus. Ruthlessly limit.
- Using OKRs as a to-do list: Key Results must be outcomes (measured results), not outputs (completed tasks).
- Linking OKR scores to performance ratings: This causes sandbagging. Keep OKRs and performance management separate.

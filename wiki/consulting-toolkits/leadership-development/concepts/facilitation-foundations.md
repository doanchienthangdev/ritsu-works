# Facilitation Foundations (Meeting Design Protocol)

*Category: facilitation · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A three-move facilitation framework — Create Foundation, Keep on Track, Get Back on Track — that enables a meeting or workshop facilitator to establish collaborative norms, maintain productive momentum, and recover when discussions stall or derail.

**Origin:** Facilitation as a discipline emerged from the National Training Laboratories (NTL) group dynamics work in the 1950s (Kurt Lewin's action research) and was codified in the International Association of Facilitators (IAF) competency framework (1994). The three-move framework is a synthesis of best practices from management consulting facilitation training programmes at McKinsey, BCG, and Deloitte.

## Why it works
Meetings fail for predictable reasons: no clear purpose (participants don't know why they're there), no agreed process (people don't know how decisions will be made), and no recovery mechanism (when discussions derail, the group stays derailed). The Facilitation Foundations framework addresses all three structural failure modes before they occur — by establishing purpose and norms upfront, using visible process tools to maintain momentum, and providing specific recovery techniques for the most common derailment patterns.

## When to use
For any meeting where a decision, plan, or creative output is needed. Essential for: strategic planning workshops, team retrospectives, problem-solving sessions, cross-functional alignment meetings, and any meeting with 6+ participants where domination by one voice is a risk.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Before the meeting, complete the facilitation planning template: What is the specific purpose? What is the desired output (decision, plan, list, or insight)? What is the agenda sequence? What facilitation techniques will you use for each agenda item? Who is the facilitator?
2. Open every meeting with Move 1 in exactly this order: (a) State the purpose: 'We are here to [specific purpose].' (b) State the desired output: 'By the end of this session, we will have [specific output — a decision, a prioritised list, an agreed plan].' (c) State the norms: 'Let's agree on 3 working norms: one voice at a time; challenge ideas not people; phones away unless urgent.' Post all three on the shared screen or whiteboard.
3. Use the parking lot throughout the session: when a valuable but off-topic contribution is made, say: 'Great point — let me park that so we don't lose it.' Write it on the parking lot (a visible list on the side). At the end of the session, review the parking lot and decide how to address each item.
4. Use structured rounds for complex topics where dominant voices tend to emerge: 'Let's go around the table — 60 seconds each on what you think the best option is.' This equalises air time and surfaces minority views that might not emerge in open discussion.
5. When the discussion stalls (group is going in circles) or derails (off-topic rabbit hole), use one of the three recovery techniques: (a) Time-check: 'We have [X] minutes left and we still need to decide [Y]. What is the most important thing to resolve?' (b) Straw-man: 'Let me put something on the table to give us something concrete to react to: [proposed decision]. Agree, disagree, or modify?' (c) Vote-and-move: 'Let's take a temperature check — how many support Option A? Option B? Let me hear one voice from the minority before we make a final call.'
6. Close every meeting with the same ritual: summarise the decisions made, confirm the owner and deadline for each action item, and identify one thing the group did well in its collaboration. This closing ritual builds cumulative meeting effectiveness.

## Real-life example — Lego Group — Design Sprint facilitation methodology (2017-2022)
Lego's innovation team adopted Design Sprint methodology (Jake Knapp, Google Ventures, 2016) which is built on the Facilitation Foundations model. In one documented sprint for a new digital play product line, the 5-day sprint facilitator opened each day with explicit Purpose ('Today we will...'), Output ('By 5pm we will have...'), and Norms ('No phones; yes-and not yes-but; every idea gets written down'). The parking lot captured 23 ideas over the week that were not in scope for the sprint but fed the next product cycle. The straw-man technique was used on Day 3 when the team was deadlocked on a user interface decision — the facilitator proposed a working solution and within 20 minutes the team had modified it into a unanimously supported design.

**So what:** The facilitator's job is to serve the group's thinking, not to contribute to it. A skilled facilitator is almost invisible — the group feels like it reached the answer on its own. The Facilitation Foundations are the invisible scaffolding that makes this possible.

## Pitfalls
- Purpose and output undefined: The single most common meeting failure. Without a clear Purpose and Output statement at the start, participants optimize for different things (some for discussion, some for decision, some for visibility). Always state both in the first 2 minutes.
- No parking lot: Without a visible parking lot, off-topic contributions either derail the agenda (if pursued) or create resentment (if dismissed). The parking lot honours every contribution while keeping the session on track.
- Facilitator also contributing content: A facilitator who contributes their own opinion on the substance of the discussion has stopped facilitating and started participating. Choose: are you the facilitator or a participant? In high-stakes decisions, the facilitator role should go to a neutral party.

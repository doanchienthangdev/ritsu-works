# Work Plan Template (Consulting Work Plan)

*Category: problem-solving · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A structured project management table that maps each priority analysis to its data source, analytical method, the team member responsible, the completion deadline, and the anticipated so-what if confirmed — creating a single shared reference that keeps complex multi-stream projects coordinated and insight-focused.

**Origin:** The consulting work plan format has been a standard McKinsey, BCG, and Bain tool for managing analytical projects since the 1970s. It is described in 'The McKinsey Engagement' (Paul Friga, 2009) and used in consulting training programmes globally as the primary project coordination tool.

## Why it works
Complex analytical projects involve multiple simultaneous workstreams, multiple data sources, and multiple team members. Without a shared, structured work plan, analysis duplication occurs, deadlines are missed, and the insight synthesis step (Pyramid Principle) lacks a coherent input set. The work plan also forces the team to articulate the anticipated so-what before running any analysis — the key discipline that makes analysis insight-driven rather than data-driven.

## When to use
At the start of every analytical project — from a 3-day analysis to a 12-week consulting engagement. The format scales from 5 rows (a quick diagnostic) to 50+ rows (a major transformation programme). Any project involving more than one person or more than one workstream benefits from a shared work plan.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. After completing the issue tree prioritisation, take the top-priority branches and list each required analysis as a row in the work plan table.
2. For each row, complete: (a) Analysis — what specifically will be done? (b) Data Source — where exactly will the data come from? Be specific: a vague 'company data' is not useful. (c) Method — what analytical technique will be used? (d) Owner — who is specifically responsible? One name, not a team. (e) Due Date — a specific date, not 'next week.' (f) Anticipated So-What — 'If this analysis shows X, then the implication for the recommendation is Y.'
3. Review the work plan in a team meeting at the start of the project. Confirm that every team member understands their analyses and agrees on the anticipated so-whats. Misalignment at this stage prevents wasted work later.
4. Update the work plan daily. Mark analyses as complete and insert the actual so-what (which may differ from the anticipated so-what). When an analysis is refuted rather than confirmed, mark it clearly and update the issue tree.
5. Build the Pyramid Principle structure from the confirmed so-whats in the work plan: the confirmed findings become the supporting evidence; the grouped themes become the key messages; the overarching implication becomes the recommendation.
6. If the project runs behind schedule, use the prioritisation matrix to triage remaining analyses: deprioritise low-impact analyses and make explicit assumptions for the ones you cannot complete.

## Real-life example — Bain & Company — retail cost transformation engagement (composite)
A Bain team managing a 12-week cost transformation for a global retailer used a work plan with 28 analysis rows across 4 workstreams (procurement, logistics, store operations, corporate overhead). Each row had a specific analyst owner, a 3-4 day deadline, and an anticipated so-what. By Week 4, 18 of 28 analyses were confirmed, 6 were refuted (branches eliminated from the issue tree), and 4 were revised based on early findings. The synthesis step in Week 10 was significantly faster than typical because every confirmed finding had already been written as a so-what — the Pyramid was assembled in 4 hours rather than the 2-3 days typical of data-first projects.

**So what:** The work plan is the bridge between the issue tree (the analytical architecture) and the Pyramid (the communication architecture). Without it, the synthesis step is chaotic; with it, the synthesis step is mechanical — assembly of pre-confirmed insights.

## Pitfalls
- Analyses without anticipated so-whats: A work plan row that says 'Analyse revenue data' with no anticipated so-what is a data collection task, not an analytical task. Every row must have a directional hypothesis and an anticipated so-what.
- Single owner per team for all analyses: When the manager is listed as the owner of every analysis, the work plan has no real accountability. Each row must have one named individual who owns the completion and quality of that analysis.
- Work plan as a one-time document: The work plan is most valuable as a daily management tool. If it is built on Day 1 and not updated until Day 10, it has failed its primary purpose. Update it daily; use it in daily stand-ups.

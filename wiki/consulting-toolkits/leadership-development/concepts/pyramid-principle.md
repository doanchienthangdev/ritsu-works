# Pyramid Principle

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A communication structure that presents the main conclusion first, then supports it with three grouped key messages, each backed by evidence — moving from answer to reasoning, not from reasoning to answer.

**Origin:** Developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey & Company in the 1960s–70s and published in 'The Minto Pyramid Principle' (1987). It became the global standard for executive communication in consulting, investment banking, and corporate strategy.

## Why it works
Executives and decision-makers are time-constrained and cognitively loaded. Leading with the answer (the apex of the pyramid) allows them to immediately evaluate whether they agree or disagree, and to engage with the supporting reasoning productively. Leading with evidence and building to a conclusion (the common academic structure) forces the audience to hold all the evidence in working memory until the conclusion arrives — a cognitive burden that results in disengagement and lost messages.

## When to use
For any written or oral communication where a recommendation or conclusion must be conveyed — executive presentations, board papers, strategy memos, email updates to senior stakeholders. Essential when the audience is time-constrained or when a decision is required.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Write your main conclusion in one sentence before touching any slides or documents. This is the apex of the pyramid. If you cannot state your conclusion in one sentence, you do not yet have a recommendation.
2. Identify the three supporting key messages that, taken together, make the conclusion irrefutable. Each key message is a grouped insight — not a data point, but a synthesised statement from multiple data points.
3. Test the grouping logic: do the three key messages follow an inductive logic (three independent pieces of evidence all pointing to the same conclusion) or a deductive logic (Message 1 + Message 2 = Message 3)? Use inductive structure for most business communications.
4. Populate each key message with 3–5 supporting data points, examples, or analyses. These are the base of the pyramid.
5. Apply the so-what test to every slide: does the action title state the so-what (a complete sentence claiming the insight), or just a label (a topic word)? Replace all label titles with action titles.
6. Build the SCR introduction: three sentences — (1) Situation (what everyone agrees is true), (2) Complication (the tension or problem), (3) Resolution (your recommendation — the apex of the pyramid). This becomes the opening of your presentation.

## Real-life example — McKinsey & Company — presentation to a Fortune 500 consumer goods CEO (published composite, McKinsey Quarterly 2021)
The team had 10 slides for a 30-minute meeting. The opening slide read: 'Recommendation: Exit the Brazilian market within 12 months and reinvest the $200M in the US direct-to-consumer channel.' Three supporting key messages followed: (1) Brazil is structurally unprofitable — contribution margin has been negative for 3 years. (2) Regulatory risk is high and increasing — proposed legislation would require 40% local content by 2026. (3) The US DTC channel offers 3x the ROI with existing infrastructure. Every subsequent slide had an action title. The CEO made the decision by minute 20, leaving 10 minutes for implementation planning.

**So what:** The Pyramid Principle is not about simplification — it is about respecting the audience's cognitive load and decision-making process. An answer-first structure makes the decision-maker's job easier, not the presenter's.

## Pitfalls
- Writing label titles ('Market Analysis', 'Financial Overview') instead of action titles ('The market is contracting 8% annually, making organic growth structurally impossible'). Label titles force the audience to draw their own conclusion — which may not be yours.
- Three key messages that are not truly grouped: If each key message is just a separate data point with no unifying theme, the pyramid structure breaks down. Each key message must be a synthesised claim that aggregates its supporting evidence.
- Building the pyramid bottom-up in a presentation: The analysis is built bottom-up (from evidence to conclusion), but the communication is always top-down (from conclusion to evidence). Never narrate the analytical journey in a presentation.

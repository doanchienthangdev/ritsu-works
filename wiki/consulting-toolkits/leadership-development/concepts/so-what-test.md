# So-What Test

*Category: problem-solving · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A quality-gate heuristic applied to every slide, data point, or paragraph in a business communication: 'So what? What should the audience think, feel, or do differently because of this?' — ensuring every piece of content earns its place by delivering a clear, actionable insight.

**Origin:** The 'so-what test' is a core tool in McKinsey's problem-solving and communication methodology, described in 'The McKinsey Way' (Ethan Rasiel, 1999) and 'The McKinsey Mind' (Rasiel & Friga, 2001). It operationalises the Pyramid Principle's 'action title' discipline: if you cannot write an action title (a claim, not a label) for a slide, the slide has no answer to the so-what test.

## Why it works
The natural tendency in analytical work is to present data and let the audience draw the conclusion. The so-what test forces the analyst to do that work — to translate data into insight ('Revenue is declining 8% annually' → 'Revenue is declining 8% annually, and our analysis shows this is entirely driven by customer churn in the 25-35 age cohort, making retention our single most critical near-term action'). Every analytical output that cannot pass the so-what test is data, not insight.

## When to use
Applied to every slide before finalising any presentation. Applied to every analysis before reporting results. Applied to every email longer than 5 sentences sent to a senior audience. This is the quality gate that separates analytical work from advisory work.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. After completing any analysis or writing any slide, read the title or opening sentence and ask: 'So what? What should I think, believe, or do differently because of this?' If the honest answer is 'I'm not sure' or 'that depends on context', the so-what is not yet clear.
2. Convert every label title to an action title: 'Revenue Analysis' → 'Revenue decline is entirely driven by churn in the 25-35 cohort, not pricing or volume' is an action title. Apply this to every slide before finalising the deck.
3. Apply the so-what test to data points: 'Revenue declined 8%' is data. 'Revenue declined 8% — and this decline is accelerating, suggesting we will reach breakeven loss in Q3 without intervention' is an insight that passes the so-what test.
4. Apply the so-what test to the whole document: 'If I removed this slide, would the story or recommendation change?' If the answer is 'not much', cut the slide. Every slide should pass both the individual so-what test (what does this specific slide tell us?) and the narrative so-what test (why does this slide need to be in this presentation?).
5. As a final check, have a colleague read the slide titles only (not the content) and ask them to reconstruct the key messages. If they can't, your action titles are not clear enough.

## Real-life example — Bain & Company — cost-reduction presentation to consumer goods client (composite)
A Bain team presented a cost-reduction analysis to the CFO of a major consumer goods company. First draft: 14 slides with label titles ('Procurement Analysis', 'Logistics Benchmarking', 'Overhead Costs'). The CFO read it and said: 'I can see you did a lot of work, but I still don't know what you're recommending.' The team applied the so-what test to every slide and rebuilt the deck: '35% of procurement spend is with suppliers offering above-market pricing', 'Our logistics cost per case is 23% above best-in-class — fixable within 18 months by consolidating to 3 carriers', 'Corporate overhead is 8% of revenue vs. 5% for best-in-class peers'. Final version: 8 slides, all action titles, clear recommendation on the front page. The CFO approved the full cost programme in the same meeting.

**So what:** The so-what test is the difference between an analyst who presents data and an advisor who tells the client what to do. Every consulting deliverable passes the so-what test by definition — if it doesn't, it is not a deliverable, it is an appendix.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing data with insight: 'Sales declined 12%' is data. 'Sales declined 12% — making this the worst Q3 in 7 years and the first quarter where we have lost money in the premium segment' is an insight. The so-what is in the implication, not the number.
- Too many so-whats per slide: A slide that tries to make three points has no so-what — it has three. One slide, one claim. If the slide cannot pass the one-point test, split it.
- Applying the so-what test at the end rather than throughout: The most efficient application is during the analysis phase — write the so-what hypothesis before running the analysis, and confirm or refute it with the data. This is what 'hypothesis-driven analysis' means in practice.

# Prioritisation Matrix (Impact-Solvability 2x2)

*Category: problem-solving · Toolkit: Leadership Development*

## What it is
A 2x2 grid that rates each identified issue or sub-problem on two dimensions — Impact (how much resolving this issue moves the overall answer) and Solvability (how feasible is it to analyse and resolve given available time and data) — to direct analytical effort to the highest-value branches first.

**Origin:** The 2x2 prioritisation approach has roots in the management consulting problem-structuring tradition (McKinsey, BCG) and in project management priority frameworks. The specific Impact-Solvability version is used in hypothesis-driven analytical processes where not all issues in an issue tree can be analysed equally. It is related to — but distinct from — the Eisenhower Matrix, which operates on tasks rather than analytical hypotheses.

## Why it works
In any complex problem, the issue tree will generate more branches than can be analysed with available time and data. Without a prioritisation mechanism, analysts tend to focus on the most interesting or most data-rich issues rather than the most important ones. The Impact-Solvability matrix forces an explicit trade-off: work first on high-impact, high-solvability issues; be explicit about deferring low-priority branches. This is the mechanism that makes hypothesis-driven consulting 3-5x faster than boil-the-ocean analysis.

## When to use
After building an issue tree and before building the work plan. Any time the analytical scope exceeds what can be addressed in the available time. Also use when a project is running behind schedule and requires rapid triage of remaining analyses.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List all branches from your issue tree on a whiteboard or spreadsheet. Each branch becomes a row in your prioritisation exercise.
2. Score each branch on Impact (1-5): 'If this branch resolves in favour of the hypothesis, how significantly does it change the overall recommendation?' A finding that changes the recommendation from 'exit the market' to 'stay and invest' is a 5; a finding that adds nuance to a minor sub-point is a 1.
3. Score each branch on Solvability (1-5): 'Given our time, data, and analytical capacity, how feasible is it to resolve this branch confidently in the time available?' A branch requiring data that would take 2 weeks to collect for a 3-week project scores 1.
4. Plot each branch on the 2x2. Confirm: are the top-right quadrant branches (high impact, high solvability) the ones that would most change the recommendation? If not, revisit your impact scores.
5. Build the work plan starting with top-right branches. For top-left branches (high impact, low solvability): identify a way to make them more solvable (proxy data, faster analysis method) or make an explicit assumption about them.
6. Communicate the prioritisation to the team and the client: 'We are focusing analysis on these 3 branches because they have the highest potential to change the answer. We are explicitly deferring these 4 branches because the time investment is not justified by their potential impact.'

## Real-life example — Bain & Company — retail turnaround engagement (composite from Bain case library, 2020)
A major European retailer was losing 3pp of market share annually across 12 potential root causes. The team built an issue tree with 14 branches covering pricing, assortment, store operations, supply chain, loyalty programme, and digital. Using the Impact-Solvability matrix, they scored each branch and identified 3 as priority analyses: Pricing (high impact — 40% of revenue at risk; high solvability — two weeks of POS data available), Store operations (high impact; medium solvability — mystery shopping data available), and Digital commerce (high impact; high solvability — web analytics immediately accessible). These 3 analyses were completed in Week 2. Finding: pricing and digital were the root causes, accounting for 2.5 of the 3pp share loss. The remaining 11 branches were either confirmed as non-causes or explicitly deferred.

**So what:** The most valuable 15 minutes in any analytical project is building the prioritisation matrix. It converts a potentially unlimited analysis scope into a focused, time-bounded work plan.

## Pitfalls
- Scoring impact based on data availability rather than importance: Analysts tend to rate impact higher for branches where they have data (because it feels solvable) and lower for branches where they don't. Impact must be scored independently of solvability — ask 'if we had unlimited time and data, how much would this change the answer?'
- Not communicating the prioritisation: Building the matrix privately and then working on only the priority branches without telling the client or team creates a trust problem. Make the prioritisation explicit and visible.
- Failing to revisit after early analyses: The first analyses completed often change the impact scores of remaining branches. Build in a mid-project review of the prioritisation matrix and update it based on findings.

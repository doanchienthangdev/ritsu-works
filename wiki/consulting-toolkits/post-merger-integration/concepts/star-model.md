# STAR Organization Design Model

*Category: org-design · Toolkit: Post Merger Integration*

## What it is
A five-element organizational design framework — Strategy, Structure, Processes, Rewards, People — that ensures all organizational elements are aligned with each other and with the integration strategy. Used to design the post-merger operating model.

**Origin:** Developed by Jay Galbraith at the University of Southern California's Center for Effective Organizations in the 1970s–1980s. Described in 'Designing Organizations' (Jossey-Bass, 1995) and 'Designing the Customer-Centric Organization' (Jossey-Bass, 2005).

## Why it works
Organizations are systems: changing one element without adjusting the others creates misalignment that degrades performance. The STAR model prevents the most common integration org-design failure — announcing a new structure (org chart) without redesigning the processes and rewards systems that determine how people actually work.

## When to use
Phase I Step 7 (operating model design) and Phase III (detailed planning). Re-run as a completeness check at the 90-day and 180-day integration reviews.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start with Strategy: confirm the integration strategy and operating model archetype are clear before touching any other element.
2. Design Structure: define reporting lines, spans of control, and decision rights for the integrated organization. Map the Day-1 interim structure and the 18-month target structure.
3. Design Processes: for each major business process (order-to-cash, hire-to-retire, procure-to-pay), determine whether to standardize on the acquirer's process, the target's process, or build a new best-of-breed process.
4. Design Rewards: align performance management, bonus structures, and recognition systems to reinforce integrated-organization behaviors — not the behaviors of two separate companies.
5. Design People: identify capability gaps in the integrated organization and determine whether to build, buy, or borrow the missing capabilities.
6. Audit for alignment: for each of the five elements, ask 'does this reinforce or undermine the other four elements?' Misalignments become integration action items.

## Real-life example — Procter & Gamble acquisition of Gillette (2005, $57B)
P&G used a disciplined STAR-model approach to integrate Gillette: Strategy (Gillette as a premium male grooming brand complementing P&G's household portfolio) drove Structure (Gillette retained brand autonomy but shared P&G's global supply chain infrastructure), Processes (Gillette adopted P&G's standardized procurement and logistics processes — a source of $1.2B in cost synergies), Rewards (sales teams rewarded on combined portfolio performance, not separate company targets), and People (Gillette's brand management talent retained; P&G's supply chain expertise extended to Gillette categories). The alignment across all five elements is credited as a key reason this integration exceeded its $1.2B synergy target.

**So what:** STAR alignment — particularly linking Rewards to integrated-organization behaviors rather than legacy company behaviors — is the most commonly neglected and highest-impact org-design lever in PMI.

## Template
Complete each of the five STAR elements for the integrated organization. Test alignment between elements — a conflict in any pair requires resolution.

- [ ] Strategy: the integration strategy statement that guides all org design
- [ ] Structure: org chart approach (Day 1 interim + 18-month target)
- [ ] Processes: standardize/harmonize/new-build decision for each major business process
- [ ] Rewards: performance management changes required; bonus structure for integrated teams
- [ ] People: key capabilities required; gap analysis; build/buy/borrow decision
- [ ] Alignment audit: which element pairs have misalignments? Resolution plan?

## Pitfalls
- Designing the org chart (Structure) without designing the processes and rewards — the most common failure: a new org chart with old incentives produces old behaviors.
- Using the STAR model only at the corporate level — the real value is in applying it to each major function and business unit where integration decisions are being made.
- Not re-running the STAR alignment check after each major integration milestone — as the integration progresses, new misalignments emerge.

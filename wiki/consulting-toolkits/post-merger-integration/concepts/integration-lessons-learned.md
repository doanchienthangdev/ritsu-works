# Integration Lessons Learned

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Post Merger Integration*

## What it is
A structured retrospective framework run at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days post-close to capture what worked, what stalled, what surprised, and what would be done differently — synthesized into a Lessons Learned Report that improves the corporate M&A playbook.

**Origin:** Standard project management retrospective practice (PMI PMBOK, Agile retrospectives); applied to M&A integration as a mandatory post-mortem in McKinsey and Deloitte PMI toolkits.

## Why it works
Most organizations repeat the same integration mistakes deal-after-deal because there is no systematic mechanism for capturing and institutionalizing integration learnings. The lessons learned retrospective converts integration experience into a corporate asset.

## When to use
Phase IV Step 4. Scheduled at Day 30, 90, 180, and 365 post-close.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Schedule retrospective sessions at Day 30 (first impressions while memory is fresh), Day 90 (first operational issues visible), Day 180 (synergy trajectory clear), and Day 365 (integration health at year one).
2. Facilitate each session with the IMO Director and all workstream leads. Use the four-question structure: what worked well, what did not work, what surprised us, what would we do differently.
3. For each 'did not work' or 'would do differently' finding, identify the root cause (was it a planning gap, an execution gap, a data gap, or an organizational capability gap?) and the recommendation for the next integration.
4. Synthesize findings into a Lessons Learned Report — a 5–10 page document, not a 100-item list. Focus on the 5–10 insights with the highest impact on deal value.
5. Present the report to the Steering Committee and corporate development leadership.
6. Incorporate approved learnings into the corporate M&A playbook so they survive beyond the individuals involved in this integration.

## Real-life example — Facebook acquisition of WhatsApp (2014, $19B)
Facebook's integration of WhatsApp was specifically designed to preserve WhatsApp's culture and operational independence. A retrospective at 90 days would have confirmed the Preservation archetype was working (WhatsApp retained its product focus and team), but also revealed the key risk: founder Jan Koum's cultural values were in tension with Facebook's data practices — a tension that ultimately led to his departure in 2018. Early lessons-learned would have surfaced this tension and allowed a proactive response.

**So what:** Founder/CEO retention risk is the most commonly missed retrospective finding in acqui-hire deals — the financial retention agreement masks the cultural and values-based fragility.

## Template
Complete one section per retrospective date. Focus on findings with deal-value implications. Incorporate learnings into the M&A playbook.

- [ ] Retrospective date (Day 30 / 90 / 180 / 365)
- [ ] Phase or area (1–6)
- [ ] What worked well: finding + root cause
- [ ] What did not work: finding + root cause + impact on deal value
- [ ] What surprised us: finding + implications
- [ ] What we would do differently: specific recommendation
- [ ] Playbook recommendation: yes/no + specific change to playbook
- [ ] Owner of playbook update

## Pitfalls
- Retrospectives dominated by success stories — the most valuable lessons are the failures; create psychological safety for candid failure disclosure.
- Findings that are too vague to act on ('communication could have been better') — each finding should be specific enough to change behavior on the next deal.
- Completing the retrospective but not updating the playbook — the lessons learned only create value when they are institutionalized.

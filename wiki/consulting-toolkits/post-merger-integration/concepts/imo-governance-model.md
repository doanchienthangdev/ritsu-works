# Integration Management Office (IMO) Governance Model

*Category: governance · Toolkit: Post Merger Integration*

## What it is
A three-tier governance architecture orchestrating the entire integration program: an Integration Steering Committee (C-suite decision authority), Integration Workstream Leads (functional owners), and the IMO PMO (day-to-day orchestration and tracking).

**Origin:** Codified in McKinsey and Deloitte PMI frameworks through the 1990s–2000s. The IMO concept became industry standard following mega-mergers in telecommunications and financial services. Described in Galpin & Herndon's 'The Complete Guide to Mergers and Acquisitions' (1999, 3rd ed. 2014).

## Why it works
Integration fails most often at the seams between functions. The IMO provides a neutral coordinating mechanism that sits above functional boundaries, enforces a common cadence, and gives the integration program a visible home with authority to resolve cross-functional conflicts.

## When to use
From Day 1 of integration planning (ideally pre-close) through the full 18–24 month integration period.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Appoint the IMO Director before close — this person needs authority, credibility with both organizations, and prior integration experience.
2. Charter the Integration Steering Committee: define its mandate, meeting cadence (bi-weekly), quorum requirements, and decision rights (what can the Steering Committee approve without Board escalation).
3. Assign a workstream lead for each major functional area. The lead owns all integration milestones and synergy commitments within their function.
4. Stand up the IMO PMO to own: the master integration plan, weekly status reporting, the issues and risks log, and the synergy tracking dashboard.
5. Define and publish the escalation protocol: workstream issue → IMO Director (48-hour resolution SLA) → Steering Committee (next bi-weekly meeting). Publish this in the IMO charter on Day 1.
6. Run the first governance cycle in Week 1: workstream leads submit status reports; IMO PMO consolidates; IMO Director reviews; Steering Committee meets in Week 2.

## Real-life example — Dow Chemical / DuPont merger (2017, $130B)
The Dow-DuPont merger created DowDuPont with a mandate to then split into three independent companies. The complexity required a two-tier IMO: an enterprise IMO managing the merger integration, and three business-unit IMOs managing the subsequent separations. Each IMO reported to its own Steering Committee but fed a unified Progress Dashboard. The discipline of this nested governance allowed three concurrent transformations to proceed without collapsing into chaos.

**So what:** For large, complex integrations, a single IMO may need to be nested — an enterprise IMO sets standards and tracks cross-cutting commitments while business-unit IMOs own domain-specific execution.

## Template
Fill in named individuals for each role. Review the governance design with the CEO before Day 1. Publish the IMO charter in Week 1.

- [ ] IMO Director: name + reporting line
- [ ] Integration Steering Committee members: names + roles
- [ ] Workstream list: function + lead name + headcount
- [ ] IMO PMO structure: PM lead + tools used
- [ ] Governance cadence: workstream meeting frequency + Steering Committee frequency
- [ ] Escalation protocol: issue → owner → SLA → escalation path
- [ ] IMO standing agenda template (workstream and Steering Committee)

## Pitfalls
- IMO Director without real authority — if the IMO can only report but not resolve, workstream leads route around it.
- Governance overload — weekly all-hands meetings with every workstream lead create fatigue and slow integration. Keep Steering Committee bi-weekly; drive workstream bilaterals.
- Disbanding the IMO too early — most integrations need 18–24 months of IMO governance; disbanding at 6–12 months leaves the integration unmoored at the most critical synergy-capture phase.

# BATNA-Based Negotiation (Principled Negotiation)

*Category: negotiation · Toolkit: Mergers & Acquisitions*

## What it is
A structured negotiation methodology from Fisher and Ury's 'Getting to Yes' (1981) that separates people from problems, focuses on interests rather than positions, generates options for mutual gain, and uses objective criteria — anchored by each party's Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement (BATNA).

**Origin:** Roger Fisher and William Ury, Harvard Negotiation Project, published as 'Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In' (1981, 2nd ed. 1991).

## Why it works
Most negotiations fail because parties argue positions rather than uncovering underlying interests. BATNA is the analytical foundation: know your best outside option (walk away), know theirs, and negotiate in the zone of agreement between the two. In M&A, BATNA structures both the bidding strategy and the SPA negotiation.

## When to use
Phase 5 (SPA negotiation). Also applicable in Phase 2 when managing a competitive bid process and in Phase 3 LOI negotiation.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. DEFINE YOUR BATNA: what is the acquirer's best outcome if this deal does not close? (Best alternative target, organic investment, partnership.) Quantify its value. This is your walk-away point.
2. 2. ESTIMATE SELLER'S BATNA: what are the seller's alternatives? (Other bidders, IPO, standalone operation.) Research recent comparable transactions and any known competing bidders.
3. 3. ESTABLISH YOUR POSITION LIMIT on each negotiable term: purchase price (ceiling), earnout structure (maximum risk), representations and warranties (minimum standard), exclusivity period. Get board approval for these limits before entering negotiation.
4. 4. PREPARE AN ISSUES LIST: list all open negotiation points (typically 15–30 in a full SPA negotiation). Prioritize into: deal-breakers (no concession), important (will concede if traded), and minor (can concede freely).
5. 5. GIVE TO GET: for every concession you make, require a concession in return. Maintain a concession log tracking what was exchanged to prevent perception of weakness.
6. 6. FOCUS ON INTERESTS: when the seller resists on price, explore why. Often there is a non-price interest (management retention, brand continuity, employee protection) that can be addressed without increasing the price.
7. 7. USE OBJECTIVE CRITERIA: anchor all price negotiations in objective data (DCF, comps, precedents) rather than arbitrary positions. Saying 'at $X price your IRR exceeds your cost of capital by only 200bps' is a facts-based anchor.

## Real-life example — Disney's negotiation with Lucas Film (2012)
Disney acquired Lucasfilm for $4.05B (half cash, half stock). George Lucas's BATNA was keeping Lucasfilm private or selling to a financial buyer (no strategic synergies). Disney's BATNA was organic franchise development (more expensive, slower). The ZOPA was significant — Disney could pay more than any financial buyer because of franchise synergies (theme parks, merchandise, film library). Disney structured the consideration as half-stock specifically to align Lucas's interest with Disney's post-acquisition success, addressing his non-price interest in seeing the franchise stewardship continue — a classic 'focus on interests' move.

**So what:** In M&A, price is rarely the only negotiation variable. Stock consideration, earnouts, management roles, and brand continuity commitments can bridge a significant price gap without increasing cash outflow.

## Pitfalls
- Revealing your BATNA too early — if the seller knows your walk-away, they will negotiate up to it. Mitigate: never reveal your BATNA; let the seller infer it from your willingness to walk away.
- Treating every point as a deal-breaker — this destroys trust and the deal itself. Mitigate: define true deal-breakers internally in advance and limit them to 3–5 genuinely material terms.

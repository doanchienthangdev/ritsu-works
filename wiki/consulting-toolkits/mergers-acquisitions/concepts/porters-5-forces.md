# Porter's Five Forces

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Mergers & Acquisitions*

## What it is
A framework for analyzing the competitive structure of an industry by examining five forces that determine long-run profitability: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of buyers, bargaining power of suppliers, threat of substitutes, and rivalry among existing competitors.

**Origin:** Michael E. Porter, Harvard Business School, 1979; foundational article 'How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy' in Harvard Business Review.

## Why it works
Industry profitability is not a function of a single company's choices but of the structural forces that govern competition. Understanding these forces tells an acquirer whether the target operates in an industry where profits can be sustained or will erode — and which specific threats the combined entity must address post-acquisition.

## When to use
Phase 1 (industry screening), Phase 2 (target assessment), Phase 4 (commercial due diligence), Phase 7 (management presentation / IM preparation).

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the industry precisely: the analysis is only meaningful for a specific, bounded market (e.g., 'enterprise cloud storage for financial services firms' not 'technology').
2. 2. Score each of the five forces on a 1–5 scale (1=low threat/power, 5=high threat/power). Use specific evidence for each score: named competitors, customer contract lengths, supplier concentration ratios.
3. 3. Calculate the industry attractiveness score: average of the five force scores inverted (a high average force score = low attractiveness).
4. 4. Identify the one or two dominant forces that most constrain profitability — these are the strategic risks the acquisition must address or leverage.
5. 5. Assess how the acquisition changes the combined entity's position on each force: does the acquisition improve negotiating leverage with suppliers? Does it create barriers to new entrants?
6. 6. Document findings in the commercial due diligence workstream (Phase 4, Step 3) and in the management presentation for carve-outs (Phase 7, Step 4).

## Real-life example — Salesforce acquisition of Slack (2021)
A Porter's analysis of the enterprise collaboration market revealed high rivalry (Microsoft Teams, Zoom, Google) and moderate buyer power (enterprise customers with multi-year contracts but high switching awareness). The acquisition strengthened Salesforce's position by increasing switching costs for customers (Slack embedded in Salesforce workflows) and raised the barrier for new entrants (Salesforce's distribution network amplified Slack's reach). The analysis justified the $27.7B premium.

**So what:** The value of an acquisition is often precisely its impact on competitive forces — not just the target's standalone financials. Always model how the acquisition reshapes each force for the combined entity.

## Pitfalls
- Conducting a static analysis without assessing how the industry structure is evolving — a low-rivalry market today may become highly competitive in 3 years due to technology disruption. Mitigate: add a 3-year force trajectory assessment.
- Treating 5 Forces as a standalone exercise rather than linking each force to a specific due diligence hypothesis. Mitigate: translate each force score into a due diligence question in the DD work plan.

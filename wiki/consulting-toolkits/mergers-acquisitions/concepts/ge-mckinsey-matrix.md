# GE-McKinsey 9-Box Matrix

*Category: portfolio · Toolkit: Mergers & Acquisitions*

## What it is
A 3x3 portfolio prioritization matrix that plots business units or acquisition targets on two axes — industry attractiveness (external) and competitive strength (internal) — to guide investment, hold, or divest decisions.

**Origin:** Developed jointly by General Electric and McKinsey & Company in the early 1970s as a more nuanced alternative to the BCG Growth-Share Matrix.

## Why it works
While BCG uses market growth rate and market share as proxies, GE-McKinsey uses multi-factor composite scores on both axes, making it more applicable to complex industries where a single metric is insufficient. In M&A target screening, the matrix forces an explicit, weighted comparison of targets across strategic dimensions rather than a one-dimensional revenue ranking.

## When to use
Phase 1 (define screening criteria), Phase 2 (assess and rank long-list targets). Revisit at Phase 3 gate to confirm business case targets are still the highest-priority options.

## Visual
`matrix-2x2`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the INDUSTRY ATTRACTIVENESS criteria (typically 5–7 factors): market size, market growth rate, industry profitability margins, competitive intensity (Porter's forces), cyclicality, regulatory risk. Assign weights summing to 100%.
2. 2. Define the COMPETITIVE STRENGTH criteria (typically 5–7 factors): relative market share, product/service quality, brand strength, distribution capability, technology/IP, management quality, financial strength. Assign weights summing to 100%.
3. 3. Score each acquisition target or business unit on each criterion (1–5 scale). Multiply by weights to get weighted scores. Sum to get composite scores for each axis.
4. 4. Plot each target on the 3x3 grid. Targets in the upper-right three cells (green zone: Invest/Grow) are the most attractive acquisition candidates.
5. 5. Present the matrix to the steering committee as a visual comparison of shortlisted targets, with the highest-priority candidates clearly in the green zone.
6. 6. Use the matrix output to prioritize which targets to advance to Phase 3 (Business Case) and how aggressively to pursue each.

## Real-life example — GE Capital M&A portfolio management (1980s–2000s)
GE, the originator of this framework, used the 9-box matrix to manage its portfolio of 150+ business units. In M&A, GE's corporate development team applied the same logic to acquisition targets: only targets in the high-attractiveness, high-strength zone received investment-grade consideration. Businesses in harvest zones were candidates for divestiture (which ultimately drove GE Capital's wind-down when healthcare and industrial units scored higher). This disciplined prioritization helped GE maintain a focused acquisition agenda in its growth decades.

**So what:** The power of the GE-McKinsey matrix is forcing a multi-dimensional, weighted comparison that prevents single-metric anchoring (e.g., only chasing revenue growth). The weighting of criteria is the most important and most contested step — get executive alignment on weights before scoring.

## Pitfalls
- Reverse-engineering the scoring to justify a pre-determined conclusion — selecting and weighting criteria after seeing the data to make a preferred target score higher. Mitigate: define and lock criteria/weights before scoring any target.
- Using the matrix without agreeing on what 'High,' 'Medium,' and 'Low' mean quantitatively for each criterion. Mitigate: define quantitative anchors for each score level.

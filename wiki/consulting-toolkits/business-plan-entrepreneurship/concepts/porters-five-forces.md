# Porter's Five Forces

*Category: market-analysis · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A framework for assessing industry structural attractiveness by evaluating five competitive forces: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of buyers, bargaining power of suppliers, threat of substitutes, and competitive rivalry.

**Origin:** Michael E. Porter, Harvard Business School, published in 'Competitive Strategy' (1980) and the HBR article 'How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy' (1979). The most cited strategy framework in academic and practitioner literature.

## Why it works
Porter's premise: industry profitability is not random — it is determined by structural forces. A company in a structurally attractive industry (low forces) earns above-average returns even with an average strategy; a company in a structurally challenged industry struggles even with an excellent strategy. Five Forces allows entrepreneurs to assess structural attractiveness before committing capital — and to identify where to build a moat.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the industry narrowly and specifically. 'Technology' is not an industry. 'AI-powered compliance training for regulated mid-market companies' is an industry. The analysis changes entirely with definition breadth.
2. Analyse Threat of New Entrants: what barriers protect incumbents? Capital requirements, IP/patents, regulatory licences, network effects, switching costs, economies of scale. Score 1–5 (1 = high barriers, hard to enter; 5 = low barriers, easy to enter).
3. Analyse Bargaining Power of Suppliers: identify top 3–5 inputs. For each: how concentrated is the supplier market? How unique is the input? What is the cost of switching supplier? Score 1–5 (1 = suppliers have low power; 5 = high power).
4. Analyse Bargaining Power of Buyers: how concentrated are customers? How price-sensitive? How differentiated is the product? Score 1–5 (1 = low buyer power; 5 = high buyer power).
5. Analyse Threat of Substitutes: list all products that satisfy the same job in a different way. At what price would customers switch to the substitute? Score 1–5 (1 = low substitution threat; 5 = cheap effective substitutes).
6. Analyse Competitive Rivalry: number and size of competitors, differentiation level, fixed cost intensity (pressure to compete on price), exit barriers. Score 1–5.
7. Sum all five scores: 5–12 = very attractive; 13–18 = moderate; 19–25 = challenging.
8. Derive 2–3 strategic implications directly from the highest-scoring forces: e.g., 'High new entrant threat → invest in network effects and switching costs early.'

## Real-life example — Netflix vs. linear TV (2010)
Five Forces on linear TV broadcasting (2010): Threat of New Entrants = 1 (broadcast licences, tower infrastructure, content rights = high barriers). Supplier Power = 4 (studios and sports rights holders have enormous pricing power). Buyer Power = 1 (consumers cannot negotiate). Threat of Substitutes = 5 (streaming, YouTube, on-demand all competing for viewer time). Competitive Rivalry = 3. Total: 14 — moderate. But the Substitute score of 5 was a red flag. Netflix correctly identified this as the structural vulnerability in linear TV and built the substitute.

**So what:** Five Forces is as useful for identifying where to attack an industry as it is for assessing whether to enter it. Netflix essentially ran a Five Forces analysis on linear TV, identified its Achilles heel, and built the substitute.

## Pitfalls
- Defining the industry too broadly, producing a generic and useless analysis.
- Treating a low score as a guarantee of profitability — the model describes structural attractiveness, not actual performance.
- Ignoring the dynamic dimension: forces change over time. Build a 'forces in 3 years' scenario for high-stakes decisions.

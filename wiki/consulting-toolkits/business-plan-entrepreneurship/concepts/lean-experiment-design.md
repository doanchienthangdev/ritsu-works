# Lean Experiment Design (MVP Typology)

*Category: validation · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A framework for selecting the minimum viable experiment type — from customer interviews to concierge MVPs to smoke tests — that will generate credible evidence for a specific hypothesis at the lowest possible cost and time.

**Origin:** Synthesised from Eric Ries's 'The Lean Startup' (2011) and Steve Blank's Customer Development methodology; MVP typology catalogued by Ash Maurya in 'Running Lean' (2012) and Alberto Savoia in 'The Right It' (2019).

## Why it works
The cost of building a product to test whether customers want it is orders of magnitude higher than running a targeted experiment to test the same assumption. Lean Experiment Design operationalises 'fake it before you make it' by providing a menu of experiment types with known fidelity-vs-cost trade-offs.

## When to use
Phase 3, after defining falsifiable hypotheses for each critical assumption. Select experiment type before designing the experiment to avoid defaulting to 'build the product.'

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Classify each critical assumption as Desirability (D), Viability (V), or Feasibility (F).
2. Select the lowest-fidelity experiment that can still generate credible evidence. Start with D assumptions using customer discovery interviews.
3. Customer Discovery Interviews: prepare 6–8 open-ended problem-focused questions. Never show the solution in the first 5 interviews — you want unbiased problem data. Interview ≥8 people for qualitative saturation.
4. Smoke Test: build a landing page in 24 hours describing the product. Add a 'Join Waitlist' or 'Pre-order' button. Drive 200–500 targeted visitors. Measure call-to-action click-through rate against pre-defined pass threshold.
5. Concierge MVP: deliver the service entirely manually. You are the product. Confirms whether customers value the outcome even if inefficiently delivered — before investing in automation.
6. Technical Spike: time-box to 1–5 days. Goal: determine feasible / infeasible / feasible with significant unknowns. Write a 1-page spike report.
7. Run experiments in parallel where assumptions are independent — compresses the validation timeline.
8. After each experiment, complete a Hypothesis Testing Card and update the Assumption Map.

## Real-life example — Airbnb
Chesky and Gebbia tested two critical assumptions before building any technology: (1) homeowners will let strangers sleep in their homes, (2) travellers will sleep in strangers' homes. Concierge MVP: photographed their own apartment, published on a basic website, hosted three paying guests during a San Francisco conference. Cost: essentially $0. Both assumptions confirmed in one weekend. They then ran a smoke test to test scalability of demand. Total validation cost before any product code: <$500.

**So what:** Airbnb's sequence is the gold standard: start with the cheapest possible experiment (rent your own home = concierge MVP), confirm desirability, then test scale. Never build before confirming critical assumptions.

## Pitfalls
- Building a full product as the first experiment. If a landing page can answer the hypothesis, use a landing page.
- Confusing a Concierge MVP with product delivery. The concierge phase deliberately does not scale — it is a learning exercise.
- Recruiting friends and family as interviewees. They will tell you what you want to hear.

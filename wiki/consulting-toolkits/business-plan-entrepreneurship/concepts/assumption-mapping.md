# Assumption Mapping (Importance × Uncertainty Matrix)

*Category: validation · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A 2×2 matrix that plots every belief embedded in the business model on two dimensions — importance (how critical to success) and uncertainty (how little evidence we have) — to identify which assumptions must be tested before proceeding.

**Origin:** Derived from the Lean Startup's 'riskiest assumption test' concept (Ries, 2011) and formalised as a visual tool by Jeff Gothelf and Josh Seiden in 'Lean UX' (2013) and by David Bland in 'Testing Business Ideas' (2019).

## Why it works
Resource misallocation is the most common cause of startup failure during validation: founders spend months testing assumptions that are already confirmed (low uncertainty) or that would not matter if wrong (low importance), while leaving the truly critical and uncertain beliefs untested. The matrix forces explicit, visual prioritisation. The top-right quadrant (high importance + high uncertainty = 'Test First') reveals the minimum set of experiments required before committing to a business plan.

## When to use
Phase 2 (identify critical assumptions), phase 3 (design experiments, update after validation), and phase 5 (ongoing business model iteration based on operating data).

## Visual
`matrix-2x2`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start with the completed Business Model Canvas. List every explicit and implicit belief across all 9 blocks. A typical canvas contains 20–40 beliefs. Examples: 'Users will share their location with us,' 'Enterprises will pay $500/month for this feature,' 'We can license content for <$3/user/year.'
2. Categorise each belief as: Desirability (customers want this), Viability (the economics work), or Feasibility (we can build/deliver this). Most desirability assumptions have the highest combined importance and uncertainty for early-stage startups.
3. Score each belief on Importance: 1 (nice-to-have; if wrong, minor adjustment needed) to 5 (existential; if wrong, the entire business model collapses).
4. Score each belief on Uncertainty: 1 (fully confirmed; multiple data points exist) to 5 (pure hypothesis; no evidence).
5. Calculate Priority Score = Importance × Uncertainty. The top 5–8 beliefs by priority score are your 'Critical Assumptions.'
6. Plot all beliefs on the 2×2 matrix. The top-right quadrant contains your Critical Assumptions.
7. Design one experiment per Critical Assumption. Write a falsifiable hypothesis for each (see Hypothesis Testing Protocol). Prioritise experiments in order of priority score — run the highest-priority experiments first.
8. After phase 3 validation, return to the matrix and update Uncertainty scores based on new evidence. Critical assumptions that are now confirmed move left (lower uncertainty) and can be treated as Safe Bets.

## Real-life example — Zappos (early days)
When Nick Swinmurn was testing the idea of selling shoes online in 1999, the most critical assumption was: 'People will buy shoes without trying them on first.' This was HIGH importance (the entire business model depends on online purchase behaviour) and HIGH uncertainty (online retail was new; shoe fitting was considered a sensory, in-store experience). Swinmurn ran a concierge MVP: he photographed shoes at local stores and posted them online. When orders came in, he bought the shoes at retail price and shipped them. Result: confirmed. The assumption mapping framework would have immediately flagged this as the #1 test to run — and it is exactly the experiment he ran.

**So what:** The genius of Zappos' early validation is that it tested the most critical and uncertain assumption (willingness to buy online) before investing in inventory, warehousing, or technology — saving millions that would have been wasted if the assumption had been wrong.

## Pitfalls
- Listing only assumptions you are uncertain about, and ignoring the importance dimension. An uncertain belief about font choice is irrelevant. An uncertain belief about whether the customer has budget authority is existential.
- Treating the assumption map as a one-time exercise. The map must be updated after every validation cycle and after every pivot.
- Testing assumptions in the wrong order — starting with feasibility (can we build it?) before desirability (do customers want it?). Build order: Desirability first, then Viability, then Feasibility.

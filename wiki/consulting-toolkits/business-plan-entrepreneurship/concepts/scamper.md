# SCAMPER Creative Thinking

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
SCAMPER is a seven-prompt creative thinking checklist — Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify/Magnify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse/Rearrange — that forces non-obvious idea generation by applying systematic transformations to an existing concept.

**Origin:** Developed by Bob Eberle in 1971, building on Alex Osborn's brainstorming work from the 1950s. Widely adopted in design thinking curricula and entrepreneurship programmes.

## Why it works
SCAMPER operationalises the insight that novelty is almost always recombination: the brain generates new ideas by remixing existing concepts. By systematically asking seven different transformation questions, it breaks the anchoring bias that causes ideators to cluster around a single concept and forces exploration of adjacent possibility spaces.

## When to use
During ideation (phase 1) to expand the idea pool beyond obvious concepts; when iterating on a product to find improvement opportunities; when analysing a competitor's disruption logic to understand their strategic approach.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Select a business idea, product, or process you want to improve or transform.
2. For each of the 7 SCAMPER prompts in sequence, write at least 3 possible applications to your selected idea. Do not filter during generation — record every answer, even implausible ones.
3. For each answer, write a one-sentence 'transformed idea' statement: 'What if we [transformation]? The result would be [new concept].'
4. Review all transformed ideas. Identify the 3–5 that are most novel AND most feasible given your constraints.
5. Combine SCAMPER outputs where two or more transformations complement each other (e.g., Substitute + Eliminate often produce platform business models).
6. Add the most promising SCAMPER-derived ideas to your Business Idea Generator log.

## Real-life example — Netflix
Netflix's journey from DVD-by-mail to streaming demonstrates all seven SCAMPER prompts: Substitute (physical DVDs → digital streaming), Combine (movie rental + subscription model), Adapt (Blockbuster's video library + Amazon's logistics efficiency), Modify (magnified content library from hundreds to thousands of titles), Put to other use (customer data from viewing behaviour used to fund original content production), Eliminate (late fees — the most hated feature of Blockbuster), Reverse (instead of going to the store, content comes to you). The result was a $200B+ media company that destroyed its industry incumbent.

**So what:** SCAMPER is most powerful when applied not just to generate ideas but to analyse the competitive disruption logic of successful companies — understanding which SCAMPER transformation a competitor exploited helps identify the white space they left behind.

## Template
Apply each of the 7 prompts to your selected idea. Write ≥3 answers per prompt. Circle the most promising 1–2 per prompt. Then identify 2–3 cross-prompt combinations.

- [ ] Idea being transformed: ___
- [ ] S — Substitute: What component, material, person, or process could be substituted? (≥3 answers): ___
- [ ] C — Combine: What could be combined with this idea to create something new? (≥3): ___
- [ ] A — Adapt: What from another industry, time period, or context could be adapted? (≥3): ___
- [ ] M — Modify/Magnify: What could be enlarged, scaled, enhanced, or changed? (≥3): ___
- [ ] P — Put to other use: What else could this be used for? Who else could benefit? (≥3): ___
- [ ] E — Eliminate: What could be removed, simplified, or streamlined? (≥3): ___
- [ ] R — Reverse/Rearrange: What if the order, roles, or process were reversed? (≥3): ___
- [ ] Top 3 SCAMPER-derived ideas to add to the idea log: ___

## Pitfalls
- Applying SCAMPER to the obvious dimensions only (e.g., Substitute = just change the material). Force yourself to apply each prompt to every dimension: the customer, the delivery model, the pricing, the channel, the team structure.
- Stopping at one answer per prompt. The first answer is always the most obvious. The third or fourth answer is where novelty lives.

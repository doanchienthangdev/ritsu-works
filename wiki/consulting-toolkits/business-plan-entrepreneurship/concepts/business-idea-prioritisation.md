# Business Idea Prioritisation Matrix

*Category: ideation · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A weighted scoring matrix that evaluates business ideas against 7 venture-success criteria to identify the one concept with the highest probability-weighted return, before committing any capital or build time.

**Origin:** Adapted from multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) techniques used in management consulting, applied specifically to entrepreneurial idea selection in the Domont Consulting toolkit.

## Why it works
Entrepreneurs consistently make two cognitive errors when selecting an idea: they anchor on the first idea that excites them (availability bias), and they evaluate ideas using implicit weights that favour their own skills over market opportunity. The weighted scoring matrix makes weights explicit and visible, allows structured comparison of multiple options simultaneously, and forces the team to agree on evaluation criteria before seeing the scores — reducing the risk of post-hoc rationalisation.

## When to use
After generating a pool of ≥10 business ideas and before committing any capital, team time, or customer conversations to a specific concept. Re-apply after a phase 3 pivot to re-score the updated business model.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List all ideas that passed the knockout filter from the Business Idea Generator.
2. Agree the 7 criteria and their weights as a team BEFORE assigning any scores. Use the standard weights (Market Size 20%, Founder Fit 20%, Problem Severity 15%, White Space 15%, Time to Revenue 10%, Capital Intensity 10%, Regulatory Risk 10%) unless your specific context justifies a different allocation.
3. Define the scoring anchors for each criterion before scoring (e.g., Market Size: 1=<$10M TAM, 3=$100M–$1B, 5=>$10B). Written anchors prevent score inflation for favourites.
4. Score each idea independently — each founder scores separately before discussing. Calculate the individual weighted totals. If scores diverge by >1.0 on any criterion, discuss until alignment is reached.
5. Calculate the team average weighted total for each idea. Rank from highest to lowest.
6. Select the top-ranked idea as the champion. Document the rationale in a 1-page Champion Idea Selection Memo that includes: winner score, runner-up score, top 3 assumptions to test, and decision to proceed.
7. File the runner-up ideas — they may become relevant after a pivot.

## Real-life example — Dropbox
Drew Houston evaluated three ideas in 2006: an online storage solution (Dropbox), a social network for college students, and a local services marketplace. The prioritisation matrix would have scored Dropbox highest on Founder Fit (5 — Houston was a developer who personally experienced the pain of lost USB drives) and Problem Severity (5 — file loss is a critical, daily problem). The social network would have scored low on Founder Fit (1 — already dominated by Facebook). The local services marketplace would have scored low on Time to Revenue (2 — complex supply-side acquisition). Dropbox's winning score on founder-relevant criteria drove the decision that led to a $10B exit.

**So what:** The Founder Fit criterion is often the most predictive of early-stage success and the most systematically underweighted. The matrix forces founders to acknowledge when a market is large but they have no genuine advantage in it.

## Pitfalls
- Setting weights after seeing the scores (post-hoc rationalisation). Always agree criteria and weights before scoring.
- Treating a high score as validation — the matrix measures relative attractiveness among the ideas you generated, not absolute market attractiveness. A high score among weak options is still a weak option.
- Over-weighting market size and under-weighting founder fit. At the seed stage, the founding team is the primary product. A large market with the wrong team loses to a smaller market with the right team almost every time.

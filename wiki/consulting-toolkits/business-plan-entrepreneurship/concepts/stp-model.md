# STP Model (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning)

*Category: marketing · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A three-step marketing strategy framework that identifies distinct customer groups (Segmentation), selects the most valuable group to focus on (Targeting), and designs how to position the product in the target group's mind relative to competitors (Positioning).

**Origin:** Developed from the work of Wendell Smith ('Product Differentiation and Market Segmentation as Alternative Marketing Strategies,' 1956) and formalised by Philip Kotler in 'Marketing Management' (first edition, 1967). The STP framework is the foundation of modern marketing strategy.

## Why it works
Trying to sell to everyone means selling to no one. STP forces market focus by dividing the market into distinct, addressable segments (each with different needs and behaviours), selecting the segment where the company has the greatest competitive advantage and can generate the most value, and then designing a positioning tailored to that segment's specific psychology. This focus dramatically improves marketing efficiency and pricing power.

## When to use
Phase 4 (marketing plan section). The STP model should be completed before writing any copy, building any landing page, or choosing any marketing channel.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Segmentation — divide the market into distinct groups. Use 4 segmentation bases: (1) Demographic (age, income, company size, industry), (2) Geographic (country, region, city size), (3) Psychographic (values, lifestyle, attitudes toward risk, technology adoption), (4) Behavioural (usage rate, purchase occasion, benefits sought, loyalty). For B2B: add firmographic (company size, industry, revenue, headcount) and behavioural (procurement sophistication, buying cycle length). Aim for 3–5 distinct segments — not too many to be useful, not too few to be meaningful.
2. For each segment, assess attractiveness using 5 criteria: (1) Size (is it large enough to be worth targeting?), (2) Measurability (can you quantify the segment?), (3) Accessibility (can you reach them with your channels?), (4) Actionability (can you serve them with your capabilities?), (5) Differentiation (do they have distinct needs from other segments, justifying separate targeting?).
3. Targeting — select 1–2 segments for initial focus. Apply the 'concentration vs. differentiation' rule: at seed stage, concentrate on 1 segment. Expansion to multiple segments is a phase 5+ move. Choose the segment where: (a) you have the strongest competitive advantage, (b) the problem is most severe (highest willingness-to-pay), and (c) you have the most credibility (founder background, network access).
4. Positioning — design the positioning for the target segment. First, identify the frame of reference (what category are you in?). Second, identify the point of difference (what are you better at than any competitor in that category?). Third, identify the reason to believe (what is the evidence that supports your point of difference?). Write the Positioning Statement in the standard format: 'To [target customer] who [need/opportunity], [brand] is the [category] that [benefit/point of difference] because [reason to believe/proof].'
5. Test the positioning: does it (a) distinguish you from all direct competitors? (b) resonate with the target customer's top-priority job or pain? (c) align with your actual capabilities? If any answer is no, revise.
6. Operationalise the positioning through the 4Ps (see Marketing 4Ps framework) — the positioning statement is the brief; the 4Ps are the implementation.

## Real-life example — Dove (Unilever relaunch, 2004)
Dove's STP: Segmentation — personal care market divided by attitude toward beauty (confident users vs. beauty-anxious users vs. functional users). Targeting — women 35–54 who feel excluded from traditional beauty advertising standards (the beauty-anxious segment, 60% of the market, massively underserved). Positioning — 'To real women who feel excluded from unrealistic beauty standards, Dove is the personal care brand that celebrates natural beauty and real women because Dove uses real women (not models) in all advertising.' The resulting 'Campaign for Real Beauty' generated $1.5B in sales growth in 3 years. The STP analysis revealed that the largest underserved segment was being ignored by every competitor chasing 'aspirational beauty.'

**So what:** The most valuable application of STP is identifying the underserved segment — the large group with strong needs that all competitors have ignored because they are all chasing the same 'attractive' customer. Dove found this by systematically segmenting on psychographics (attitude toward beauty), not just demographics.

## Pitfalls
- Targeting multiple segments simultaneously at launch — resources are divided, messaging is diluted, and no segment is served excellently.
- Writing a positioning statement that describes the product rather than the customer benefit ('We are the fastest platform' vs. 'You close deals 3× faster').
- Choosing the targeting decision before the segmentation is complete — you need to see all segments before you can choose the best one.

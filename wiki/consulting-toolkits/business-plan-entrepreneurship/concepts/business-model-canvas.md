# Business Model Canvas (BMC)

*Category: business-model · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A one-page visual framework with 9 building blocks that describes how an organisation creates, delivers, and captures value. It is the most widely used business modelling tool in the world, enabling teams to describe, challenge, and pivot business models quickly.

**Origin:** Developed by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, documented in 'Business Model Generation' (2010). Adopted by Stanford d.school, Singularity University, and most top-10 business schools worldwide.

## Why it works
Traditional business plans are linear documents that obscure the interdependencies between commercial decisions. The BMC places all 9 elements on a single visual canvas, making interdependencies visible: the Value Proposition must align with Customer Segments; the Revenue Streams must be sufficient to cover the Cost Structure; the Key Activities must deliver the Value Proposition. When you change one block, you can immediately see which other blocks are affected — making it a superior tool for iteration and pivoting.

## When to use
Phase 2 (define the business model), phase 3 (update based on validation data), phase 4 (embed in business plan), and phase 5 (update quarterly based on operating data). The BMC is the living heartbeat of the business model — not a one-time artifact.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Gather the founding team. Work on a physical whiteboard or in a shared Miro/FigJam space. Use sticky notes — not permanent markers — because the canvas will change.
2. Complete block 1 — Customer Segments: Name 1–3 distinct customer groups. For each, write a specific demographic + psychographic + behavioural description. Be concrete: not 'small businesses' but 'B2B SaaS companies with 10–50 employees in the US in fintech or healthcare.'
3. Complete block 2 — Value Propositions: For each customer segment, write the value proposition using the formula: 'We help [segment] who want to [job-to-be-done] by [solution] unlike [alternative].' Identify whether the value comes from: newness, performance improvement, customisation, convenience, design, brand, price, cost reduction, risk reduction, or accessibility.
4. Complete block 3 — Channels: Map the full customer journey (Awareness → Interest → Purchase → Delivery → After-sales). For each stage, name the specific channel (e.g., LinkedIn ads → sales demo call → Stripe checkout → onboarding email sequence → Intercom support chat).
5. Complete block 4 — Customer Relationships: For each segment, specify the relationship type: personal assistance, dedicated personal assistance, self-service, automated, community, or co-creation. Justify why this type of relationship fits the segment's expectations.
6. Complete block 5 — Revenue Streams: Name each revenue stream, its pricing mechanism (fixed list price, dynamic pricing, market price, volume-based, yield management, auction), and estimate the percentage of total revenue it represents.
7. Complete blocks 6, 7, and 8 (Resources, Activities, Partners): Work backwards from what the Value Proposition and Channels require. Ask: what must we have, do, and partner with to deliver the VP at scale?
8. Complete block 9 — Cost Structure: List the top 5–8 cost items by magnitude. Classify each as fixed or variable. Determine whether the business is cost-driven (low price, lean operations, e.g., Ryanair) or value-driven (premium value justifies premium cost, e.g., Apple).
9. Step back and check for logical consistency: does the Revenue Stream cover the Cost Structure at scale? Does the Channel reach the Customer Segment? Does the Key Activity enable the Value Proposition? Mark any block where the answer is uncertain — those are candidate critical assumptions.

## Real-life example — Spotify
Spotify's BMC (at launch, 2008): Customer Segments (music listeners who pirate + music listeners who buy CDs); Value Propositions ('all the music, instantly, legally, for free or a small fee — unlike piracy which is risky, and unlike CDs which are expensive and inconvenient'); Channels (web, then mobile app store); Customer Relationships (self-service for free tier, automated personalised recommendations for paid); Revenue Streams (freemium → premium subscription €9.99/month, + ad revenue for free tier); Key Resources (music licensing agreements with major labels, streaming technology, recommendation algorithm); Key Activities (licensing negotiation, playlist curation, algorithm development); Key Partners (4 major music labels without whom the model fails); Cost Structure (70%+ of revenue paid in royalties — the structural constraint that makes Spotify's economics challenging at scale).

**So what:** The BMC immediately reveals the most critical aspect of Spotify's business: the Cost Structure block is dominated by label royalties (Key Partners), which means Spotify's profitability depends entirely on its negotiating power with its most powerful partners — a structural vulnerability that is visible on the canvas but easy to miss in a linear business plan.

## Template
Complete each block in order 1–9. Each block must contain 3–5 specific statements. Avoid generic descriptions. Use sticky notes so the canvas can be updated as assumptions are validated.

- [ ] 1. Customer Segments: Segment A (demo+psycho+behavioural profile): ___ | Segment B: ___
- [ ] 2. Value Propositions: For A: 'We help [A] who want [job] by [solution] unlike [alt]': ___ | For B: ___
- [ ] 3. Channels: Awareness: ___ | Interest: ___ | Purchase: ___ | Delivery: ___ | After-sales: ___
- [ ] 4. Customer Relationships: Segment A (type + why): ___ | Segment B: ___
- [ ] 5. Revenue Streams: Stream 1 (name, pricing mechanism, % of revenue): ___ | Stream 2: ___
- [ ] 6. Key Resources: Resource 1 (name, type: physical/intellectual/human/financial): ___ | Resource 2: ___ | Resource 3: ___
- [ ] 7. Key Activities: Activity 1: ___ | Activity 2: ___ | Activity 3: ___
- [ ] 8. Key Partners: Partner 1 (name, why they are critical): ___ | Partner 2: ___
- [ ] 9. Cost Structure: Top 5 costs (item, fixed/variable, estimated monthly $): ___ | Business type: Cost-driven / Value-driven
- [ ] Logical consistency check: Revenue covers Cost at scale? (Y/N): ___ | Channel reaches Segment? (Y/N): ___ | Activity enables VP? (Y/N): ___ | Uncertain blocks (→ candidate critical assumptions): ___

## Pitfalls
- Starting with block 2 (Value Proposition) instead of block 1 (Customer Segments). If you define the VP before the customer, you will build a solution in search of a problem.
- Writing generic, non-falsifiable statements: 'Our customers are businesses' or 'We create value through quality.' These cannot be tested. Specificity is the point.
- Treating the first canvas as the final canvas. The BMC is a hypothesis, not a description. It should be updated after every phase of validation.

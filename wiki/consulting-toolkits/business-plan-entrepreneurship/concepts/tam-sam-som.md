# TAM / SAM / SOM Market Sizing

*Category: market-analysis · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A three-tier market sizing framework that quantifies the Total Addressable Market, Serviceable Addressable Market, and Serviceable Obtainable Market to set credible revenue ambitions in the business plan and communicate market opportunity to investors.

**Origin:** Standard framework in venture capital and investment banking; widely taught at Harvard Business School, MIT Sloan, and the Kauffman Foundation; embedded in most VC pitch-deck templates since the 1990s.

## Why it works
Investors need answers to two questions: (1) Is the total opportunity large enough? (TAM ≥ $1B is the standard VC threshold for venture-scale returns.) (2) Is the founder's revenue projection credible? (SOM must be derivable bottom-up from realistic customer acquisition assumptions.) The three-tier approach answers both in a single exhibit.

## When to use
Phase 4 (target market section of the business plan). Always present all three tiers. Re-apply after a pivot to a new customer segment or geography.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the market category clearly and at the right level of specificity — not 'technology' (too broad) and not 'AI compliance training for fintech in Chicago' (too narrow). The category you are competing in.
2. Calculate TAM top-down: find a credible published market size from an industry research firm (Gartner, IDC, Statista, IBISWorld). Cite source. If no published figure exists for your exact category, use adjacent categories and add sizing rationale.
3. Calculate SAM by applying filters to TAM: geography (what % of global spend is in your target markets?), regulation/segment (what portion applies to your specific customer type?), channel (what portion can you reach given your sales model?). SAM = TAM × geographic% × segment% × channel%.
4. Calculate SOM bottom-up: (number of target customers reachable by sales team) × (conversion rate %) × (ARPU/year). This must equal 1–5% of SAM for early-stage companies. If SOM bottom-up and SOM top-down disagree by more than 5×, revisit assumptions.
5. Sanity check: if SOM exceeds 10% of SAM, you need a very credible explanation. Markets rarely tip this fast.
6. Present all three tiers in a single funnel exhibit with sources cited. Include the ICP definition that drives the SOM.

## Real-life example — Zoom (2011 pitch context)
A TAM/SAM/SOM for Zoom at launch: TAM = global enterprise communications market = $43B (Gartner). SAM = video conferencing for SMBs and mid-market underserved by Cisco WebEx/Microsoft Lync = $6B. SOM bottom-up: 1M target SMBs × $200/year ARPU × 5% 5-year penetration = $100M ARR year 5. This SOM was plausible and buildable — though dramatically underestimated ($4B ARR actual by 2021). The point: the bottom-up SOM was credible and defensible assumption-by-assumption.

**So what:** The value of TAM/SAM/SOM is not precision — it is credibility. A bottom-up SOM that founders defend assumption-by-assumption beats '2% of the market' every time with sophisticated investors.

## Template

## Pitfalls
- Using only top-down SOM without a bottom-up cross-check. '1% of the global market' is arithmetic, not a strategy.
- Conflating TAM and SAM by failing to apply realistic constraints. Not every person on earth is your customer.
- Citing market research without checking the methodology — some figures include the entire value chain, others count only addressable spend.

# Marketing Mix (4Ps Framework)

*Category: marketing · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A framework that defines the four controllable elements of a marketing strategy — Product, Price, Place, and Promotion — and their alignment to deliver the value proposition to the target customer.

**Origin:** Developed by E. Jerome McCarthy in 'Basic Marketing: A Managerial Approach' (1960) and popularised by Philip Kotler. Later extended to 7Ps (adding People, Process, Physical Evidence) for service businesses. The 4Ps remain the most widely used marketing planning framework globally.

## Why it works
Every marketing failure traces to a misalignment among the 4Ps: a product that does not match the value proposition (product-market mismatch), a price that does not reflect perceived value (pricing mismatch), a channel that does not reach the target customer (place mismatch), or messaging that does not motivate the target customer to act (promotion mismatch). The 4Ps framework makes all four decisions explicit and aligns them with the positioning statement.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start with the Positioning Statement from STP as the brief for the 4Ps. Every 4P decision must serve the positioning.
2. Product: define what will be offered at launch (MVP scope — resist feature creep). For each feature, ask: does this directly deliver the stated value proposition? If not, defer it. Define quality standards, packaging, branding, and any guarantees or warranties.
3. Price: select the pricing model first (subscription, per-use, freemium, tiered, or one-time purchase), then the price point. Use the 'Good-Better-Best' tiering model if multiple segments exist. Set the price based on perceived value to the customer, not on cost-plus. Calculate CAC and LTV at this price point — if LTV/CAC < 3×, the price is too low or the acquisition cost is too high. Define discounting rules (when, how much, who can approve) — uncontrolled discounting destroys pricing power.
4. Place: select the primary distribution channel for the launch — direct (inbound or outbound sales, self-serve website) or indirect (partners, resellers, platforms, marketplaces). For B2B SaaS at seed stage, direct sales is almost always the answer (only move to indirect channels once the sales playbook is proven). Define the steps in the distribution/delivery process.
5. Promotion: list all acquisition channels being considered. For each channel, estimate: monthly reach, expected conversion rate, and resulting CAC. Select 2–3 channels for initial focus (diffusion of effort is the #1 marketing mistake). For each, define the content type, cadence, and KPI. Set total monthly marketing spend and expected leads and customers from that spend.
6. Align all 4Ps with each other: Premium price requires premium product quality and premium placement (not discount channels). Freemium price requires viral promotion (the product must market itself). Self-serve place requires strong content promotion (customers must find you without a sales team).
7. Calculate the full unit economics: Revenue per customer (ARPU) – COGS per customer = Gross Profit. Gross Profit / CAC = Payback Period (target <18 months). ARPU × Retention Rate / Churn Rate = LTV. LTV / CAC = LTV:CAC ratio (target ≥3×).

## Real-life example — Spotify
Spotify's 4Ps at launch (2008): Product — streaming music platform, free tier (ad-supported) + premium tier, mobile and desktop apps; high-quality audio (160kbps free, 320kbps premium). Price — freemium ($0 free, €9.99/month premium); anchored against iTunes ($0.99/track purchase = ~10 purchased tracks/month cost = $9.90, comparable to premium). Place — direct digital distribution; app stores; partnerships with telecoms for bundled offerings. Promotion — invitation-only waitlist (artificial scarcity drove virality); influencer seeding with music bloggers; PR campaign emphasising the legal alternative to piracy narrative. All 4Ps aligned to the positioning: 'All the music, legal, instant, free or cheap.' The freemium price enabled mass adoption; the premium-tier anchor price captured willingness-to-pay from heavy users.

**So what:** Spotify's 4P alignment is textbook: every element reinforces the positioning. The freemium price creates the mass user base; the conversion rate from free to paid (26–30% historically) makes the economics work; the digital-only Place reduces distribution costs; the viral Promotion leverages the product's inherent shareability.

## Pitfalls
- Setting price based on cost-plus rather than perceived value. If customers would pay $500 for your product, charging $100 because it cost $30 to build destroys value.
- Pursuing too many promotion channels simultaneously at launch — resources and attention are divided, no channel is executed excellently.
- Allowing product features to accumulate without testing whether they serve the positioning (feature bloat).

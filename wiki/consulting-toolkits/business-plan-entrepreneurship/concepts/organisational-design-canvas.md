# Business Structure and Organisational Design

*Category: operations · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A structured framework for defining the legal, governance, and operating model of a new venture, covering entity choice, shareholding structure, board design, organisational chart, key hire planning, and intellectual property strategy.

**Origin:** Synthesised from corporate law, organisational design theory (Galbraith's Star Model, 1973), and practitioner experience from entrepreneurship programmes at Stanford, Harvard, and Y Combinator.

## Why it works
Structural decisions made early in a venture's life — particularly entity choice, cap table structure, and IP ownership — are disproportionately difficult and expensive to change later. A Delaware C-Corp with poorly designed founder vesting is a common and avoidable mistake that has destroyed more than one founding team. This framework forces these decisions to be made deliberately and documented before the business scales.

## When to use
Phase 4 (business structure section of the business plan). Also use when bringing on a co-founder, accepting the first external investment, or hiring the first senior employee who receives equity.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Choose the legal entity: For US-based ventures seeking VC funding, Delaware C-Corp is standard — it is familiar to investors, allows stock options, and has a well-developed legal framework. For bootstrapped or revenue-funded businesses, an LLC may offer tax advantages. For UK/EU ventures, a Limited Company or equivalent. Do not spend >2 hours on this decision — follow the convention for your geography and funding path.
2. Set up the cap table from day one: list every founder with their equity percentage and the rationale (time commitment, capital contribution, idea origination, execution role). Agree on the vesting schedule before a dollar of revenue is made — the market standard is 4 years with a 1-year cliff (no shares vest if a founder leaves in the first 12 months; 25% vest at month 12, then monthly thereafter). Document a buyback right for unvested shares if a founder departs.
3. Design the board: at seed stage, a board of 3 (2 founders + 1 independent with domain expertise) is optimal. Add an investor board seat only when accepting institutional capital. Agree in the founders' agreement what decisions require board approval vs. founder discretion. Reserve approval rights for: equity issuance, material contracts >$X, hiring/firing of C-suite, M&A.
4. Build the organisational chart: draw the year-1 org chart with actual names and roles. Then draw the year-2 and year-3 org charts with the roles you will need to hire. The org chart reveals the operational gap: the difference between your current team and your year-2 chart is your hiring plan.
5. Prioritise the top 3–5 key hires for the next 12 months: define the role title, core responsibilities, the 3 most important skills, the profile of the ideal candidate (industry background, functional experience, values fit), and the target compensation range.
6. Document the IP strategy: what will be patent-applied-for (where novel, non-obvious, and useful)? What will be kept as a trade secret (where patenting would require disclosure that competitors could design around)? What will be built as a network-effects moat (where the data or community itself becomes the barrier)? Ensure all IP is assigned to the corporate entity — never to individual founders.

## Real-life example — Facebook (2004 founding structure)
Facebook's early structural decisions illustrate both best practices and cautionary tales. Best practice: Delaware C-Corp from day one, enabling VC investment. Cautionary tale: Eduardo Saverin's 34% equity stake was diluted in a series of financing rounds to <3% — a dispute that cost both parties millions in legal fees and became the subject of a major film. The lesson: vesting schedules and dilution mechanics should be explicitly agreed and documented in a founders' agreement before any capital is raised. Facebook's Harvard Network IP was assigned to the corporate entity from the beginning — enabling clean investment and eventual $104B IPO.

**So what:** Structural decisions are irreversible in the short term. Spend a day with a lawyer at founding to set up the entity, vesting, and IP assignment correctly. This investment ($5,000–$10,000 in legal fees) prevents disputes that cost $1M+ to unwind.

## Pitfalls
- Skipping the founders' agreement ('We trust each other, we don't need it'). 65% of startups experience a co-founder conflict; the agreement is what prevents it from destroying the company.
- Failing to implement vesting for existing shares — the founder who leaves in month 3 should not walk away with 33% of the company.
- Letting IP remain with individual founders rather than the corporate entity — this is a blocking issue for most institutional investors.

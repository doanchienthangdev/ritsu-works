# OKR (Objectives and Key Results) Framework

*Category: operations · Toolkit: Business Plan & Entrepreneurship*

## What it is
A goal-setting methodology that pairs ambitious qualitative Objectives with measurable, time-bound Key Results, enabling teams to align on what matters most each quarter and track execution with unambiguous accountability.

**Origin:** Developed by Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s as Management by Objectives (MBO) adapted for fast-moving technology companies. Popularised by John Doerr (who learned it from Grove) at Google, Kleiner Perkins, and documented in 'Measure What Matters' (2018).

## Why it works
Startups suffer from two common execution failures: too many priorities (everything is urgent, nothing gets done) and unclear success criteria (tasks are completed but don't move the needle). OKRs solve both: the 3–5 Objectives per quarter enforce focus (if everything is an objective, nothing is), and the 3–5 Key Results per objective enforce clarity (if a KR cannot be measured, it has not been achieved). The quarterly cadence creates urgency without the chaos of week-to-week priority changes.

## When to use
Phase 5 (implementation). OKRs are the primary execution mechanism for translating the business plan into quarterly action. Begin at launch and maintain every quarter.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Set Company OKRs first (top-down) at the start of each quarter. The CEO/founding team proposes 3–5 Objectives for the quarter. Each objective should be: (a) qualitative and inspiring (not a metric — that belongs in the Key Results), (b) achievable within the quarter, (c) directionally aligned with the annual strategy.
2. For each Objective, write 3–5 Key Results: make them measurable, specific, time-bound, and outcome-based (not task-based). 'Launch the mobile app' is a task. 'Achieve 500 mobile app installs and 35% day-7 retention by December 31' is a Key Result.
3. Cascade to team OKRs: each function/team (product, sales, marketing, engineering) writes their own OKRs that support the company OKRs. The cascade creates alignment without micromanagement.
4. Break Key Results into weekly tasks and assign to named owners with due dates. Use a project management tool (Notion, Linear, Asana) to track.
5. Weekly sprint review (30 minutes): what was planned, what was done, what is blocked? Update KR progress scores.
6. Monthly retrospective (90 minutes): are we on track vs. OKRs? What needs to change? What new information have we learned that should update our priorities?
7. End-of-quarter OKR scoring: score each KR 0.0–1.0. Average KR scores to get the Objective score. Target average 0.7 — consistently scoring 1.0 means goals were not ambitious enough.
8. Conduct a start-of-quarter OKR planning session (2–3 hours): review learnings from the prior quarter; set new Objectives for the next quarter; ensure every team's OKRs support the company OKRs.

## Real-life example — Google (2000 — early OKR use)
Larry Page's Q1 2000 OKR: Objective — 'Make Google's web index the most comprehensive and freshest on the internet.' Key Results: (1) 'Index 200M web pages by March 31 (vs. 60M today)'; (2) 'Achieve <30 days lag between page publication and index inclusion'; (3) 'Achieve zero major crawl failures per week.' These OKRs forced the engineering team to prioritise index expansion and freshness over feature work for one full quarter. The OKR created alignment: every engineer knew that their work in that quarter was measured by the KRs, not by individual managers' preferences.

**So what:** Google's early OKRs reveal two critical design principles: (1) Key Results are outcomes, not tasks — 'index 200M pages' is an outcome, 'run the crawl jobs' is a task; (2) The numerical threshold eliminates ambiguity — there is no argument about whether 195M pages 'counts.' This precision is what makes OKRs powerful.

## Pitfalls
- Writing task-based Key Results instead of outcome-based KRs ('Launch feature X' instead of 'Achieve X users adopting feature Y within 30 days of launch').
- Setting too many Objectives (>5 per quarter). Three to five is the maximum for genuine focus.
- Using OKRs for performance management — grading employees on their KR scores destroys the system because people set sandbagged, achievable KRs instead of ambitious ones.

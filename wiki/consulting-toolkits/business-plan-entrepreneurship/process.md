# Business Plan & Entrepreneurship Toolkit

**Eight-phase, ex-McKinsey playbook to build, fund, and scale any new venture from idea to exit.**

*Core value: The practitioner can transform a raw idea into a funded, operating venture by following an end-to-end process — from generating and filtering ideas, through validating assumptions with real data, building a bankable business plan, implementing with KPI discipline, raising capital with professional-grade valuation and term-sheet literacy, conducting rigorous financial analysis, and managing stakeholders — without ever needing to hire an expensive consulting firm.*

---

## Start with the Right Problem: 80% of Businesses Fail — and Most Failures Are Predictable

More than 80% of new businesses fail within their first five years. The failure modes are well-documented: no market need (the #1 cause, cited in 42% of post-mortems), running out of cash, wrong team, getting outcompeted, and pricing/cost failures. What is striking is that virtually all of these are predictable and preventable if the entrepreneur runs a disciplined process before committing capital.

This toolkit is the process. It was refined over 20 years by ex-McKinsey, Deloitte, and BCG consultants and entrepreneurs who have collectively built, advised, and funded hundreds of ventures. It is not a theoretical framework — it is the process the world's best consultants would run if they were building a company from scratch.

---

## When to Use This Toolkit (and Who It Is For)

This toolkit applies at any of the following trigger situations:

1. **Evaluating a new idea before committing capital**: You have a business concept but are not sure whether it is the right one. Phases 1–3 are your filter.
2. **Building a business plan for investors**: You have a validated idea and need a compelling, data-grounded plan. Phases 2–4 are your core.
3. **Scaling an operating startup**: You are already live but need tighter financial discipline, a professionalised management system, or capital to grow. Phases 5–7 are your operating manual.
4. **Preparing for a funding round**: You need a valuation, cap table, and term-sheet literacy. Phase 6 is your toolkit.
5. **Managing investors and the board**: You need a systematic stakeholder engagement process. Phase 8 is your playbook.

**Who is this for?** First-time entrepreneurs, startup founders, corporate innovators, MBA students, and the management consultants who advise them.

## Phase 1: Generate Business Ideas and Select the Most Promising One

**Goal:** Produce a shortlist of high-quality business ideas and select one for deep development.
**Duration:** 1–2 weeks.

### Step 1.1 — Run the Business Idea Generator

Facilitate a structured ideation session using four lenses: (a) problems you personally experience and understand deeply, (b) industries undergoing regulatory or technological disruption, (c) jobs-to-be-done that existing products handle poorly, and (d) adjacencies to markets where you already have unfair advantages.

Apply SCAMPER prompts (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse) to each lens to force non-obvious combinations. Record every idea in an idea log with a one-sentence problem statement and a one-sentence proposed solution. Target: 20–40 raw ideas minimum.

**Input:** Founder skills inventory, market trend scan, personal pain-point list
**Output:** Idea log with 20–40 raw ideas
**Owner:** Founding team

### Step 1.2 — Prioritise Business Ideas Using a Weighted Scoring Matrix

Build a weighted scoring matrix with 7 criteria: market size, founder fit/unfair advantage, problem severity and urgency, competitive white space, time to first revenue, capital intensity (lower is better), and regulatory risk (lower is better). Assign weights that sum to 100%. Score each idea 1–5 per criterion, multiply by weight, and sum to get a weighted total.

Apply a mandatory knock-out filter first: any idea requiring capital beyond the founder's access path, or facing an insurmountable regulatory barrier, is eliminated before scoring. Select the top-ranked idea as the 'champion.' The champion idea should score ≥70% of the maximum possible score.

**Input:** Idea log from step 1.1
**Output:** Ranked ideas scorecard; champion idea with documented rationale
**Owner:** Founding team

**Gate:** The founding team unanimously agrees the champion idea is worth the next 2–4 weeks of hypothesis testing. If no idea clears 70%, restart ideation.

## Phase 2: Define Your Business Model and Identify Critical Assumptions

**Goal:** Translate the champion idea into a complete Business Model Canvas and surface the riskiest assumptions.
**Duration:** 1–2 weeks.

### Step 2.1 — Complete the Business Model Canvas (BMC)

Fill in all 9 BMC blocks in a specific order designed to prevent circular reasoning: (1) Customer Segments, (2) Value Propositions, (3) Channels, (4) Customer Relationships, (5) Revenue Streams, (6) Key Resources, (7) Key Activities, (8) Key Partners, (9) Cost Structure. Each block must contain 3–5 specific, concrete statements. For Value Propositions, use the format: 'We help [segment] who want to [job-to-be-done] by [solution] unlike [alternative].'

**Input:** Champion idea, desk research on target customers and competitors
**Output:** Completed Business Model Canvas
**Owner:** Founding team

### Step 2.2 — Identify and Rank Critical Assumptions

List every explicit and implicit belief in the BMC (typically 20–40 beliefs). Categorise each as a desirability assumption (customers want this), viability assumption (the economics work), or feasibility assumption (we can build it). Score each on importance and uncertainty, both 1–5. Multiply: importance × uncertainty = priority score. The top 5–8 beliefs (highest priority score, i.e., the top-right quadrant of the importance-vs-uncertainty matrix) are the 'critical assumptions' that must be tested in phase 3.

**Input:** Completed Business Model Canvas
**Output:** Assumption register with top 8 critical assumptions highlighted
**Owner:** Founding team

**Gate:** The team can articulate the top 3 critical assumptions that, if wrong, would invalidate the business model.

## Phase 3: Gather Data to Validate or Invalidate Critical Assumptions

**Goal:** Test the riskiest assumptions with real-world evidence before committing to a full business plan.
**Duration:** 2–6 weeks.

### Step 3.1 — Define Data Requirements for Each Assumption

Write a falsifiable hypothesis for each critical assumption: 'We believe [specific belief]. We will know this is TRUE when we see [observable metric] from [number] of [participants] within [time].' Define pass and fail thresholds in advance. This prevents post-hoc rationalisation — the most common and damaging cognitive trap in early-stage validation.

**Input:** Critical assumptions register
**Output:** Research brief with one hypothesis card per critical assumption
**Owner:** Founder / designated researcher

### Step 3.2 — Select and Execute Data-Collection Methods

For each hypothesis, select the fastest, cheapest method that generates credible evidence: customer discovery interviews (8–12 for desirability; qualitative saturation), smoke tests/landing pages (for willingness-to-pay), concierge MVP or Wizard-of-Oz prototype (for viability), and technical spike (for feasibility). Run experiments in parallel where possible. Document raw data immediately after each event.

**Input:** Research brief, target customer contact list, MVP budget
**Output:** Raw data log
**Owner:** Founding team

### Step 3.3 — Synthesise Data and Update the Business Model

For each hypothesis, record the result (confirmed / disconfirmed / ambiguous). Identify implications: does this update the BMC? If ≥3 of the top 5 critical assumptions are disconfirmed, conduct a structured pivot decision. Document the pivot rationale. Update the assumption register and the business idea prioritisation score to reflect new evidence.

**Input:** Raw data log, original hypothesis cards, Business Model Canvas
**Output:** Updated BMC, updated assumption register, pivot/persist decision memo
**Owner:** Founding team

**Gate:** At least 4 of 5 top critical assumptions confirmed (or a new model's assumptions defined post-pivot). ≥10 target customers interviewed.

## Phase 4: Build a Business Plan

**Goal:** Produce a complete, investor-ready business plan translating the validated model into a compelling narrative and credible 3-year financial model.
**Duration:** 3–6 weeks.

### Step 4.1 — Articulate the Problem and Solution
Use the Before-After-Bridge structure. Quantify the problem wherever possible. Lead the solution description with the primary benefit, not the technology.

### Step 4.2 — Define Mission, Vision, and Strategic Objectives
Write a mission statement (present tense, ≤25 words, action-oriented). Write a vision statement (aspirational, time-bounded). Define 3–5 SMART strategic objectives that cascade from the vision.

### Step 4.3 — Define the Target Market Using TAM/SAM/SOM
Quantify the market with a top-down check (TAM → SAM via geographic/regulatory filters) and a bottom-up build for SOM (units × price × realistic share). The SOM bottom-up and top-down should agree within a 2× range.

### Step 4.4 — Conduct Market Analysis Using Porter's Five Forces
Score all five forces 1–5. An aggregate score above 18 is a red flag — reconsider positioning. Draw 2–3 strategic implications directly linked to the business model.

### Step 4.5 — Conduct Competitor Analysis
Map direct, indirect, and substitutable competitors. Build a comparison matrix and a positioning map on the two axes most relevant to the target customer. Identify defensible white space.

### Step 4.6 — Build the Marketing Plan
Apply the STP model (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning) and the 4Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion). Define CAC, LTV, and payback period targets.

### Step 4.7 — Define the Business Structure
Document the legal entity, shareholding table, board composition, operating model, organisational chart, key hires plan, and IP strategy.

### Step 4.8 — Build the Financial Plan
Construct an integrated 3-statement model with 3-year monthly projections, three scenarios (base/bull/bear), breakeven analysis, and a use-of-funds table.

**Gate:** A trusted adviser has reviewed the plan and rated it credible. Every financial assumption is defensible with data.

## Phase 5: Implement Your Business Plan and Gather More Data

**Goal:** Launch, track performance with a KPI dashboard, and update the strategy continuously based on real operating data.
**Duration:** Ongoing (12–24 months post-launch).

### Step 5.1 — Define and Build the KPI Dashboard
Select 8–12 KPIs covering all AARRR stages. For each KPI: define the metric, name the data source, assign an owner, set a target, and set a reporting cadence. Build a single-page executive dashboard reviewed weekly.

### Step 5.2 — Build and Execute the Action Plan with OKRs
Translate the business plan into 90-day OKRs: 3–5 Objectives per quarter, each with 3–5 measurable Key Results. Break into weekly tasks in a project management tool. Weekly sprint review + monthly retrospective.

### Step 5.3 — Monitor the Monthly Budget
Track all budget vs. actuals monthly. Colour-code variances: ≤5% green, 5–15% amber, >15% red. For red lines, document root cause and corrective action. Maintain a rolling 3-month cash runway projection.

### Step 5.4 — Collect New Data and Update the Business Plan
Run a continuous loop: monthly customer success interviews (5–10 customers), weekly NPS pulse, monthly win/loss analysis, quarterly market scan. Route insights to the relevant plan section. Update the business plan quarterly.

**Gate:** One full quarter of operating data available; team can name the 2–3 most important strategic changes made based on real data.

## Phase 6: Estimate the Value of Your Business and Raise Funds

**Goal:** Produce a credible multi-method valuation, understand venture capital mechanics and term sheets, and close a funding round at a fair price.
**Duration:** 3–6 months.

### Step 6.1 — Value the Business Using Three Methods
Apply DCF (project FCFs, apply WACC of 30–50% for early-stage, calculate terminal value), Comparable Company Analysis (5–10 comps, apply median revenue or EBITDA multiple), and Precedent Transaction Analysis (3–5 recent transactions, apply median transaction multiple). Display results as a 'football field' chart. The defensible point is the intersection of all three methods.

### Step 6.2 — Understand VC Mechanics and Build the Cap Table
Study the VC fund structure (LP → GP → portfolio), investment decision criteria (market, team, traction, defensibility), and how each funding round dilutes ownership. Build a cap table showing all shareholders, fully diluted share count, post-money ownership, and exit waterfalls at multiple scenarios ($5M, $10M, $50M).

### Step 6.3 — Negotiate the Term Sheet
Master 10 key provisions before entering negotiations: post-money valuation, financing amount, liquidation preference (1× non-participating is founder-friendly), anti-dilution protection (broad-based weighted average is reasonable), option pool size and timing (pool shuffle is a hidden dilution source), board composition, protective provisions, drag-along rights, pro-rata rights, and vesting schedule (4 years, 1-year cliff, credit for past service). Know your walk-away position for each term before the negotiation starts.

**Gate:** Signed term sheet from a reputable investor at a valuation within the football field range; no founder-unfriendly terms.

## Phase 7: Carry Out a Financial Analysis

**Goal:** Conduct rigorous ongoing financial analysis to monitor health, identify risks, and provide decision-quality reporting.
**Duration:** Monthly, ongoing.

The six analyses work as a system:

1. **Income Statement Analysis**: Revenue → Gross Profit → EBITDA → Net Income. Track gross margin % and EBITDA margin % month-on-month. Benchmark against industry peers.
2. **Balance Sheet Analysis**: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Monitor working capital trends and the quick ratio (below 1.0 signals liquidity risk).
3. **Cash Flow Statement**: Indirect method from net income. Calculate operating cash flow — the purest signal of business model health. Track burn rate and runway (alert if <6 months).
4. **Three Statements Model**: Link all three statements so changes cascade automatically. Stress-test with -20% revenue and +30% costs simultaneously.
5. **Sales and Costs Analysis**: Decompose revenue by product, customer, geography, channel. Build customer cohort analysis. Classify costs as fixed vs. variable. Calculate operating leverage.
6. **Financial Ratios Dashboard**: Calculate liquidity (current ratio, quick ratio), efficiency (AR days, inventory days, CCC), profitability (gross margin, EBITDA margin, ROE), growth (MoM, YoY revenue), and startup-specific (CAC, LTV, LTV/CAC ≥3× required, payback period, NRR) ratios monthly.

**Gate:** Board receives the monthly financial package within 10 business days of month-end, with no material restatements.

## Phase 8: Engage Your Stakeholders Effectively

**Goal:** Identify all stakeholders, understand their interests and influence, and execute a differentiated engagement strategy.
**Duration:** Ongoing — begin in phase 4, formalise post-funding.

### Step 8.1 — Conduct Stakeholder Analysis
List all internal and external stakeholders. Score each on influence (1–5) and interest (1–5). Classify using the 2×2 influence-interest matrix: Key Players (manage closely), Keep Satisfied (high influence, low interest), Keep Informed (low influence, high interest), Monitor (low/low).

### Step 8.2 — Build the Stakeholder Engagement Strategy
Define a differentiated engagement approach for each quadrant. For any 'resistant' stakeholder, develop a tailored influence plan. Document frequency, channel, content, and owner for each group.

### Step 8.3 — Execute the Stakeholder Engagement Detailed Plan
Translate the strategy into a 90-day action calendar. Build standard templates for recurring communications (monthly investor update, quarterly board pack, product newsletter). Track completion and measure sentiment quarterly.

**Gate:** All Key Players briefed on strategy and performance; no unresolved high-priority concerns.

## The Frameworks and Tools

*Full anatomy of each framework — what it is, the logic, how to apply it, a real company example, a fill-in template, and pitfalls — appears below.*

### Business Idea Generator and SCAMPER
The Business Idea Generator is a structured ideation protocol that uses four strategic lenses — personal experience, market disruption, jobs-to-be-done, and capability adjacency — combined with SCAMPER prompts to generate non-obvious business concepts systematically. It prevents the most common entrepreneurial error: anchoring on the first idea that feels exciting rather than systematically searching the space.

### Business Model Canvas
Alexander Osterwalder's 9-block visual framework maps how an organisation creates, delivers, and captures value. It is the most widely used business modelling tool in the world and the foundation of phase 2.

### Porter's Five Forces
Michael Porter's framework for assessing industry structural attractiveness, developed at Harvard Business School in 1979. It prevents entrepreneurs from entering markets that look attractive on the surface but are structurally unattractive due to competitive dynamics.

### DCF Valuation, Comparable Company Analysis, and Precedent Transaction Analysis
The three standard investment-banking valuation methodologies used to triangulate a defensible valuation range for any business.

## Pitfalls and Best Practices Across the Whole Process

**Pitfall 1: Falling in love with your first idea**
Most entrepreneurs anchor on the first idea that excites them and skip structured idea generation and prioritisation. The result: they spend 18 months building a business that a proper screening would have eliminated in two weeks. *Counter:* Complete the full Business Idea Prioritisation Matrix before committing a single dollar or hour of build time.

**Pitfall 2: Treating desk research as customer validation**
Reading industry reports and competitor websites tells you what the market looks like, not whether your specific customers will pay for your specific solution. *Counter:* Require ≥10 in-person or video-call interviews with real target customers before writing a business plan. If a customer won't give you 30 minutes, that is a signal about their problem severity.

**Pitfall 3: Writing the financial plan without having talked to a paying customer**
Spreadsheets are infinitely flexible and will prove any case you want them to make. A financial model built before customer validation is a work of fiction. *Counter:* Phase 3 must precede phase 4. Every major financial assumption must be traceable to a real data point — not an assumption borrowed from a comparable company.

**Pitfall 4: Optimising for valuation rather than terms**
Founders often focus entirely on the headline valuation and fail to read the downstream implications of a participating preferred liquidation preference, a full-ratchet anti-dilution clause, or an aggressive board composition. *Counter:* Build the exit waterfall for multiple scenarios before accepting any term sheet. Understand that a $10M valuation with founder-friendly terms is almost always better than a $15M valuation with investor-friendly terms.

**Pitfall 5: Treating financial analysis as a compliance exercise**
Many startup founders only look at the bank balance. *Counter:* Build and review all three financial statements monthly. The cash flow statement, specifically operating cash flow, is the purest signal of whether the business model itself is working.

**Pitfall 6: Surprising your board and investors**
The single fastest way to destroy investor trust is to surface a major problem for the first time at a board meeting. *Counter:* Communicate bad news early and proactively — frame it as 'here is what we have learned and here is what we are doing about it.' The monthly investor update is your primary tool for maintaining trust.

**Best Practice 1: Be iterative, not sequential**
The 8 phases are presented in order, but the process is explicitly iterative. Data from phase 3 updates phase 2. Operating data from phase 5 updates phases 2 and 4. Build feedback loops into your process calendar from day one.

**Best Practice 2: Define your metrics before you need them**
Before you launch, define every KPI you will track, the data source, the owner, and the target value. Founders who try to define metrics after they have data tend to pick metrics that flatter their current performance rather than metrics that predict future health.

**Best Practice 3: Treat cash runway as a constraint, not a comfort**
Sufficient cash runway enables good decision-making. When runway falls below 6 months, every decision becomes distorted by desperation. Maintain at least 12 months of runway and raise your next round when you have 12–18 months left — not 3.

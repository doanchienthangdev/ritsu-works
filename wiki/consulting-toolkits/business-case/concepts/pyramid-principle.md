# Pyramid Principle

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Business Case*

## What it is
A top-down communication structure in which the governing conclusion (the 'so what') is stated first, supported by 3–4 MECE arguments, each backed by evidence — the inverse of how analysis is built but the correct order for presenting to busy executives.

**Origin:** Barbara Minto, 'The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking' (1987), developed as a writing and communication standard at McKinsey & Company in the late 1960s and 1970s.

## Why it works
Executives read executive summaries, not appendices. If the conclusion is buried at the end of a lengthy analysis, most readers will never reach it — or will form the wrong impression from the journey. Stating the answer first, then proving it, respects the reader's time, forces the writer to have a clear point of view, and makes the logic testable: if the slide titles alone form a coherent argument, the structure is sound.

## When to use
For every board presentation, executive summary, written recommendation, or email to a senior executive where the answer matters.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Build the analysis bottom-up: run all the frameworks, collect all the data, find the answer.
2. Identify the single governing thought: what does all the evidence say the audience should do or believe? Write it as a complete sentence with a verb and a specific claim (e.g. 'We recommend funding Project X at $50M; it delivers NPV of $120M at 28% IRR and is consistent with our top strategic priority').
3. Group the supporting evidence into 3–4 MECE buckets — these become the second-level arguments. Each argument should be stated as a claim (e.g. 'Strategic alignment is strong across all three pillars'), not a topic ('Strategic Alignment').
4. For each argument, identify the 2–3 data exhibits that prove it. Each exhibit should appear on a slide with an action title that states the point the exhibit makes.
5. Write every slide title as an action statement — the argument the slide makes, not the topic it covers. Test: read only the slide titles. Does the logical argument come through without the presenter? If yes, the structure is right.
6. Open the verbal delivery with SCQ (Situation-Complication-Question) to establish context before the governing thought is stated.

## Real-life example — McKinsey (standard board presentation practice)
McKinsey's standard board presentation begins with the governing thought on slide 1: for example, 'Divesting the consumer division will unlock £2B in value and improve ROCE by 6 points within three years, and we recommend proceeding with a structured sale process immediately.' This is followed by three supporting arguments ('The consumer division is strategically non-core', 'The financial case is compelling at current valuations', 'Execution risk is manageable with the right buyer'), each backed by two or three exhibits. A McKinsey senior partner can review the deck by reading only the slide titles — and if the titles form a logical argument, the deck is ready for the client. This test is taught to new associates in their first week.

**So what:** Action-titled slides that read as a logical sequence — even without the presenter — are the hallmark of a top-tier business case. The test: read only titles.

## Template
Write the governing thought as a complete, specific claim. Write each second-level argument as an action statement. Write each slide title as the point the slide makes. Test by reading titles only.

- [ ] Governing Thought (Slide 1 title): [Full sentence recommendation or finding — includes the 'what' and 'so what'. E.g. 'Investing $50M in Project X delivers $120M NPV at 28% IRR and directly advances our Priority 1 strategic goal — we recommend Phase 1 approval today.']
- [ ] Argument 1 (Slide 2 title): [Action statement for first pillar. E.g. 'Strategic alignment is strong: Project X advances 3 of our 4 strategic priorities at a score of 8.2/10.']
- [ ] Argument 2 (Slide 3 title): [Action statement for second pillar. E.g. 'The financial case is compelling even in the downside scenario: NPV remains positive at $65M with a 4.5-year payback.']
- [ ] Argument 3 (Slide 4 title): [Action statement for third pillar. E.g. 'Implementation ease is manageable: an ease score of 3.8/5 indicates moderate complexity with a clear mitigation plan.']
- [ ] Supporting exhibit titles: [Each exhibit slide title should state what the exhibit shows. E.g. 'Revenue ramp is benchmarked against three comparable product launches — all above our base case target.']
- [ ] Test: Read only the slide titles in order. Does the governing thought → arguments → evidence form a logical, self-contained argument? Yes/No

## Pitfalls
- A 'mystery novel' structure that builds to the conclusion at the end — executives will ask 'what's the point?' before you get there. State the recommendation on slide 1.
- Topic-titled slides ('Strategic Alignment', 'Financial Model') that reveal nothing — fix: rewrite as claims ('Strategic alignment scores 8/10 across our top priorities').
- Too many arguments at the second level — more than 4–5 means the governing thought is not clear enough to be supported concisely. Consolidate until the logical structure is tight.

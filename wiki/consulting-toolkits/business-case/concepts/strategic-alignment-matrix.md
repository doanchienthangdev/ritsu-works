# Strategic Alignment Matrix

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Business Case*

## What it is
A scoring tool that measures how directly a proposed project or initiative supports each of the organisation's strategic priorities, producing a weighted alignment score that answers 'does this project belong in our portfolio?'

**Origin:** A practitioner tool developed within strategy consulting (McKinsey, BCG, Bain) and formalised in strategy execution literature, including Kaplan and Norton's Balanced Scorecard framework (1992) which introduced the concept of cascading strategic alignment from corporate strategy to initiatives.

## Why it works
An organisation has many compelling projects competing for limited capital. Without an explicit alignment test, investment decisions favour the loudest internal advocates or the most politically connected sponsors — not the most strategically important initiatives. The Strategic Alignment Matrix forces a structured comparison: how much does each project contribute to each strategic priority? The answer makes implicit strategic bias explicit and auditable.

## When to use
In Phase 2 (Step 2.1) to assess whether the project belongs in the portfolio; revisit when the corporate strategy changes.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List the organisation's current strategic priorities — typically 3–5 pillars from the corporate strategy document or last strategic plan. Use the exact language from the published strategy.
2. Assign weights to each priority that sum to 100%: the most important pillar gets the highest weight. Agree weights with the strategy team or sponsor before scoring.
3. Score the project's contribution to each priority on a 0–10 scale: 10 = this project directly delivers this priority; 5 = moderate contribution; 0 = no connection.
4. Compute the weighted score for each priority: weight × score.
5. Sum the weighted scores to get the overall strategic alignment rating (0–10). A score above 7 is strong; 4–7 is moderate; below 4 means the project needs strategic re-framing or should be deprioritised.
6. Write a one-sentence rationale for each score — this is what the board will ask about.
7. Also assess organisational capability alignment: does the company have the culture, skills, and operating model to execute this type of project? Score separately (0–10).

## Real-life example — Microsoft (shift to cloud-first strategy under Nadella, 2014)
When Satya Nadella became CEO in 2014, Microsoft ran every major initiative through strategic alignment scoring against the 'Mobile-first, Cloud-first' pivot. Office 365 scored 10/10 (direct cloud revenue) and 9/10 (mobile reach). The Windows Phone hardware business scored 3/10 on cloud contribution and 4/10 on mobile strategy coherence. The alignment matrix made the decision to write down Nokia (acquired for $7.2B in 2013) analytically defensible — it simply did not score well against the new strategic priorities. Nadella acknowledged this discipline publicly in his book 'Hit Refresh' (2017).

**So what:** Explicit strategic alignment scoring makes investment decisions auditable and defensible — and prevents sunk-cost bias from overriding strategic coherence.

## Pitfalls
- Scoring after the advocacy decision — teams score projects they have already decided to fund at 10/10 across the board. Fix: agree the scoring weights before any projects are evaluated.
- Missing capability alignment — a project can score 9/10 on strategic fit and still fail because the organisation cannot execute it. Score both strategic fit AND capability fit.
- Not using the actual corporate strategy document — scores that reference the strategy document are far more credible to a board than scores that reference what the team believes the strategy is.

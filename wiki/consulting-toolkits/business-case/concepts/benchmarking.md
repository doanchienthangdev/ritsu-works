# Benchmarking

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: Business Case*

## What it is
The practice of comparing project assumptions, performance targets, and cost estimates against peer organisations, industry data, or analogous past projects — to convert contested internal estimates into defensible, externally-grounded evidence.

**Origin:** Formalised in management practice by Robert Camp at Xerox in the 1980s ('Benchmarking: The Search for Industry Best Practices', 1989) after Xerox used competitor analysis to close a cost gap with Japanese rivals. Adopted as a standard tool in strategy consulting and financial modelling.

## Why it works
Internal estimates are optimistic by default — teams building a business case have an incentive to make the numbers work. External benchmarks introduce an independent reality check. A revenue assumption benchmarked against 'top-quartile performance in peer companies' is far more persuasive to a board than 'our commercial team believes it is achievable' — because it transfers the burden of proof from the project team to the external data.

## When to use
During Phase 2 (Step 2.2) value quantification; integrated into the financial model in Phase 3 (Step 3.2). Every material assumption should be benchmarked.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List every material assumption in the financial model — the 10–15 numbers that drive most of the NPV.
2. For each assumption, identify the best available benchmark source: (a) industry research (Gartner, IDC, McKinsey Global Institute), (b) comparable past projects in the same organisation, (c) peer company public filings or benchmarking databases.
3. Record the benchmark range and the source. If no external benchmark exists, note 'internal estimate — validated by [expert name/date]'.
4. Compare the internal estimate to the benchmark range: is it conservative (below the range), within range, or optimistic (above the range)?
5. Flag optimistic assumptions (above benchmark) for sensitivity analysis in the downside scenario. Present them to the board with explicit acknowledgement: 'This assumption is above the industry average; here is why we believe it is achievable, and here is the downside scenario if we are wrong.'
6. Use benchmarks in the presentation slides — cite the source on each slide. A benchmarked assumption is far harder to dismiss than an internal estimate.

## Real-life example — Walmart (e-commerce investment business case, 2016)
When Walmart built the internal business case for its acquisition of Jet.com and subsequent e-commerce acceleration investment, the team benchmarked every assumption against Amazon's scaling trajectory and Target's digital conversion rates. The customer acquisition cost assumption ($35 per digital customer) was benchmarked against Amazon's $25–40 range (from SEC filings and analyst research). The revenue ramp assumption was benchmarked against Target's digital revenue growth of 40% per year after their omnichannel investment in 2013. Benchmarked assumptions enabled Walmart's board to approve the $3.3B Jet.com acquisition with a shared understanding of what 'reasonable' looked like.

**So what:** Benchmarked assumptions shift the discussion from 'do we trust the team?' to 'do we accept the external evidence?' — a much stronger position for gaining board approval.

## Pitfalls
- Cherry-picking benchmarks that validate the preferred number — use the full benchmark range (low to high) and present honestly. A board that catches cherry-picking will question the entire model.
- Outdated benchmarks — a 2015 benchmark for cloud adoption rates is useless in 2025. Check the publication date and note it.
- No benchmarks at all — 'our team believes X is achievable' is not sufficient for a board investment decision on any assumption above $1M NPV impact.

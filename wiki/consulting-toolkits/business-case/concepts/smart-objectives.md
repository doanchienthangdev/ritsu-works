# SMART Objectives

*Category: planning · Toolkit: Business Case*

## What it is
A method for writing measurable, accountable objectives: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — each with a KPI, a baseline, and a target.

**Origin:** George T. Doran, 'There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives', Management Review, November 1981.

## Why it works
Vague objectives cannot be tracked, reported, or defended to a board. Every word of the SMART criteria closes a specific accountability gap: Specific eliminates ambiguity; Measurable enables tracking; Achievable prevents fantasy; Relevant aligns to strategy; Time-bound creates urgency and enables a definitive success/failure verdict.

## When to use
When translating a proposed solution into a trackable, board-reportable commitment — always before Phase 2 assessment begins.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Write each objective as an outcome (a state of the world), not an activity (a task). 'Reduce churn to 5%' is an outcome; 'Conduct customer interviews' is an activity.
2. Attach exactly one KPI — the metric that will be reported to the board at each review cycle.
3. Set a baseline: where are you today? Without a baseline, improvement cannot be measured or reported.
4. Set a target: ambitious but achievable — benchmark against comparable organisations or past projects to validate realism.
5. Set a deadline and an owner; without both, an objective is a wish. Assign one named individual as the owner.
6. Limit total objectives to 3–5: more than 5 dilutes focus and makes the board presentation unwieldy.

## Real-life example — Google (OKRs as SMART operationalised at scale)
Google's OKR (Objectives and Key Results) system is SMART institutionalised: every objective has 3–5 key results with specific numerical targets and quarterly deadlines. Sundar Pichai's company-level OKRs cascade to every team, and each KR is scored 0–1.0 publicly each quarter. Google employees describe OKRs as the system that lets 100,000+ people coordinate without constant top-down direction — the SMART criteria in action at scale.

**So what:** Measurable, time-bound objectives with public scoring create accountability at any scale — and give the board a concrete, unambiguous success criterion.

## Template
Complete one row per objective. The KPI must be a single, quantifiable metric. The baseline must be verified, not estimated. The target must be benchmarked against peers or prior projects.

- [ ] Objective number: [1, 2, 3…]
- [ ] Objective statement: [An outcome, e.g. 'Increase net revenue retention in Enterprise segment from 105% to 120%']
- [ ] KPI: [The metric, e.g. 'Net Revenue Retention %']
- [ ] Baseline: [Current verified value, e.g. '105% (Q3 2024 actuals)']
- [ ] Target: [Desired value, e.g. '120%']
- [ ] Source for target: [Benchmark, e.g. 'Top-quartile SaaS: 120–130% per KeyBanc SaaS Survey 2024']
- [ ] Deadline: [Date, e.g. 'Q4 2026']
- [ ] Owner: [Named individual, e.g. 'VP Customer Success — Jane Smith']
- [ ] Measurement cadence: [Monthly / Quarterly]

## Pitfalls
- Objectives that describe activities ('conduct a market study') rather than outcomes ('increase market share to X%') — fix: rewrite as a state of the world, not a task.
- Too many objectives — more than 5 dilutes focus. More than 3 per person creates paralysis. Prioritise ruthlessly.
- Targets without benchmarks — a board will ask 'why is 5% the right target?' without a peer comparison, the answer is unconvincing.

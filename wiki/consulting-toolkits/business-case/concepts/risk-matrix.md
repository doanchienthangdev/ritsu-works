# Risk Matrix

*Category: risk · Toolkit: Business Case*

## What it is
A tool for identifying, rating, and prioritising project risks by plotting each on a Likelihood × Impact grid, then defining mitigation and contingency actions for each critical risk.

**Origin:** A standard risk-management tool formalised in PMI's PMBOK and ISO 31000:2018 Risk Management Guidelines. Widely used in project governance since the 1990s.

## Why it works
A board will not fund a project that ignores risk. Systematically listing, rating, and mitigating risks demonstrates that the team has thought rigorously about what could go wrong — and builds credibility for the financial model, which must reflect a risk-adjusted picture. The matrix forces prioritisation: not all risks deserve equal attention, and scarce mitigation resources should go to the highest-score quadrant.

## When to use
When building a business case; when presenting to any governance body that will ask 'what could go wrong?'; revisit monthly throughout project execution.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Brainstorm risks in four categories: execution risks (timeline, resources, key-person dependency), market risks (demand, competition, price), technology risks (integration, reliability, data), and regulatory risks (approvals, compliance, legal).
2. Rate each risk: Likelihood (1–5) × Impact (1–5) = Risk Score.
3. Plot on the matrix; focus mitigation resources on the top-right quadrant (high × high, score ≥15).
4. For each critical risk (score ≥15), assign a named owner and define: (a) a mitigation action to reduce likelihood or impact before the risk materialises, and (b) a contingency action — what you do if the risk happens despite mitigation.
5. Present the risk register to the board alongside the financial model and link the downside scenario to the top-right quadrant risks.

## Real-life example — Netflix (DVD-to-Streaming Transition, 2007)
Netflix's internal risk assessment for streaming flagged three critical risks: bandwidth availability (high likelihood in 2007), content licensing cost (high impact — studios could price streaming rights prohibitively), and customer adoption speed (uncertain). Each had a named owner, a mitigation (ISP partnerships; staged content deals; freemium trial offer), and a contingency (maintained DVD as an ongoing hedge until streaming reached critical mass). The risk discipline let leadership commit to the transformation without betting the company — the DVD business provided the cash flow while streaming scaled.

**So what:** A structured risk register with mitigations and contingencies signals board-level maturity and protects the financial model's credibility by linking risk to scenarios.

## Template
List 5–10 risks. Rate each on Likelihood (1–5) and Impact (1–5). Compute score. For every score ≥15, assign an owner, a mitigation action, and a contingency action. Revisit monthly.

- [ ] Risk ID: [R1, R2…]
- [ ] Risk description: [Specific event that could happen — not a category]
- [ ] Category: [Execution / Market / Technology / Regulatory]
- [ ] Likelihood (1–5): [Rating with rationale]
- [ ] Impact (1–5): [Rating with rationale]
- [ ] Risk Score: [Likelihood × Impact]
- [ ] Owner: [Named individual]
- [ ] Mitigation action: [What we do now to reduce likelihood or impact]
- [ ] Contingency action: [What we do if the risk materialises despite mitigation]
- [ ] Status: [Open / Mitigated / Closed]

## Pitfalls
- Generic risk labels ('market risk', 'technology risk') with no specific trigger — name the specific event: 'Key supplier X has one production line; if it goes down, we lose 40% of supply for 6 weeks.'
- Risk register without owners — every critical risk must have a named person responsible for both mitigation and contingency.
- Updating the register only at project initiation — the register must be reviewed at every steering committee and updated as risks materialise or evolve.

# Prioritisation Matrix (Value vs Ease)

*Category: decision · Toolkit: Business Case*

## What it is
A 2x2 matrix that plots all investment candidates simultaneously on two axes — Value (strategic and financial impact) and Ease of Implementation — to force a portfolio investment call rather than evaluating each project in isolation.

**Origin:** A standard portfolio-management and PMO tool; the Value-vs-Effort (or Impact-vs-Complexity) matrix is widely attributed to consulting practice at McKinsey and BCG. The 2x2 format follows the tradition of the BCG Growth-Share Matrix (1968) and the GE-McKinsey Nine-Box Matrix (1970s).

## Why it works
Organisations have more compelling projects than available capital. Evaluating each project individually — even with rigorous Phase 2 assessment — produces sub-optimal portfolios because trade-offs are invisible. The prioritisation matrix makes the trade-offs visible by forcing all candidates onto the same axes simultaneously, where the committee can see the portfolio implications of every funding decision.

## When to use
In Phase 3 (Step 3.1) when the executive committee must choose among competing investment requests; revisit quarterly as part of portfolio governance.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Gather Phase 2 assessment outputs for all investment candidates: the Value score (from strategic alignment + value driver tree) and the Ease score (from the Implementation Ease Scorecard).
2. Normalise scores to a 0–10 scale if the source assessments use different scales.
3. Plot all candidates on the matrix simultaneously — every project in the portfolio, not just the one being proposed.
4. Group the matrix: top-right (Quick Wins) = fund first; top-left (Major Projects) = sequence carefully, phase the investment; bottom-right (Fill-Ins) = do only if capacity allows; bottom-left (Time Sinks) = kill or defer.
5. Draw a 'funding line': given the available capital budget, where does funding stop? Projects above the line are funded; below the line are deferred.
6. Present the full matrix to the board — it shows that the recommendation is not just 'this is a good project' but 'this is the best use of capital relative to all alternatives.'

## Real-life example — P&G (Brand Portfolio Rationalisation under A.G. Lafley, 2014)
CEO A.G. Lafley used explicit value-vs-complexity portfolio logic to cut P&G from 170+ brands to ~65 core brands. High-value, lower-complexity global brands (Tide, Pampers, Gillette, Always) received full investment — they landed in the 'Quick Win' or 'Major Project' quadrant. Low-value, high-complexity regional brands (many acquired during the Gillette era) were sold or discontinued. The systematic portfolio view enabled $10B in divestiture proceeds, accelerated organic growth, and lifted ROCE — outcomes that project-by-project evaluation could never have achieved.

**So what:** A portfolio view of investment candidates forces trade-offs that project-by-project evaluation cannot — and produces better capital allocation decisions.

## Template

## Pitfalls
- Subjective scoring that reflects political preferences — counter: use the quantified Phase 2 assessment outputs as the inputs, not gut feel from individual managers.
- Evaluating only the project being proposed — counter: all competing investment candidates must be on the same matrix, or the trade-off logic is invisible.
- Not revisiting quarterly — the matrix is only as current as the assessment data. As strategic priorities shift or new projects emerge, re-plot the matrix.

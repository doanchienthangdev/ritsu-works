# SCQ — Situation, Complication, Question

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Business Case*

## What it is
A three-sentence narrative structure that opens any communication by establishing shared facts (Situation), introducing the tension (Complication), and posing the question the rest of the document answers — followed by the governing answer.

**Origin:** Barbara Minto, 'The Pyramid Principle' (1987), developed as an internal McKinsey communication standard.

## Why it works
Decision-makers are busy and defensive. Opening with established facts (Situation) creates agreement; the Complication creates urgency; the Question signals that what follows is the answer. This structure earns attention before making any claim — the opposite of leading with a conclusion the audience hasn't yet accepted.

## When to use
At the opening of every business case, board presentation, proposal, or executive email.

## Visual
`staircase`

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Write the Situation: the stable, factual backdrop that all readers accept without argument (e.g. 'Our cost base has grown 18% over three years while revenue grew only 9%').
2. Write the Complication: the specific event or trend that disrupts the situation and demands action (e.g. 'A new entrant with 30% lower prices captured 8 points of market share last quarter').
3. Write the Question: the implicit question the complication forces (e.g. 'How do we restore competitiveness while protecting margin?').
4. State the Answer/Governing Thought immediately after: the single claim that answers the question (e.g. 'We recommend a two-year cost transformation focused on procurement and shared services').
5. Test: can a reader who reads only these four sentences understand the argument? If yes, the opening works.

## Real-life example — Apple (iPhone launch keynote, 2007)
Jobs opened with SCQ precision: Situation — 'The most advanced phones out there are called smartphones.' Complication — 'They are not so smart. They have the problem of using a stylus or a keyboard that's there whether you want it or not. And if you think of a great idea six months from now, you can't add it.' Question — 'How do we create a phone that is far more powerful than anything out there, that is easy to use?' Answer — 'We're introducing a revolutionary and magical product.' Every subsequent slide proved the answer. The audience was with him before the first feature was shown.

**So what:** A crisp SCQ opening earns the audience's attention before any data is shown — and sets the governing thought that everything else supports.

## Pitfalls
- Embedding the solution in the Complication — the complication describes a problem, not a fix. Save the solution for the Answer.
- Making the Situation too long — it must be accepted, not argued. One to three sentences maximum.
- A generic complication ('the market is changing') — it must be specific and urgent enough that the Question follows inevitably.

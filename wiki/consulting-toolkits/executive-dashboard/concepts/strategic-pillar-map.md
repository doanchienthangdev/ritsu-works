# Strategic Pillar Map

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Executive Dashboard*

## What it is
A structured decomposition of the company's overall strategic intent into 3–5 named pillars, each representing a distinct strategic priority area (e.g., Growth, Customer Excellence, Digital Transformation, People). Each pillar is associated with flagship initiatives and outcome KPIs.

**Origin:** Derived from the strategic planning practice of grouping strategic objectives into thematic clusters, as recommended in the McKinsey Strategic Planning Frameworks and the Kaplan-Norton Strategy Maps (2004). Widely used in corporate strategy offices as the organising structure for annual plans and board presentations.

## Why it works
Strategy is most powerful when it is concentrated — a company cannot outperform on 20 dimensions simultaneously. The pillar structure forces strategic choices by limiting the number of priority themes. Grouping initiatives by pillar also creates accountability clarity: each pillar has an executive sponsor, and the performance of initiatives within the pillar rolls up to that sponsor's scorecard.

## When to use
Use at the start of Phase 4 (Strategic Plan Dashboard build) to structure the roadmap. Also use in annual planning to allocate investment across strategic priorities.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Read the company's approved strategy document and extract the 3–5 strategic themes the leadership team has committed to.
2. Name each theme as a 'pillar' with a short 2–3 word label (e.g., 'Profitable Growth', 'Customer First', 'Operational Excellence').
3. For each pillar, list the 3–6 flagship initiatives that operationalise the strategy within that pillar.
4. For each initiative, identify the primary outcome KPI — the metric that will change if the initiative succeeds.
5. Assign a distinct colour to each pillar; use this colour consistently across the roadmap chart, initiative ROI scatter, and project portfolio table.
6. Validate the pillar map with the CEO: ask 'If we execute all initiatives in Pillar X, does that deliver the strategic outcome we have committed to? What is missing?'

## Real-life example — Marks & Spencer (M&S) — 'Reshape for Growth' strategy, 2020
M&S's turnaround strategy was structured around three pillars: Food (maintaining food margin and introducing new ranges), Clothing & Home (reducing store estate, improving range), and Digital & Data (accelerating online penetration). Each pillar had 3–5 flagship programmes and clear outcome KPIs (Food: gross margin %; Clothing: online revenue %: Digital: data analytics capability index). The three-pillar structure gave every manager a clear line of sight from their day-to-day work to the strategic pillar it contributed to.

**So what:** Strategic pillars work because they make the strategy legible throughout the organisation — every employee can see which pillar their work belongs to and therefore why it matters to the company's future.

## Pitfalls
- Creating more than 5 pillars — with more than 5 pillars, the strategy is effectively saying 'everything is a priority', which means nothing is. If the organisation's strategy genuinely requires more than 5 themes, consolidate or make hard choices.
- Pillar names that describe activities rather than outcomes — 'Digital Transformation' is an activity; 'Digital-Enabled Customer Experience' is an outcome. Name pillars after the change you want to see, not the work you will do.
- Not assigning an executive sponsor per pillar — without a named owner, the pillar is an aspiration, not a commitment.

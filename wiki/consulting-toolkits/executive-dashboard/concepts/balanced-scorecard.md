# Balanced Scorecard

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Executive Dashboard*

## What it is
A strategic performance measurement framework that structures KPIs across four perspectives — Financial, Customer, Internal Process, and Learning & Growth — ensuring that financial results are contextualised alongside the operational and people indicators that drive them.

**Origin:** Developed by Robert S. Kaplan (Harvard Business School) and David P. Norton in their landmark Harvard Business Review article 'The Balanced Scorecard — Measures That Drive Performance' (January–February 1992). Further developed in *The Balanced Scorecard* (1996) and *Strategy Maps* (2004). One of the most cited management frameworks of the 20th century.

## Why it works
Managing a business using only financial KPIs is analogous to flying an aircraft using only the fuel gauge. Financial results are lagging indicators — by the time they deteriorate, the underlying operational and people problems have been accumulating for months. The Balanced Scorecard forces the inclusion of leading indicators (Customer: NPS, Customer Satisfaction; Internal Process: Cycle Time, Quality; Learning & Growth: Employee Engagement, Training Hours) that predict future financial results. The four-perspective structure embodies a causal hypothesis: L&G improvements → better processes → better customer outcomes → better financial results.

## When to use
Use in Phase 4 of the Executive Dashboard build to structure the strategic plan's KPIs across the four perspectives. Also use when a new CEO or strategy director is designing the organisation's performance management system from scratch.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start with the company's strategic objectives (from the strategy document). Map each objective to one of the four perspectives.
2. For each perspective, identify 3–5 KPIs that measure progress toward the strategic objectives in that perspective — use the SMART-KPI filter.
3. Arrange the perspectives in the standard 2×2 grid with Financial top-left, Customer top-right, Internal Process bottom-left, Learning & Growth bottom-right.
4. For each KPI, create a mini-scorecard tile showing: KPI name, current value, target, variance, RAG indicator, and a 12-month trend sparkline.
5. Draw causal linkage arrows between the perspectives to visualise the hypothesis: improving Employee Engagement (L&G) should reduce Defect Rate (IP) which should improve Customer Satisfaction (Customer) which should grow Revenue (Financial).
6. Review the causal linkages quarterly: test whether leading indicators are actually predicting financial results by calculating the correlation with a 3-month lag.
7. Present the Balanced Scorecard page to the board as the 'dashboard behind the dashboard' — the financial results on one page; the drivers of those results on the Balanced Scorecard page.

## Real-life example — Mobil Oil (now ExxonMobil) — Division-Level Balanced Scorecard, 1993–1998
Mobil Oil was one of the first companies to implement the Balanced Scorecard at scale, beginning in 1993 under CFO Bob McCool. Mobil's North American Marketing & Refining division implemented scorecards for its 18 business units, each covering the four perspectives. The Financial perspective targeted ROCE; the Customer perspective targeted 'Share of Target Customer Segment'; the Internal Process perspective targeted refinery efficiency; the Learning & Growth perspective targeted employee skill development. Within 3 years, Mobil moved from the bottom quartile of industry profitability to the top. The causal logic — investing in people → improving processes → improving customer service → financial outperformance — was validated empirically.

**So what:** Mobil's experience demonstrates the Balanced Scorecard's core hypothesis: financial results are the outcome of the operational and people investments you make; measuring and managing the leading indicators is what creates the financial results, not managing the results themselves.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the Balanced Scorecard as a KPI list rather than a causal model — the four-perspective structure only adds value if the causal linkages between perspectives are identified and tested. A scorecard without arrows is just a dashboard.
- Including too many KPIs per perspective — Kaplan and Norton recommend no more than 4–5 KPIs per perspective (16–20 total). More than 25 KPIs in a Balanced Scorecard indicates the organisation has not made strategic choices.
- Cascading the corporate scorecard to business units without customising the KPIs — a corporate customer KPI (e.g., market share globally) is meaningless for a regional business unit; cascade the perspective, not the metric.

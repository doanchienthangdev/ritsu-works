# Pyramid Principle (SCQA / Minto Pyramid)

*Category: communication · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A structured thinking and communication framework that organises any business message as a pyramid: the answer (recommendation) at the top, supported by three governing thoughts, each proven by evidence below — so the audience receives the conclusion first and evidence only if needed.

**Origin:** Barbara Minto, McKinsey & Company, 1966–1973. Formalised in her 1987 book 'The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking'. Became the standard communication framework at McKinsey and most major consulting firms.

## Why it works
The brain processes information more efficiently when it receives the conclusion first and then the supporting logic, rather than building up to a conclusion through evidence. The pyramid structure ensures every message is 'answer-first' and every level of the pyramid answers the question raised by the level above it (Minto's 'So What?' test). The SCQA variant (Situation–Complication–Question–Answer) creates a narrative entry point that aligns the audience on context before delivering the recommendation.

## When to use
Use for any situation where you need to persuade a senior audience: business cases, strategy recommendations, board presentations, investor decks, and executive memos. Particularly powerful when the audience is time-constrained and resistant to detail.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Define the Key Question your document must answer (e.g., 'Should we launch a digital transformation programme?').
2. 2. Write the SCQA entry: Situation (2 sentences of agreed context), Complication (the tension or trigger — what changed), Question (the question this creates), Answer (your recommendation — this becomes the pyramid apex).
3. 3. Draft the Governing Idea at the apex: a single sentence that directly answers the Key Question (e.g., 'We recommend launching a 3-year digital transformation programme with a £65M investment to capture £180M in NPV').
4. 4. Apply the 'So What?' test upward: for each supporting point, ask 'So what does this mean for the level above?' — if you cannot answer, the point does not belong in the pyramid.
5. 5. Identify exactly 3 Key Lines (governing thoughts) that, taken together, prove the Governing Idea. Group your evidence under these 3 thoughts.
6. 6. Populate the evidence tier: use data, analyses, benchmarks, or case studies. Each piece of evidence must prove its Key Line, not just be related to it.
7. 7. Apply the MECE test: check that the Key Lines are Mutually Exclusive (no overlap) and Collectively Exhaustive (cover all necessary ground).
8. 8. Write the document top-down: title = Governing Idea; H2 headings = Key Lines; body = evidence. Executive summary replicates the full pyramid in miniature.

## Real-life example — McKinsey & Company (client: major European bank)
McKinsey consultants presenting a cost reduction strategy to a European bank's Board used the Pyramid Principle as follows: Governing Idea: 'Reduce cost-to-income ratio from 68% to 55% within 3 years by digitising retail banking operations.' Key Line 1: 'Branch digitalisation eliminates £240M of avoidable cost.' Key Line 2: 'Straight-through processing of 70% of loan applications reduces processing time by 80%.' Key Line 3: 'Cloud migration of core banking reduces infrastructure cost by £80M.' Each Key Line was then supported by market benchmarks, internal diagnostics, and detailed financial models. The Board approved the programme in a 45-minute meeting because the structure allowed them to engage at the level of abstraction they chose.

**So what:** The Pyramid Principle reduces the cognitive load on senior decision-makers. In high-stakes engagements, the ability to deliver the answer in the first sentence — and prove it only as deeply as the audience needs — dramatically increases approval rates and reduces revision cycles.

## Pitfalls
- Starting with the data instead of the answer: consultants under pressure often present all the analysis first and the conclusion last — the audience loses interest or draws their own (wrong) conclusion before you arrive at yours. Always lead with the answer.
- Three Key Lines that are not MECE: overlapping Key Lines cause confusion; gaps create vulnerability to challenge. Apply the MECE test explicitly before writing.
- Key Lines that are descriptive rather than argumentative: 'The market is growing' is a fact; 'Market growth makes this the ideal moment to invest' is a Key Line. Each Key Line must make an argument, not just describe a situation.
- Using the Pyramid for operational documents: the Pyramid Principle is optimised for persuasion and decision-making. For operational guides, instruction manuals, or how-to content, a sequential (process-based) structure often works better.

# Stakeholder Influence-Interest Matrix

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A 2×2 framework that maps stakeholders on two axes — Influence (power to affect the programme) and Interest (degree of concern about the programme's outcomes) — to prioritise engagement and tailor communication strategies.

**Origin:** Derived from Freeman's stakeholder theory (1984) and the Power-Interest grid popularised by Mendelow (1991) and extensively used in project management (PMI, Prince2) and consulting from the 1990s onwards.

## Why it works
Not all stakeholders require the same type or intensity of engagement. High influence/high interest stakeholders (Key Players) require intensive management — they can either champion or derail the programme. High influence/low interest stakeholders (Keep Satisfied) must be managed carefully — they are potential vetoers who may not be actively engaged but can block progress. Low influence/high interest (Keep Informed) are important for morale and word-of-mouth. Low influence/low interest (Monitor) require minimal attention. The matrix converts a stakeholder list into a prioritised engagement strategy.

## When to use
Use at the start of Phase IV (Change Management) to map and prioritise stakeholders, and at the beginning of each major project deployment. Also use in Phase I before presenting the Business Case for Change — knowing who the key decision influencers are is essential for winning Board approval.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Identify all stakeholders: brainstorm every individual or group with a stake in the digital transformation — internal (Board, ExCo, business units, IT, HR) and external (key customers, suppliers, regulators).
2. 2. Score each stakeholder on Influence (1–5): how much power do they have to affect the programme's success? (Board = 5; individual contributor = 1).
3. 3. Score each stakeholder on Interest (1–5): how concerned are they about the programme's outcomes? (someone whose job is directly affected = 5; someone in an unaffected business unit = 1).
4. 4. Plot on the matrix: place each stakeholder using their Influence (y) and Interest (x) scores.
5. 5. Classify into quadrants: Key Players (>3,>3), Keep Satisfied (>3,≤3), Keep Informed (≤3,>3), Monitor (≤3,≤3).
6. 6. Profile Key Players: for each Key Player, document their current attitude (Champion/Neutral/Resistant) and the primary WIIFM (What's In It For Me) message. Assign a named relationship owner.
7. 7. Design engagement plans per quadrant: Key Players — co-design involvement, steering committee membership, regular 1:1s with sponsor; Keep Satisfied — monthly briefings, no surprises; Keep Informed — newsletter, town halls; Monitor — general communications only.
8. 8. Reassess quarterly: attitudes change as the programme progresses. A previously neutral Key Player who becomes resistant after a negative experience requires immediate escalation.

## Real-life example — NHS England (National Health Service)
NHS England's digital transformation of patient records (NHS App, FHIR API integration) identified clinical staff (doctors, nurses) as the most critical stakeholder group: high influence (clinical adoption drives usage), high interest (directly affects their workflow). Initial attitude: mostly resistant (past NHS IT failures, privacy concerns, time cost). The engagement plan for clinical staff: (1) Awareness — respected Chief Medical Officer championed the programme publicly; (2) Desire — GPDPs (GP Digital Pioneers) programme paid early-adopter GPs to co-design and advocate; (3) the clinical interface was redesigned based on clinician feedback from the pilot. By 2022, the NHS App had 29 million registered users. The pivotal decision was classifying clinicians as Key Players requiring co-design involvement, not merely 'Keep Informed' recipients of change communications.

**So what:** The Stakeholder Matrix's most important output is the engagement strategy differentiation — not everyone gets the same message or the same depth of involvement. The NHS App's success was built on identifying that clinicians were Key Players requiring co-design, not passive recipients.

## Pitfalls
- Only mapping internal stakeholders — external stakeholders (key customers, regulators, suppliers) are often more influential than internal ones for customer-facing digital transformation.
- Static classification — reassess quarterly; a supportive Key Player who experiences a failed go-live can become resistant quickly and must be re-engaged before they influence others.
- WIIFM messages that are corporate, not personal — 'the organisation will benefit from efficiency gains' is not a WIIFM. 'You will spend 2 fewer hours per week on manual data entry' is.

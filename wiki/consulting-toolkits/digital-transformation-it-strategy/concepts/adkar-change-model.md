# ADKAR Change Model

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A goal-oriented change management model from Prosci that defines five sequential outcomes required for successful individual change: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement — used to design targeted interventions for each stage of the change journey.

**Origin:** Developed by Jeff Hiatt at Prosci, first published in 1996 based on research into what differentiates successful change implementations. ADKAR stands for Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement. Now the most widely-adopted individual change model globally.

## Why it works
ADKAR is grounded in the insight that organisational change happens one person at a time. You can deploy the best technology in the world, but if individuals do not know why it is happening (Awareness), do not want to change (Desire), do not know how to use it (Knowledge), cannot use it effectively (Ability), or revert to old ways after go-live (Reinforcement), the change fails. ADKAR is sequential: Awareness before Desire, Knowledge before Ability. Interventions must target the lowest-scoring stage for each individual or group — otherwise resources are wasted on stages that are not the bottleneck.

## When to use
Use throughout Phase IV (Change Management) for all major digital transformation initiatives. Particularly critical for: ERP implementations, operating model changes, new ways of working (agile), AI deployments where staff fear job displacement.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Segment stakeholders: group the people affected by the change into 4–6 cohorts (e.g., branch staff, buying team, finance team). Each cohort will have different ADKAR profiles.
2. 2. Assess each cohort's ADKAR stage: use the 5-question ADKAR survey (one question per stage, rated 1–5). The lowest-scoring stage is the 'building block' — the bottleneck to address first.
3. 3. Design Awareness interventions: for cohorts with Awareness <3, create compelling 'why' communications. Use the CEO or most credible leader as messenger. Include both organisational rationale (why the company is changing) and personal relevance (what it means for their role).
4. 4. Design Desire interventions: for cohorts with Desire <3 despite Awareness, investigate resistance sources. Common causes: job security concerns, past change failures, WIIFM not clear. Address with direct dialogue, co-design involvement, and success stories from early adopters.
5. 5. Design Knowledge interventions: identify the specific skills and knowledge needed (new system, new process, new behaviours). Design a blended learning curriculum: e-learning for basics, instructor-led for complex skills, job aids for day-to-day reference.
6. 6. Design Ability interventions: schedule go-live support (hypercare team, floor walkers, support hotline). Build a 'safe to fail' practice environment. Set realistic performance targets for the first 90 days post-go-live.
7. 7. Design Reinforcement interventions: update performance targets to include new behaviours. Establish recognition for early adopters. Schedule a 90-day post-implementation review. Monitor reverting behaviours and address promptly.
8. 8. Track monthly: run a change readiness pulse survey and score each cohort on each ADKAR stage. Plot against a target readiness curve aligned to the deployment calendar. Escalate to Programme Board any cohort below threshold 4 weeks before go-live.

## Real-life example — Boeing
Boeing's transition to digital engineering tools (Model-Based Systems Engineering, MBSE) in the 2010s illustrates ADKAR in practice. The initial deployment (Awareness only — 'we are moving to MBSE') failed because engineers had Desire <2 (most believed the traditional drawing-based approach was superior and could not see personal benefit). Boeing redesigned the change programme: Desire — engineers were invited to co-author the MBSE standards (involvement created ownership); Knowledge — a 6-week MBSE certification programme was introduced; Ability — a dedicated MBSE Support Centre provided real-time help during the first year; Reinforcement — MBSE adoption rate was added to team leader performance targets. Within 3 years, MBSE adoption reached 78% across design teams, compared to 12% under the original Awareness-only approach.

**So what:** ADKAR's diagnostic power is in identifying the bottleneck stage. Boeing's initial deployment had perfect Awareness but failed at Desire — adding more Awareness communications would have made no difference. Diagnosing the correct bottleneck stage and designing targeted interventions for that stage is the entire value of the model.

## Pitfalls
- Treating ADKAR as a communications plan — ADKAR is not just about communications; Desire and Ability require fundamentally different interventions (co-design, hypercare) that comms alone cannot provide.
- Applying the same intervention to all cohorts — different cohorts will be at different ADKAR stages; a one-size-fits-all approach is inefficient and often counterproductive.
- Starting ADKAR too late — ADKAR launched 3 months before go-live cannot overcome 18 months of accumulated resistance. Launch ADKAR in parallel with Phase II strategy definition.
- Failing to diagnose the bottleneck stage — adding more Awareness content when the bottleneck is Desire wastes resources. Score each cohort on each stage before designing interventions.

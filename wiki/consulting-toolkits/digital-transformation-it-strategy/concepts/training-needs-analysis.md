# Training Needs Analysis (TNA)

*Category: change-management · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A structured assessment that identifies the skills gaps created by digital transformation initiatives, defines the required training interventions, and designs a blended learning curriculum that builds the Knowledge and Ability required for ADKAR-based change adoption.

**Origin:** Training Needs Analysis was formalised in HRD (Human Resource Development) practice by Boydell (1971) and expanded by Goldstein (1993). Applied to digital transformation change management from the 2010s as the scope of workforce upskilling required by digital programmes became clear.

## Why it works
Digital transformation requires workers to develop new skills at scale — new systems, new processes, new digital literacies. Without a structured TNA, training is typically under-resourced (organisations underestimate the training volume required by 40–60%) and poorly targeted (generic digital literacy training delivered to specialists who need specific system training). The TNA ensures training investment is proportional to the actual skills gap and targeted to the specific capabilities each role needs.

## When to use
Use in Phase IV Step 5 (Build Capability and Training Programmes) after the Change Impact Assessment identifies skills gaps for each affected role.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Map affected roles: from the Change Impact Assessment, identify all roles with significant process or system changes.
2. 2. Define required skills (to-be) for each role: what must each role be able to do after the digital change? Be specific (e.g., 'Process a customer refund using the new CRM system without supervisor assistance').
3. 3. Assess current skills (as-is) for each role: survey or interview role representatives. For technical skills, use a brief skills test.
4. 4. Calculate the gap: Required – Current = Training Need. Classify gaps as: Knowledge (they need to know new information), Skill (they need to practise a new process), or Attitude (they need to change behaviour).
5. 5. Design interventions for each gap type: Knowledge → e-learning module (30–60 min); Skill → instructor-led workshop with practice scenarios (half-day); Attitude → coaching, change manager intervention, peer conversations.
6. 6. Select the delivery format: blended is usually optimal — e-learning for foundational knowledge (accessible, scalable, trackable), ILT for complex skills (interactive, scenario-based), job aids for day-to-day reference (one-page quick reference cards), coaching for leaders.
7. 7. Sequence training: system training should occur no more than 2 weeks before go-live — training too early is forgotten; too late does not build ability before the go-live gate.
8. 8. Define completion targets and track: 90% completion before go-live gate is the standard target. Track by role group and escalate any group below 80% at 2 weeks before go-live.

## Real-life example — Lloyds Banking Group (retail banking digital transformation)
Lloyds' retail banking digital transformation identified that branch advisers needed 3 distinct training interventions: (1) New CRM system proficiency (e-learning 90 min + ILT half-day); (2) Digital conversation skills — how to demonstrate digital products to customers (ILT 1 day with role-play scenarios); (3) Self-service product knowledge — to support customers using the Lloyds mobile app (e-learning 45 min). The TNA identified that digital conversation skills had the largest gap (average: 1.8/5; required: 3.5/5) and would take the longest to build. This insight moved the ILT programme to Month 3 of the programme (not Month 10 as originally planned), preventing a bottleneck at go-live.

**So what:** The TNA's most valuable output is the sequencing insight: which skill has the largest gap and the longest training lead time? This skill must be trained earliest. Lloyds' digital conversation skills required 6 months of practice to reach competence — starting training in Month 3, not Month 10, was the TNA's primary contribution.

## Pitfalls
- One-size-fits-all training — a generic 'digital literacy' programme delivered to all roles fails to address the specific skill gaps of each role group.
- Training too early — training delivered >4 weeks before go-live is substantially forgotten by go-live; the optimal window is 1–2 weeks before.
- Completion rate as the measure of success — 90% completion at the right time does not guarantee 90% competence; include a competency assessment or a supervised practice session.

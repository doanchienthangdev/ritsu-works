# Design Thinking (5-Stage Human-Centred Process)

*Category: innovation · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A human-centred problem-solving methodology with five stages — Empathise, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test — that begins with deep user research and iterates through low-fidelity prototypes to validate solutions before full investment, radically reducing the cost of being wrong.

**Origin:** IDEO (Tim Brown) and Stanford d.school (Hasso Plattner Institute). The 5-stage model was formalised and widely distributed by Stanford d.school from 2010. Synthesises prior work in human factors engineering (1950s) and Scandinavian participatory design (1970s).

## Why it works
Most digital transformation adoption failures are people failures, not technology failures — solutions designed without user input. Design Thinking reverses the technology-first sequence: understand the human problem first (Empathise/Define), then generate solutions (Ideate), build the cheapest possible test version (Prototype), and validate with real users (Test) before committing full investment. Iteration at the prototype stage costs 1/100th of iteration at the deployed product stage.

## When to use
Use in Phase III for customer-facing and experience-redesign projects where user behaviour is uncertain. Use in Phase II Step 6 for 'How Might We' ideation sessions. Particularly valuable for digital customer journeys, employee-facing tools, and any project where adoption risk is the primary concern.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. EMPATHISE: Conduct 10–15 user interviews (60 min each). Use open-ended prompts: 'Walk me through the last time you did X.' Observe users doing the task in their actual environment. Capture verbatim quotes AND observed behaviours — what they do vs. what they say.
2. 2. EMPATHISE: Create Empathy Maps per user type: What they Say / Think / Do / Feel. Contradictions between Say and Do are the richest design insights.
3. 3. DEFINE: Cluster observations into themes via affinity mapping (sticky notes / Miro). Identify 3–5 most significant pain points. Write a HMW (How Might We) problem statement: 'How might we help [user] to [goal] despite [barrier]?'
4. 4. IDEATE: Run Crazy 8s: each participant sketches 8 ideas in 8 minutes (sheet folded into 8 sections). No judgment. Dot-vote on the most promising concepts. Narrow to 3–5 using impact/feasibility sort.
5. 5. PROTOTYPE: Build the lowest-fidelity version that tests the key assumption. For a digital product: a 2-hour Figma clickable prototype. Never invest more than 1 day in a first prototype — it will change.
6. 6. TEST: Recruit 5–8 real target users. Give a task; observe silently. Capture: where do they hesitate, succeed, say aloud? After 5 sessions, patterns are typically clear (Nielsen's Law of Usability).
7. 7. ITERATE: Major assumption wrong → return to Define. Concept sound but execution issues → return to Prototype. User satisfaction threshold met → proceed to build.

## Real-life example — Airbnb
In 2009, Airbnb had a functional product but low bookings. Co-founder Joe Gebbia flew to New York to observe their users and noticed property photos were universally poor (dark, blurry, phone-quality). The insight was invisible in the data — discoverable only through empathy (being in the room with users). The prototype: hire a camera, take professional photos of New York listings. Within one week, revenue doubled. This $200 prototype validated a decision worth billions, leading to Airbnb's professional photography service as a core competitive advantage.

**So what:** The most powerful design insight is almost never visible in the metrics. Airbnb's booking problem looked like a pricing or UX issue in the data — it was a trust and visual quality problem, discoverable only through empathetic observation. The prototype stage cost $200 and validated a strategic decision before any software was written.

## Template
Complete the Design Thinking sprint template for one specific user problem. Aim to complete Empathise through Test in one 5-day sprint.

- [ ] EMPATHISE: User segment | Number of interviews | Top 5 verbatims | Top 5 observed behaviours | Empathy Map summary (Says / Thinks / Does / Feels)
- [ ] DEFINE: Key pain points (3–5) | HMW problem statement: 'How might we help [user] to [goal] despite [barrier]?'
- [ ] IDEATE: Concepts generated (Crazy 8s count) | Top 3 concepts (3-sentence each) | Winning concept | Key assumption to test
- [ ] PROTOTYPE: Format (Figma/paper/role-play) | Time to build | Key assumption the prototype tests
- [ ] TEST: Users tested (n) | Task given | Success metric | Task completion rate (%) | Key failure points observed | Iteration decision (Return to Define / Return to Prototype / Proceed to Build)

## Pitfalls
- Skipping Empathise to save time — generates elegant solutions to the wrong problem; Empathise is non-negotiable.
- Testing with colleagues or friendly stakeholders — not user testing; recruit real target users with the problem.
- High-fidelity prototype too early — creates sunk-cost bias; start with paper or a 2-hour Figma sketch.
- Designing for technology rather than for people — 'we have AI, what can we use it for?' reverses the Design Thinking logic; start with the human problem.

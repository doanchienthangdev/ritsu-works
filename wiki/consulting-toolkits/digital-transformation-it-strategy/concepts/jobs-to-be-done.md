# Jobs-To-Be-Done (JTBD) Framework

*Category: innovation · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A customer-centric framework that defines what customers (or users) are trying to accomplish (the 'job') when they use a product or service — moving beyond demographic or feature-based thinking to understand the progress the customer is seeking to make.

**Origin:** Developed by Clayton Christensen (Harvard Business School) with Anthony Ulwick (Strategyn), published in 'The Innovator's Solution' (2003) and 'Competing Against Luck' (Christensen, 2016). Ulwick's 'Outcome-Driven Innovation' (2005) formalised the JTBD research methodology.

## Why it works
Customers do not buy products — they hire them to do a job. A milkshake is not bought for its flavour; it is hired to make a commute more interesting and to stave off hunger until lunch. This reframe shifts innovation from 'what features do customers want?' to 'what job are customers trying to get done, and what current solutions are they firing to hire ours?' Understanding the job-to-be-done enables more accurate initiative generation than feature requests.

## When to use
Use in Phase IT-III Step 1 (Generate IT Initiatives) to ensure IT initiatives address real business needs. Also use in Phase II Step 6 (Generate Digital Projects) as a structured alternative to unconstrained brainstorming.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Identify the job performer: who is doing the job you are designing for? Be specific (e.g., 'a first-time home buyer', not 'a customer').
2. 2. Define the main job: what is the main job the performer is trying to get done? Use the structure: 'Get [object of the job] [state], given [context constraints].' Example: 'Get qualified for a mortgage, given limited time and financial complexity.'
3. 3. Unpack desired outcomes: interview 10–20 job performers to identify 50–100 desired outcomes. Format: '[Direction of improvement] + [Unit of measure] + [Object of control] + [Clarifying context].' Example: 'Minimise the time it takes to understand which mortgage products I qualify for.'
4. 4. Rate outcomes on Importance and Satisfaction: survey 100+ performers on how important each outcome is (1–10) and how satisfied they are with current solutions (1–10).
5. 5. Calculate Opportunity Score: Importance + max(Importance – Satisfaction, 0). Scores >10 are underserved opportunities. Scores <5 are over-served (do not invest).
6. 6. Generate solutions for underserved outcomes: for each high-opportunity-score outcome, ideate solutions — both digital and non-digital. The JTBD framework does not prescribe solutions; it identifies the problem space.
7. 7. Validate solutions against the job: before building, validate that the proposed solution addresses the specific underserved outcome (prototype and test).
8. 8. Use for IT initiative generation: in Phase IT-III, apply JTBD to business user needs (not end customers) to generate IT initiatives that address genuine, unserved business capability needs.

## Real-life example — Intercom
Intercom's messaging product was designed using JTBD. The founding team identified the job: 'When my business gets new customers, I want to understand and communicate with them individually, so I can help them succeed with my product.' The key underserved outcome: 'Minimise the time it takes to identify which new customers are at risk of churning.' Traditional CRM systems required manual analysis; Intercom automated this by surfacing behavioural signals in real time. By understanding the specific underserved outcome (not 'customers want better CRM'), Intercom built a product that disrupted a $20B CRM market within 5 years.

**So what:** JTBD's power is specificity: the underserved outcome ('minimise time to identify at-risk customers') is specific enough to design a solution for. A generic job ('customers want better CRM') is too vague to design anything meaningful.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing the job with the solution — 'I want a faster horse' is a feature request, not a job; the job is 'I want to get somewhere faster.' Focus on the outcome, not the solution.
- Small sample sizes — opportunity scoring requires 100+ survey respondents to be statistically reliable; smaller samples produce misleading priority rankings.

# OKR Framework (Objectives and Key Results)

*Category: performance · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A goal-setting framework that defines ambitious qualitative Objectives (what you want to achieve) and measurable Key Results (how you know you achieved them), cascaded from organisation to team to individual, reviewed quarterly.

**Origin:** Developed by Andrew Grove at Intel in the 1970s as 'iMBOs'. Introduced to Google by John Doerr in 1999. Documented in Doerr's 2018 book 'Measure What Matters'. Now used by Google, Amazon, LinkedIn, Spotify, and thousands of organisations globally.

## Why it works
OKRs solve the alignment and ambition problem. Traditional targets are often too low, too numerous, and disconnected across levels. OKRs enforce: (1) stretch — 70% achievement is success; (2) measurability — every Key Result has a number; (3) transparency — all OKRs are public; (4) alignment — team OKRs directly support company OKRs. The quarterly cadence creates urgency without sacrificing strategy.

## When to use
Use in Phase II to cascade Digital Transformation strategic objectives to workstream and squad level. Use in Phase IT-I to cascade IT Strategic Objectives. The OKR framework operationalises the Balanced Scorecard at team level.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Set company-level OKRs: CEO and ExCo define 3–5 Objectives for the quarter. Each Objective is qualitative and directional. Each has 2–4 Key Results: specific, measurable, time-bound.
2. 2. Cascade to workstream OKRs: each workstream lead sets 3 Objectives contributing to company-level OKRs. Verify: if the workstream hits its OKRs, does the company hit its company OKR?
3. 3. Set squad OKRs aligned to workstream OKRs, creating a direct line from sprint output to company strategy.
4. 4. Apply 60-40 rule: 60% of team OKRs top-down (aligned to company); 40% team-initiated (autonomy and innovation).
5. 5. Set stretch targets: Key Results at a level where 70% achievement is success. Consistent 100% achievement signals under-ambition.
6. 6. Make OKRs public across the organisation — transparency enables coordination and prevents conflicts.
7. 7. Review monthly: rate each KR 0–1. KRs below 0.5 at mid-quarter trigger corrective action.
8. 8. Grade at quarter end: 0.7+ = success; 0.5– = learning opportunity. Never punish ambitious failure.

## Real-life example — Spotify
Spotify uses OKRs at company, tribe, squad, and chapter levels. A Discovery squad might set Objective: 'Make discovering new music magical' with KR1: Daily active users on Discover Weekly: 40M→50M; KR2: User satisfaction rating: 4.1→4.4/5; KR3: Skip rate: 28%→22%. These cascade to tribe and company OKRs. Spotify's transparency (all OKRs visible to all employees) and cultural tolerance of ambitious failure is credited as a key factor in sustaining innovation at scale across 350+ squads.

**So what:** OKRs require a cultural shift: from 'hit the target or else' to 'set a stretch target and learn from near-miss.' This cultural shift is itself a digital transformation outcome the Change Management programme must address.

## Pitfalls
- Tying OKRs to compensation — kills stretch-target setting; people sandbag to protect bonuses.
- Too many OKRs — 3–5 Objectives per level per quarter is the maximum.
- Key Results that are activities not outcomes — 'Complete training programme' is an activity; '90% completion with >80% score' is a Key Result.
- No mid-quarter review — OKRs reviewed only at quarter end have no corrective mechanism.

# Benefits Realisation Framework

*Category: performance · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A structured methodology for planning, tracking, and reviewing the realisation of business benefits from a digital transformation programme — ensuring that technology delivery translates into measured business value and programme investment is justified.

**Origin:** Developed in project and programme management practice, formalised by Gerald Bradley in 'Benefit Realisation Management' (2nd edition, 2010) and embedded in OGC's Managing Successful Programmes (MSP) framework. Benefits realisation management became a required component of UK Government major programme approvals from approximately 2003.

## Why it works
The gap between programme outputs (delivered technology) and programme outcomes (business value realised) is the most common failure mode in digital transformation. Technology is delivered; adoption is low; benefits do not materialise; ROI is not demonstrated; future investment is questioned. Benefits Realisation Management closes this gap by: (1) defining benefits at the start with explicit measurement mechanisms; (2) assigning benefit owners who are accountable for realisation; (3) tracking benefits throughout delivery (not just at the end); and (4) making benefits realisation data the primary input to programme continuation decisions.

## When to use
Use from Phase II (benefits are defined in the Digital Transformation Strategy) through Phase V (benefits are tracked in the Strategic Dashboards). The Benefits Register is the document that connects the Phase I Business Case to the Phase V dashboard.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Create the Benefits Register: from the Digital Value Driver Tree (Phase I), list all expected benefits. For each benefit: name, description, benefit category (financial/non-financial), the project that delivers it, the measurement metric, the baseline value, the target value, the realisation date, and the benefit owner.
2. 2. Assign benefit owners: each benefit must have a named business owner (not the IT project manager) who is accountable for the benefit materialising. The benefit owner controls the business change needed to realise the benefit.
3. 3. Define the measurement mechanism: for each benefit, specify exactly how it will be measured. Financial benefits (cost savings, revenue uplift) must be confirmed with Finance. Non-financial benefits (customer satisfaction, employee engagement) must have a baseline survey.
4. 4. Build the Benefits Realisation Profile: plot expected benefits by quarter over the programme life. This shows when cumulative benefits exceed cumulative investment (the payback date) and what portion of total benefit is realised in each year.
5. 5. Track leading indicators during delivery: for each benefit, identify a leading indicator that predicts realisation. Example: the leading indicator for 'e-commerce revenue uplift' is 'e-commerce conversion rate'. Monitor leading indicators monthly; if they are not improving, the benefit will not materialise as planned.
6. 6. Hold quarterly Benefits Reviews: in the quarterly programme review, compare benefit actuals to plan. For benefits on track, celebrate. For benefits behind plan, diagnose: is it a delivery delay, a change management failure, or an original assumption error? Agree corrective action.
7. 7. Update benefit forecasts: as actuals accumulate, update the benefit forecast for remaining quarters. Adjust the programme business case accordingly. Escalate material negative variances to the Programme Board.
8. 8. Conduct Post-Programme Evaluation: 6 months after the last project closes, measure all benefits against their original targets. Produce a final Benefits Realisation Statement. Present to the Board. Feed lessons learned back into the organisation's transformation practice.

## Real-life example — UK Ministry of Defence (MOD) — Defence Equipment & Support (DE&S)
DE&S's 2015–2020 digital transformation included a Benefits Realisation Framework aligned to the UK Government's Green Book and MSP framework. Each of the 14 transformation projects had a defined Benefits Register with named benefit owners from the business (not IT). Benefits were tracked quarterly in a Benefits Realisation Dashboard shared with the MOD's Investment Approvals Board. At the 3-year mark, DE&S had realised £340M of the £420M planned benefits (81% realisation rate). The two under-delivering benefit areas — supply chain efficiencies and workforce productivity — were diagnosed through the quarterly benefits reviews, and corrective action (targeted change management and process redesign) was authorised at 18 months, recovering 60% of the expected shortfall. Without the Benefits Realisation Framework, the under-delivery would likely have been discovered only at programme close.

**So what:** Benefits Realisation Management is a diagnostic and governance tool, not just a reporting mechanism. The MOD's quarterly reviews enabled corrective action at 18 months, 18 months before the original detection point. In digital transformation, early detection of benefit underperformance is the most valuable function of the framework.

## Pitfalls
- Benefits owned by IT project managers — IT project managers control technology delivery, not business outcomes; benefit owners must be business leaders who control the business change.
- Benefits measured only at programme close — end-of-programme measurement provides no corrective opportunity; quarterly tracking is the minimum cadence.
- Adjusting baselines instead of forecasts — when benefits are behind plan, teams sometimes adjust the baseline to make the variance disappear; maintain the original baseline and update the forecast instead.
- Non-financial benefits not measured — customer satisfaction, employee engagement, and brand perception are legitimate benefits but require measurement mechanisms (NPS surveys, engagement surveys) that must be established at programme launch, not at programme close.

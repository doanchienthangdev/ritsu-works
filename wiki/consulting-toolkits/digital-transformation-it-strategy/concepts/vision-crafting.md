# Vision & Mission Crafting

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A structured process for crafting a compelling, memorable, and achievable transformation vision statement (3-year aspiration) and mission statement (enduring purpose), validated against a set of quality criteria to ensure it is actionable rather than aspirational wallpaper.

**Origin:** Synthesised from McKinsey's transformation vision methodology, Collins & Porras 'Built to Last' (1994), and Simon Sinek's 'Start With Why' (2009). The specific construct (aspiration + mechanism + beneficiary) is derived from McKinsey's operating model for transformation vision in large-scale change programmes.

## Why it works
A vision statement serves two functions: (1) it provides direction — teams making daily decisions should be able to derive which decisions are consistent with the vision and which are not; (2) it provides motivation — a compelling vision creates the 'desire' component of ADKAR by giving people a picture of the future worth working towards. Most organisational visions fail at both functions because they are too vague ('be the best'), too long (multi-paragraph), or not connected to any specific business outcome.

## When to use
Use in Phase II Step 4 (Craft Digital Transformation Vision) and Phase IT-I Step 4 (IT Vision, Mission & Values). A compelling vision is the foundation for the ADKAR Desire phase in Phase IV — people change because they want to be part of the future the vision describes.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Anchor to the financial case: the vision must be achievable if the programme delivers its planned NPV. Extract the 2–3 most impactful outcomes from the Digital Value Driver Tree — these are the candidates for the vision statement.
2. 2. Use the McKinsey vision construct: 'By [year], [organisation] will be [aspiration] so that [beneficiary benefit], enabled by [key capabilities].' Draft 3 candidate vision statements using this construct.
3. 3. Apply the T-shirt test: can the vision fit on a T-shirt? If not, it is too long. A memorable vision can be recalled by employees 6 months after they first heard it.
4. 4. Apply the ambition test: is the vision stretching? A vision that describes the current state is not a vision. The vision should describe a state that requires the transformation to be successful.
5. 5. Apply the credibility test: is the vision achievable in the stated timeframe? A vision that is fantasy (not achievable even if the programme succeeds) damages credibility. The vision should be achievable in the base-case scenario.
6. 6. Validate with the CEO: the CEO must own the vision — not just endorse it but be able to explain it in their own words and commit to defending it in Board discussions. If the CEO cannot do this, the vision needs revision.
7. 7. Separate vision (3-year aspiration) from mission (enduring purpose): the vision can change when achieved; the mission is permanent. For the IT function: Mission = 'Enable the business to deliver its strategy through reliable, secure, and innovative technology.' Vision = 'By 2027, be the most business-partnered IT function in our sector.'
8. 8. Cascade to values and guiding principles: from the vision and mission, derive 4–5 values (the behaviours the transformation demands) and 8–12 guiding principles (the rules that govern decisions in service of the vision).

## Real-life example — Amazon Web Services (AWS)
Amazon's overall company vision ('To be Earth's most customer-centric company') meets every quality criterion: fits on a T-shirt, is ambitious (global ambition, not 'best in our sector'), has an explicit customer/user benefit ('customer-centric'), and is enduring (it guided decisions in 1997 as it does today). For digital transformation engagements, Amazon's vision for its AWS-enabled cloud transformation of internal operations was: 'By 2010, all Amazon infrastructure will run on AWS services, making Amazon the world's largest AWS customer.' This vision: (1) was time-bound; (2) was specific (all infrastructure, not some); (3) had a clear measurable outcome; and (4) was CEO-driven (Jeff Bezos mandated it). The result was the internal dogfooding that accelerated AWS's commercial product quality.

**So what:** The best transformation visions are specific enough to make daily decisions easier ('Does this decision move us toward being Earth's most customer-centric company?') and memorable enough to be recalled without prompting. Vague visions ('be a digital leader') fail both tests.

## Pitfalls
- Vision by committee — a vision crafted by a committee of 15 people ends up as a compromise that pleases no one and inspires nothing; the vision should be drafted by 2–3 people close to the CEO and then validated broadly.
- Aspirational wallpaper — a vision that sounds good in the slide deck but has no connection to operational decisions provides no direction; the test is whether a team lead can use the vision to make a daily trade-off decision.
- Vision without mission — the vision is temporary (it can be achieved and replaced); the mission is permanent. Without a mission, the organisation loses its North Star when the vision is achieved.

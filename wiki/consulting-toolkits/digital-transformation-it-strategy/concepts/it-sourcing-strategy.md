# IT Sourcing and Procurement Strategy

*Category: operations · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A structured framework for making IT sourcing decisions (build vs. buy vs. partner vs. outsource) across all IT categories, establishing vendor management practices, contract governance, and KPIs to optimise total cost, quality, and risk.

**Origin:** IT sourcing strategy practice derives from make-or-buy analysis (transaction cost economics, Oliver Williamson, 1975) applied to IT. Formalised as a strategic IT management capability in McKinsey, KPMG, and Gartner IT advisory practice from the 1990s. The maturity model approach (reactive procurement → strategic partner) draws on Gartner's IT vendor management maturity model.

## Why it works
IT sourcing decisions are among the largest, most consequential cost decisions an IT organisation makes. Without a sourcing strategy, decisions are made reactively (whoever knocks on the door first wins), resulting in vendor lock-in, inconsistent contract terms, and missed innovation. A sourcing strategy defines: which IT categories to build internally (where differentiation is required), which to buy as products (where standard solutions exist), which to outsource (non-core, commodity), and how to manage vendors across the full relationship lifecycle.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Categorise IT spend: segment all IT spend into 4–6 categories (e.g., Infrastructure, Applications: ERP, Applications: CRM, Applications: Analytics, Cybersecurity, Professional Services).
2. 2. Assess current sourcing model per category: for each category, document current vendor mix, contract terms, spend, and satisfaction.
3. 3. Apply the build/buy/partner/outsource framework: for each category, determine the appropriate sourcing model based on business differentiation potential and internal IT maturity.
4. 4. Define the vendor management approach per category: strategic partners (deep relationship, co-innovation, executive engagement) vs. preferred suppliers (standard contract, periodic review) vs. commodity vendors (purchase order, no relationship).
5. 5. Assess vendor concentration risk: identify categories with single-vendor dependency. Define a risk mitigation strategy (second-source, contract exit terms, skills to in-source if needed).
6. 6. Run a TCO analysis for major sourcing decisions: for decisions between in-house and outsource, or between competing vendors, build a TCO model covering: licence cost, implementation cost, integration cost, ongoing maintenance, training, and exit cost.
7. 7. Establish procurement governance: define minimum contract requirements (SLAs, data rights, exit clauses, security standards), approval thresholds (IT Steering Committee for contracts >£/$X), and contract review frequency.
8. 8. Define sourcing KPIs: cost vs. budget (%), vendor SLA compliance (%), contract renewal on-time rate (%), vendor satisfaction score (business survey), IT cost as % of revenue.

## Real-life example — BP (British Petroleum)
BP's IT sourcing transformation (2018–2020) moved from a predominantly outsourced model (90% of IT outsourced to a small number of mega-vendors) to a multi-sourcing model: cloud infrastructure (AWS, Azure — hybrid multi-cloud), specialist partners for domain applications (SAP for ERP, Salesforce for CRM), and a rebuilt internal capability for digital innovation and data science. The TCO analysis showed that insourcing data science and digital development saved 35% vs. the outsourced model, while the cloud migration reduced infrastructure TCO by 28%. The vendor concentration risk (previously: a single outsourcer for 90% of IT) was eliminated by the multi-sourcing model.

**So what:** The optimal IT sourcing model shifts over time as internal capabilities mature and cloud reduces the advantage of scale outsourcing. BP's experience is instructive: the mega-outsource model that made sense in 2005 (limited internal capability, high economies of scale) was sub-optimal by 2018 (cloud eliminating infrastructure scale advantages, digital capability as a competitive differentiator).

## Pitfalls
- Outsourcing to cut short-term cost without a TCO model — exit costs and capability loss are typically not included in the initial outsourcing ROI calculation.
- Strategic partner relationships without executive engagement — a 'strategic partnership' where only procurement managers communicate is a preferred supplier relationship with a better name.

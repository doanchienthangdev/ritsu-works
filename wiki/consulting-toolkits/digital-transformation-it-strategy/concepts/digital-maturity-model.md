# Digital Maturity Model

*Category: diagnosis · Toolkit: Digital Transformation & IT Strategy*

## What it is
A 5-level assessment framework that measures an organisation's digital capability across five dimensions (Strategy, Culture, Technology, Operations, Customer Experience), producing a scored radar chart used to baseline current capability, set targets, and prioritise investment.

**Origin:** Multiple parallel developments: Forrester Research, MIT Sloan CISR, McKinsey Digital, and Deloitte Digital all published versions in 2014–2018. The 5-level, 5-dimension structure has become the de facto standard, synthesised from these sources. The exact version used in this toolkit follows the MIT/McKinsey synthesis.

## Why it works
Digital transformation is not a single capability — it is a portfolio of interdependent organisational capabilities. A maturity model makes the diagnosis concrete (scored, benchmarkable) and prioritisable (which dimensions to invest in first). The five dimensions reflect the full organisational system: strategy gives direction, culture enables adoption, technology enables delivery, operations executes, and customer experience is the ultimate output. All five must advance; over-investment in any one dimension without the others produces brittle results (e.g., advanced technology in a low-maturity culture = low adoption).

## When to use
Use at the start of any digital transformation engagement to baseline current capability and set targets. Re-administer at programme milestones to measure progress. Also use when justifying the size and scope of a digital transformation investment to the Board — the maturity gap is a powerful visualisation of the 'burning platform'.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. 1. Prepare the survey: use the 25-question Digital Maturity Assessment Survey (5 questions per dimension, rated 1–5). Brief participants on the scoring scale and the importance of honest self-assessment.
2. 2. Administer the survey: select 10–20 respondents spanning C-suite, senior management, and functional leads (not just IT). Avoid selecting only digital champions — a representative sample is essential.
3. 3. Aggregate scores: calculate the mean score per dimension (average of 5 questions) and the overall Digital Maturity Score (average of 5 dimension scores). Compute standard deviation to understand consensus vs. divergence.
4. 4. Benchmark: compare dimension scores against the sector benchmark (sourced from Forrester, McKinsey, or relevant analyst data). Identify dimensions where the organisation is furthest below benchmark — these are the priority gaps.
5. 5. Plot the radar chart: current state (solid line), sector benchmark (dashed), and — after Step 6 — the target (shaded area). Share the radar with the executive team to anchor the conversation on gaps, not scores.
6. 6. Set target scores: for each dimension, agree the target score at 12-month, 24-month, and end-state horizons. Targets must be feasible given the investment level approved. A 1-point improvement per dimension per year is achievable with focused effort.
7. 7. Identify priority interventions: for each below-benchmark dimension, map the specific capabilities that are weakest (e.g., within Technology: data quality, cloud adoption, cybersecurity). These drive the initiative pipeline.
8. 8. Re-administer at programme milestones (12 months, 24 months, programme end) to measure progress. The improvement in the Overall Digital Maturity Score is a headline benefit in the Benefits Realisation Report.

## Real-life example — National Australia Bank (NAB)
When NAB launched its 2017–2020 digital transformation programme, the bank commissioned a digital maturity assessment across 30 senior leaders. The assessment revealed an overall score of 2.3/5. Technology scored 3.1 (a legacy of prior IT investment) but Customer Experience scored 1.8 and Culture scored 1.6 — reflecting a deeply traditional banking culture slow to adopt digital ways of working. The assessment directly shaped the investment thesis: the bulk of the £A2.1B transformation budget was allocated not to technology (already relatively mature) but to a cultural transformation programme, the 'NAB Academy' (digital skills training for 30,000 employees), and a customer journey redesign programme. By 2020, NAB had reached an overall digital maturity score of approximately 3.4/5, and became the first Australian major bank to offer real-time mortgage approvals.

**So what:** A high technology score does not mean high digital maturity. The model reveals the cross-dimensional gaps — in NAB's case, culture and customer experience — that technology investment alone cannot close. The radar chart is a powerful tool for redirecting investment from the obvious (more technology) to the necessary (culture, customer experience).

## Pitfalls
- Surveying only IT leaders: the digital maturity assessment is an organisational assessment, not an IT assessment. If only IT leaders respond, the scores will be systematically biased towards Technology (which IT controls) and miss the gaps in Culture, Operations, and Customer Experience that are the actual constraint on transformation success.
- Treating the score as the goal: organisations that optimise for improving the maturity score (rather than building real capability) game the assessment by selecting optimistic respondents or interpreting questions favourably. The score is a directional indicator, not a certification.
- Setting unrealistic targets: jumping from 2.0 to 4.5 in 2 years requires massive investment and is rarely achievable. Calibrate targets against the investment budget and sector benchmarks — a 1-point improvement per dimension per year is stretching but achievable.
- One-time assessment: a baseline-only assessment provides a snapshot but no learning curve. Re-run the assessment at 12 and 24 months to measure actual progress and adapt the programme.

# ADKAR Change Model

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A five-milestone model for managing individual change: Awareness (understanding why change is needed), Desire (personal motivation to support the change), Knowledge (knowing how to change), Ability (skills and behaviours to implement the change), and Reinforcement (sustaining the change over time).

**Origin:** Created by Jeff Hiatt, founder of Prosci, in 1998 based on research into the patterns of successful and failed change initiatives in over 700 organisations. Published in 'ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and Our Community' (Prosci, 2006).

## Why it works
The ADKAR model's key insight is that change fails when the intervention addresses the wrong milestone. If people lack Awareness, training programmes (which address Knowledge) will fail. If people have Knowledge but lack Ability, communication campaigns will fail. By diagnosing which milestone is the current barrier, change managers can prescribe the precise intervention type — making change management scientific rather than intuitive. Research shows that 70% of transformation initiatives fail primarily due to people-side resistance, not technical failure.

## When to use
Phase 6 (Change Management) — specifically at the start of each major initiative to assess the change-readiness of the target population. Also use as a diagnostic tool any time an initiative has stalled or is experiencing resistance.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. For each major HR initiative, identify the target population — the employees whose behaviour must change for the initiative to succeed. Be specific: it may be all 180 people managers for an OKR implementation, or all 500 individual contributors for a new performance system.
2. Design and administer the ADKAR Pulse Survey: five questions, one per milestone, rated 1-5 by a sample of the target population (at minimum 30% sample, ideally 60%+). Administer before the initiative launch to establish the baseline.
3. Calculate the average score per milestone. The lowest-scoring milestone is the 'first barrier.' Do not attempt to address all five milestones simultaneously — focus the intervention on the first barrier. Addressing a later milestone when an earlier one is unresolved wastes resources and creates frustration.
4. Design the targeted intervention for the first barrier. If Awareness is the barrier: the CEO must deliver a compelling 'why' message with data, not HR sending an email about a policy change. If Desire is the barrier: identify what specific concern or self-interest is blocking support and address it directly in manager one-on-one conversations.
5. Implement the intervention, then re-survey 4-6 weeks later. Has the first barrier score moved above 3.0? If yes, move to the next milestone. If no, escalate to the executive sponsor — the barrier may be a structural issue (e.g., the manager's own Desire is low, causing their team's Desire to remain low).
6. Repeat the cycle for each subsequent milestone until all five average above 3.5 (indicating solid adoption). At this point, focus shifts to Reinforcement — the structured sustainability phase.

## Real-life example — Siemens
During Siemens's 100,000-employee digital transformation (2018-2021), the change team used the ADKAR model to diagnose and address adoption barriers for its new digital platform. The initial ADKAR assessment revealed that Awareness was high (people knew the change was coming) but Desire was low (people felt the current tools were adequate and feared complexity). The change team's intervention targeted Desire specifically: team leads were given data showing that competitor efficiency gains from the same platform were eroding Siemens's competitive position — converting an abstract 'technology change' into a concrete business threat. Desire scores moved from 2.4 to 3.8 within 8 weeks. Result: 78% platform adoption within 12 months, significantly above the industry average for comparable transformations.

**So what:** The ADKAR model's most valuable contribution is not its five milestones — it is the diagnostic precision that prevents organisations from investing in the wrong intervention. Siemens did not waste budget on more training (addressing Knowledge when Desire was the barrier). Diagnosing first saved significant investment and accelerated adoption.

## Template
Complete one ADKAR assessment per major initiative. Administer the pulse survey to at least 30% of the target population. Repeat every 4-6 weeks and track score progression. Escalate to the Steering Committee if any milestone score remains below 2.5 for more than 8 weeks.

## Pitfalls
- Running the ADKAR survey once at project launch and never repeating it. ADKAR is a recurring diagnostic tool — the milestone barriers change as the initiative progresses. A population that started with a Desire gap may develop a Knowledge gap at go-live. Without repeat measurement, the change team is flying blind.
- Averaging ADKAR scores and missing critical sub-group issues. The average score may be 3.2 across the full population, but one department may score 1.8 on Desire while another scores 4.5. The department with 1.8 is a resistance hotspot — it will not emerge in aggregate data. Always disaggregate scores by team or manager.
- Treating the Reinforcement milestone as automatic once Ability is achieved. Reinforcement requires deliberate design: performance criteria that include the new behaviour, recognition of adopters, manager accountability for their team's adoption, and removal of competing incentives (old behaviours that are still rewarded).

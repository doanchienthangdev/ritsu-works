# 70:20:10 Learning Model

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A framework for designing balanced talent development programmes that allocates learning investment across three sources: 70% from on-the-job experience (stretch assignments, new responsibilities), 20% from social learning (coaching, mentoring, peer feedback), and 10% from formal training (courses, workshops, e-learning).

**Origin:** Based on research by Morgan McCall, Michael Lombardo, and Robert Eichinger at the Center for Creative Leadership in the 1990s, which found through longitudinal studies of executives that 70% of development came from challenging assignments, 20% from relationships and feedback, and 10% from coursework. Published in 'The Career Architect Development Planner' (Lombardo & Eichinger, 1996).

## Why it works
The model works because it aligns development investment to where learning actually occurs. Neuroscience research supports this: behavioural change requires practice in context, spaced repetition, and corrective feedback — all of which are features of experiential and social learning, not classroom training. Training transfer research (Baldwin & Ford, 1988) shows that without a structured experiential follow-on, fewer than 15% of classroom training behaviours transfer to the workplace within 12 months.

## When to use
Phase 2, Pillar 6 (Talent Development) as the design framework for all individual and group development programmes. Also use when designing a graduate or early-career programme, and when evaluating existing L&D investments for effectiveness.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. For each high-potential employee, identify the top 2-3 competency gaps from their IDP and 9-Box assessment. For each gap, design interventions across all three channels — not just formal training.
2. For the 70% channel (experiential): define one specific stretch assignment or role change for each high-potential in the next 12 months. The assignment must involve genuine stretch (first-time challenge, higher scope, unfamiliar domain). Examples: leading a cross-functional project, taking on a P&L responsibility, managing an international team for the first time. Document the assignment in the IDP with a clear learning objective.
3. For the 20% channel (social): assign every high-potential a named coach and/or mentor. Coach = external professional coach for skill development; Mentor = internal senior leader for career guidance and advocacy. Implement 360-degree feedback as a structured development tool (not just as a performance input) — specifically request behavioural feedback on the target competency gaps.
4. For the 10% channel (formal): curate a competency-linked learning catalogue. Each formal learning option should be tagged to the competency it develops, making it easy for managers and employees to select the right programme for the right gap. Do not offer a topic-based catalogue ('Marketing 101') — offer a competency-based catalogue ('Courses that develop Commercial Acumen').
5. Build a tracking mechanism: for each high-potential, log the three-channel interventions in the HRIS or IDP system. Hold quarterly IDP check-ins between the employee and their manager to assess progress on all three channels — not just formal training completion.
6. Evaluate at 12 months: pre-post competency assessment using the same tool used to identify the gap. Did the combined 70-20-10 intervention close the gap? Adjust the design for the next development cycle based on what worked.

## Real-life example — Unilever
Unilever's Future Leaders Programme (UFLP) explicitly uses 70:20:10 as its design framework. Graduate recruits enter a 2-year rotational programme: the 70% is structured rotations across different functions and geographies (sales in one country, supply chain in another, finance in a third), deliberately placing graduates in unfamiliar contexts. The 20% is a mandatory executive sponsor relationship plus monthly mentoring sessions with senior leaders. The 10% is the 'Unilever Management Essentials' modular classroom programme, designed specifically to complement the rotations. Unilever reports that UFLP graduates reach first-level management roles 18-24 months faster than non-UFLP recruits, which it attributes to the experiential design.

**So what:** The insight from Unilever is that the 70% (experiential) component requires the most investment in design — not time, but design. The rotations must be genuinely challenging, not just observational. A poorly designed stretch assignment (where the 'stretch' is doing the same work in a different office) produces no more development than staying in the same role.

## Template
Complete one IDP per high-potential employee. Define specific, named development actions across all three channels. Review quarterly with the manager. Assess competency progression annually using the same tool used to identify the initial gap.

## Pitfalls
- Treating 70:20:10 as a budget allocation formula rather than a design principle. The model describes the relative contribution of each source to actual learning — not a directive to spend exactly 70% of the L&D budget on experiential interventions. In practice, the 10% formal channel often costs more per learning hour than the 70% experiential channel (which is largely free — stretch assignments carry no additional cost).
- Designing stretch assignments that are not actually stretching. 'Leading the team while the manager is on holiday' is not a stretch assignment if the team is functional and the work is routine. A genuine stretch involves: encountering a type of problem the employee has never faced before, being uncertain about the right approach, and having consequential outcomes.
- Implementing 70:20:10 only for high-potentials and ignoring the rest of the population. While differentiated investment makes strategic sense, even box-5 'Core Players' benefit from the 70:20:10 design approach — particularly the 20% channel (coaching conversations and peer learning) which has a very high impact-to-cost ratio.

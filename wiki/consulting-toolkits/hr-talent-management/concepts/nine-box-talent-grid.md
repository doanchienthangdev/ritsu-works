# 9-Box Talent Grid (Performance × Potential)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A 3×3 matrix that plots all employees on two axes — Performance (Low/Medium/High) and Potential (Low/Medium/High) — creating nine distinct talent segments, each with a standardised investment and development strategy.

**Origin:** Developed at McKinsey & Company and General Electric in the 1970s as part of the 'War for Talent' initiative. Jack Welch institutionalised it at GE through the 'Session C' talent-calibration process. The framework became widely adopted following McKinsey's 2001 research report 'The War for Talent.'

## Why it works
Performance alone is a backward-looking measure — it tells you what an employee has achieved. Potential is forward-looking — it tells you what an employee is capable of achieving in more complex, senior roles. Combining both dimensions creates a decision framework that avoids two common talent-management errors: (1) promoting the best current performer who lacks the potential for the next role ('Peter Principle'), and (2) overlooking a rough diamond who performs modestly today but has exceptional future ceiling.

## When to use
Phase 2, Pillar 6 (Talent Development) as the foundation for all talent investment decisions. Also use for succession planning (Phase 2, Pillar 6) and as the annual talent review mechanism for all levels above individual contributor.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define 'potential' behaviourally before the calibration session using your organisation's leadership model. 'Potential' should mean: learning agility (how quickly does this person learn from experience?), leadership aspiration (do they want to lead at a higher level?), and complexity ceiling (can they handle greater ambiguity and scope?). Avoid using 'years of experience' or 'tenure' as proxies for potential.
2. Ask each people manager to independently rate each of their direct reports on a 1-3 scale for both Performance and Potential, with written justification. Collect these ratings before the calibration session — individual ratings tend to be more honest than group ratings.
3. Convene a 3-hour talent calibration session with all people managers (for that level/group). One manager presents their team's ratings; the group questions and challenges each rating. Key calibration prompt: 'What evidence do you have for that Potential rating? Have you seen them operate above their current scope?'
4. Map all employees to the grid and produce a distribution view. Challenge: if more than 25% of employees are in boxes 7-9 (high potential), the organisation is likely over-rating potential — this is a consistent bias. Facilitate recalibration.
5. For each employee in boxes 7-9, define specific development actions: What stretch assignment or role change will they get in the next 12 months? Who is their executive sponsor? What development investment is authorised? Document these commitments.
6. For employees in boxes 1-4, ensure a clear, time-bound performance action is defined: for box 1, a Performance Improvement Plan (PIP) with a 90-day review; for box 4, a manager coaching plan and role-fit assessment. Never leave low-performance boxes without a defined intervention.
7. Review the grid every 6-12 months. Employees can and should move — this is not a permanent label.

## Real-life example — General Electric
Jack Welch made GE's Session C talent review (using the 9-Box Grid) the most important management meeting in the company's calendar. Welch personally participated in talent calibrations for GE's top 500 managers. The grid-driven process drove differentiated investment: the top 20% (boxes 7-9) received significant investment and compensation; the bottom 10% (boxes 1-2) were exited from the organisation. This forced differentiation was controversial but correlated with GE's decade-long outperformance of the S&P 500 in the 1980s-1990s.

**So what:** The discipline of GE's Session C process was not the grid itself — it was the management time Welch personally invested and the fact that talent decisions (promotions, investments, exits) were transparently linked to box placement. Without that linkage, the grid becomes a labelling exercise rather than a decision-making tool.

## Pitfalls
- Rating potential based on personality likeability or cultural similarity to the rating manager. Potential must be rated on evidence of learning agility, scope performance, and aspiration — not on 'I like how she presents in meetings.' Training managers on potential indicators is essential before the first calibration.
- Using the 9-Box Grid as a performance management tool — it is not. It is a talent investment and succession planning tool. Using it to determine compensation or bonuses creates perverse incentives and legal risk.
- Over-populating box 9 due to 'grade inflation' or manager advocacy for their team. Research consistently shows that in unmoderated calibration sessions, 30-40% of employees end up in the top boxes. Facilitate strongly: for every box-9 placement, require specific behavioural evidence.

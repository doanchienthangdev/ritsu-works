# HR Business Partner Model (Three-Legged Stool)

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
An HR operating model that divides the function into three distinct service tiers: Strategic HR Business Partners (embedded in business units), Centres of Excellence (specialist functions), and HR Shared Services (transactional processing) — matching service delivery to the nature and strategic value of each HR activity.

**Origin:** Formalised by Dave Ulrich in 'Human Resource Champions' (1997) at the University of Michigan Ross School of Business. The 'three-legged stool' metaphor — BPs, CoEs, Shared Services — became the dominant HR operating model framework globally by the early 2000s.

## Why it works
The HRBP model exists because HR activities have radically different requirements: strategic talent advice for a business unit requires consultative relationship skills and business acumen; designing a compensation benchmarking study requires specialist analytical expertise; processing payroll requires process efficiency and error-free execution. Combining all three in one role (the 'generalist HR Manager') creates a function that does none of them well. Separation by tier enables each part to be optimised for its distinct purpose.

## When to use
Phase 1, Step 7 — when designing the HR team structure and service delivery model. Also use when restructuring an HR function that has become too generalist or too specialist, or when integrating two HR functions post-merger.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Audit your current HR headcount and time allocation: for each HR role, estimate what % of time is spent on strategic advisory (HRBP activities), specialist design (CoE activities), and transactional processing (Shared Services activities). This reveals the current implicit model.
2. Define the HRBP tier: identify which business units need embedded HR strategic partners. Typically, each BU with >300 employees and a senior business sponsor gets a dedicated HRBP. For smaller BUs, one HRBP may support 2-3 units. Define the HRBP competency profile: business acumen and consulting skills are mandatory.
3. Define the CoE tier: identify the specialist domains your organisation needs (Talent Acquisition, L&D, Total Rewards, OD, Employee Relations). Each CoE owns the design of its domain's programmes and tools. CoE specialists should be deep experts — not generalists.
4. Define the Shared Services tier: list all transactional HR tasks (payroll processing, benefits queries, onboarding paperwork, policy questions). Evaluate which can be standardised and automated (e.g., FAQ chatbot for policy queries), which should be kept in-house (high-sensitivity tasks), and which should be outsourced (e.g., payroll to ADP or Workday).
5. Size each tier using benchmarks: total HR headcount = 1.4 FTE per 100 employees (Mercer); Shared Services is typically 40-60% of that; CoEs are 15-25%; HRBPs are 20-35%.
6. Define the operating model interfaces: how do HRBPs commission work from CoEs? How do Shared Services escalate cases beyond their scope? Build a simple RACI that governs these hand-offs.

## Real-life example — Nestlé
Nestlé implemented the Ulrich HRBP model globally in 2012, creating a three-tier structure across its 300,000-employee global workforce. The transformation moved 60% of HR headcount into a shared services structure (centralised in Geneva and regional hubs), freeing HRBP capacity from administrative work. HRBPs were reallocated to strategic talent advisory roles embedded in business units. Nestlé reported that HRBP satisfaction scores from business leaders increased by 35% within 18 months of the transition — primarily because HRBPs were now spending 60% of their time on strategic activities versus 25% before the model change.

**So what:** The biggest risk in HRBP model transitions is under-investing in the HRBP capability upgrade. Moving someone from a generalist HR Manager role to an HRBP role does not automatically make them consultative or commercially minded. Invest heavily in HRBP coaching and capability development alongside the structural change.

## Pitfalls
- Implementing the HRBP model as a structural reorganisation without investing in HRBP capability development. The model requires HRBPs to have genuine business acumen and consultative skills — most generalist HR managers do not start with these competencies. A structural change without a capability investment produces HRBPs who continue to do administrative work in their new title.
- Building Shared Services without sufficient technology investment. Shared Services only achieves efficiency when the transactional processes are standardised and largely automated. A Shared Services centre without a strong HRIS is just a call centre.
- Not defining clear operating interfaces between tiers. In practice, the most common failure point is the HRBP-to-CoE interface — HRBPs need talent solutions quickly; CoEs need design time. Without a formal commissioning process, HRBPs improvise solutions that duplicate or contradict CoE work.

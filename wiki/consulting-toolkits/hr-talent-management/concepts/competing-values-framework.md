# Competing Values Framework (CVF)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A culture-assessment and culture-design tool that maps an organisation's dominant culture type on two axes — Internal vs. External focus and Stability vs. Flexibility — producing four culture quadrants (Clan, Adhocracy, Hierarchy, Market) and enabling evidence-based culture-change design.

**Origin:** Developed by Robert Quinn and John Rohrbaugh at the University of Michigan in 1983 ('A Spatial Model of Effectiveness Criteria', Management Science 29(3)). The associated Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI) has been validated in hundreds of research studies across dozens of countries.

## Why it works
The CVF works because it acknowledges that organisations embody multiple, competing cultural values simultaneously (hence 'Competing Values') — no organisation is purely one quadrant. The diagnostic reveals which quadrant is currently dominant vs. desired, enabling leaders to design specific interventions targeting the gap. The framework's power is its diagnostic precision: rather than vague culture prescriptions ('we need to be more innovative'), the CVF maps the specific cultural shift required (from Hierarchy to Adhocracy) and the levers that drive it.

## When to use
Phase 2, Pillar 3 (Culture) as the primary diagnostic and design tool. Also use when a company is undergoing a strategic pivot that requires a culture shift (e.g., moving from a cost-efficiency to an innovation strategy), or post-merger when two cultures need to be integrated.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Select a representative cross-section of employees to survey (25-50 for a business unit, 100-200 for an enterprise). Aim for diversity in level, function, and tenure — particularly include both long-tenured employees (cultural memory) and recent joiners (fresh perspective).
2. Administer the OCAI (Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument): 6 questions, each requiring the respondent to allocate 100 points across four culture descriptions corresponding to the four quadrants. The forced allocation (summing to 100) prevents middle-of-the-road responses.
3. Aggregate responses per quadrant across all respondents. Plot the current culture profile as a diamond on the 2×2 grid — the larger the triangle in each quadrant, the more dominant that culture type. Calculate the dominant quadrant.
4. Repeat the OCAI asking respondents to describe the DESIRED culture in 3-5 years. Plot the desired profile on the same chart. The gap between current and desired highlights the culture-change priorities.
5. Facilitate a leadership workshop presenting the current vs. desired profiles. Discuss: 'What are the specific leader behaviours, rituals, and symbols that currently reinforce the dominant quadrant? Which of these need to change to shift toward the desired quadrant?' Identify 3-5 high-leverage culture levers.
6. Design culture-change interventions targeting the specific levers: leadership behaviour modelling (the most powerful lever), rituals and ceremonies, recognition systems, physical environment, communication style, and performance criteria.

## Real-life example — Spotify
In 2014, Spotify was experiencing the culture-change challenges typical of a hyper-growth technology company: it had built engineering capabilities that reflected a Hierarchy culture (structured processes, approval hierarchies, predictable output) while needing an Adhocracy culture (autonomous innovation, rapid experimentation, tolerance for failure) to sustain product innovation. Rather than run a formal CVF assessment, Spotify used an equivalent culture-mapping exercise that identified the dominant culture gap. The resulting 'Squad/Tribe/Chapter/Guild' model (the 'Spotify Model') was the structural intervention designed to shift from Hierarchy to Adhocracy — removing approval layers and granting engineering squads full autonomy over their product areas.

**So what:** The CVF is not just a measurement tool — it is a design tool. The value of the assessment is not the score but the specific culture-change interventions it enables. Leaders who use CVF only as a measurement benchmark are getting 20% of its value.

## Pitfalls
- Designing the desired culture without CEO alignment. The CEO's natural leadership style powerfully shapes culture — if the CEO operates in Hierarchy mode (approval-seeking, process-oriented), no programme will shift the culture to Adhocracy regardless of what it says on the values poster. The CVF workshop must include the CEO, and the CEO's own behaviour must be included in the culture-change plan.
- Using the CVF to diagnose culture without following through on the culture-change interventions. Culture assessments that produce reports but not actions damage morale — employees who participated in the survey feel their input was ignored. Commit to at least 3 concrete culture-change actions before running the OCAI.
- Running the CVF at the enterprise level only. Culture varies significantly by business unit and geography. For a global enterprise, run the CVF separately in each major BU or country — the results will likely differ and the interventions need to be localised.

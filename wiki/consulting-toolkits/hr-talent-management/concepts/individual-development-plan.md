# Individual Development Plan (IDP)

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A structured, documented agreement between an employee and their manager defining the employee's career goals, the competency gaps that stand between current state and those goals, and the specific development actions (across the 70:20:10 model) that will close those gaps within a defined timeframe.

**Origin:** IDPs emerged as a formal HR tool in the 1980s-1990s as performance management evolved from purely backward-looking appraisals to forward-looking development planning. Standardised IDP frameworks were developed by HR consultancies (Lominger/Korn Ferry) and adopted by leading corporations including GE, IBM, and Unilever in the 1990s.

## Why it works
IDPs work by converting vague development intent ('I should improve my leadership skills') into specific, time-bound, measurable commitments ('I will take on the role of leading the Q3 product launch project as a stretch assignment by June, coached weekly by [senior leader]'). The documented nature of an IDP creates mutual accountability: the employee is accountable for execution, and the manager is accountable for creating the conditions (time, sponsorship, assignments) that make development possible.

## When to use
Phase 2, Pillar 6 (Talent Development) — create IDPs for all employees above a defined level (typically all employees at L3 and above in a 6-level job architecture, or all employees flagged in boxes 7-9 of the 9-Box Grid). Also create for all employees on a performance improvement trajectory.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Schedule a dedicated 60-minute IDP conversation between the employee and their manager (separate from the performance review — development deserves its own conversation). Start with the employee's career goal: 'Where do you want to be in 3-5 years, and what draws you to that?' Listen first — the manager's role here is not to prescribe but to understand.
2. Identify the top 3 competency gaps by comparing the employee's current proficiency (assessed via the most recent performance review and 360 feedback) against the target competency requirements of the aspired role. Prioritise by strategic importance and development tractability.
3. For each competency gap, design one specific action in each of the three 70:20:10 channels. The 70% action must be a genuine stretch — not 'work on more complex cases' but 'lead the integration workstream in the new-market entry project.' Be specific about timeline and expected output.
4. Agree the resource commitments: What time will the manager protect for development activities? What is the allocated coaching budget? What is the L&D programme enrolment? Document commitments explicitly — vague intentions produce no development.
5. Set a quarterly review date and an annual reassessment date. Enter both in the employee's calendar and the manager's calendar at the time of IDP sign-off. A quarterly review that is not calendared at IDP creation will not happen.
6. At the annual reassessment: compare current competency ratings to baseline. Have the gaps closed? Validate whether the career goal is still accurate — goals legitimately evolve. Update the IDP for the next 12-month cycle.

## Real-life example — McKinsey & Company
McKinsey's talent development model is built around what the firm calls 'the obligation to dissent and the obligation to develop.' Every consultant (from Business Analyst to Partner) has a formal development dialogue with their PD counsellor twice a year, producing a development plan with specific client assignments selected for developmental stretch rather than only for staffing efficiency. McKinsey's 'development model for client service' explicitly uses the 70% experiential channel (client assignments as the primary development vehicle) with the 20% channel (counsellor coaching and peer feedback) as the enabling mechanism. The result: McKinsey alumni disproportionately hold CEO, CFO, and board positions globally — widely attributed to the intensity of this development system.

**So what:** The McKinsey insight is that development requires choosing assignments for what the employee needs to learn, not just for what the project needs. This is a resource-allocation decision that requires management courage — it is easier to staff the person who is already strong at the required skills than the person who needs to develop them. The IDP creates the explicit commitment that enables this kind of developmental staffing decision.

## Template
Complete one IDP per employee per annual development cycle. Review quarterly. Ensure the manager signs off on the resource commitments before the employee can be held accountable for development actions. File in the HRIS.

- [ ] Employee Name, Role, Level, and Business Unit
- [ ] Manager Name
- [ ] IDP Period (e.g., January-December 2025)
- [ ] Career Goal: Aspired Role in 3-5 Years / Why this goal motivates me
- [ ] Current Competency Strengths (top 3 to leverage)
- [ ] Competency Gap 1: Name / Current Level / Target Level / Priority
- [ ] Gap 1 — 70% Action: Stretch assignment description / Start date / End date / Learning objective / Success measure
- [ ] Gap 1 — 20% Action: Coaching or mentoring relationship / Frequency / Competency focus
- [ ] Gap 1 — 10% Action: Programme name / Dates / Competency addressed
- [ ] Competency Gap 2 and actions (same structure)
- [ ] Competency Gap 3 and actions (same structure)
- [ ] Manager Resource Commitments (time protection / budget / sponsorship / assignment decisions)
- [ ] Quarterly Review Dates (Q1/Q2/Q3)
- [ ] Annual Reassessment Date
- [ ] Employee Signature and Date
- [ ] Manager Signature and Date

## Pitfalls
- Creating IDPs as a compliance exercise that is filed and never reviewed. An IDP that is not reviewed quarterly is not a development plan — it is a form. Build quarterly review conversations into both the manager and employee's calendars at the time of IDP creation, making the review a structural commitment rather than an optional extra.
- Designing the IDP without genuine employee input. Managers who prescribe development plans ('you need to improve your communication skills') rather than co-creating them with the employee produce plans the employee does not own and will not execute. The career goal and development priorities must come primarily from the employee.
- Focusing exclusively on the 10% formal training channel. It is much easier for managers to approve e-learning enrolments than to create meaningful stretch assignments — the latter requires planning, delegation, and tolerance for some short-term performance risk. Ensure that every IDP has a documented 70% action, not just a list of courses.

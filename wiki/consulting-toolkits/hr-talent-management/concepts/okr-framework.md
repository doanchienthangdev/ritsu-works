# OKR Framework (Objectives & Key Results)

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A goal-setting methodology in which each Objective (qualitative, directional, motivating) is supported by 3-5 Key Results (quantitative, time-bound, measurable outcomes) that define what 'achievement' looks like — creating alignment from company level to individual level.

**Origin:** Invented by Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s as Management By Objectives (MBO) reimagined with better cadence and transparency. Popularised by John Doerr at Kleiner Perkins who introduced it to Google in 1999. Documented in Doerr's 'Measure What Matters' (2018).

## Why it works
Traditional annual goal-setting suffers from three fatal flaws: goals are set once and reviewed once, making course correction impossible; goals are often private, preventing alignment; and goals conflate activity (what you did) with outcome (what you achieved). OKRs fix all three: quarterly cycles enable course correction, public transparency creates alignment, and Key Results are always outcomes — not tasks. The 70% rule (a score of 0.7 is a success — lower and you set too easy; 1.0 and you set too conservative) forces ambition.

## When to use
Phase 1, Step 6 (for HR strategic objectives) and Phase 2, Pillar 8 (Performance Management implementation). OKRs are best suited to organisations that need cross-functional alignment, rapid iteration, and tolerance for ambitious targets — i.e., most growth-stage and innovation-driven companies. Not ideal for highly regulated, compliance-driven environments where process adherence is more important than outcome innovation.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Start at the company level: the CEO writes 3-5 company Objectives for the quarter. Each Objective should answer 'where do we want to go?' and be inspiring rather than purely operational. Then write 3-5 Key Results per Objective — each KR must be measurable (a number), time-bound (by end of quarter), and an outcome (not a task).
2. Cascade to business unit level: each BU MD writes their BU OKRs, ensuring at least 2 of their Objectives explicitly support a Company-level Objective. Allow 40% of BU OKRs to be bottom-up (things the BU team identifies as important that weren't in company OKRs).
3. Cascade to team and individual level using the same process. Important: this is not decomposition (breaking the company OKR into pieces). Each level writes their own OKRs aligned to, but not mechanically derived from, the level above.
4. Make all OKRs public across the organisation — this is the transparency that creates alignment. Use a shared platform (Workboard, Lattice, or even a shared Google Sheet) so anyone can see anyone else's OKRs.
5. Run weekly 10-minute OKR check-ins within each team: each Key Result owner reports current score (0.0-1.0) and any blockers. The focus is on what needs to change to move the score — not status reporting.
6. At the end of each quarter: score all Key Results (0.0 = not started, 0.7 = achieved target, 1.0 = exceeded). Run retrospectives. Set next quarter's OKRs within the first week of the new quarter — do not let there be a gap.

## Real-life example — Google
Google has used OKRs since Larry Page and Sergey Brin met John Doerr in 1999. At Google, all OKRs — including Sundar Pichai's personal OKRs — are visible to every employee. This radical transparency is cited by Google's leadership as a critical reason the company maintained strategic focus while growing from 10 to 100,000 employees. Google's OKR success also lies in enforcing the 70% rule: if a team consistently scores 1.0 on all Key Results, their OKRs are considered too easy and they are coached to set more ambitious targets. As a result, Google teams routinely set targets they know they will only partially achieve — which drives greater stretch than any traditional goal-setting process.

**So what:** The 70% rule is the most counter-intuitive and most important element of OKRs. Leaders who have never used OKRs instinctively resist it ('Why would I commit to something I might not achieve?'). Investing time in CEO and CFO education on this rule before OKR launch is essential to adoption.

## Template
Complete one OKR record per Objective. Each Objective should have 3-5 Key Results. Score Key Results weekly (0.0-1.0 scale). Review quarterly and set new OKRs within the first 5 business days of the new quarter.

- [ ] Objective (qualitative, directional, inspiring — not a metric)
- [ ] Why it matters this quarter (1-2 sentences connecting to company priority)
- [ ] Key Result 1: Statement / Baseline (current value) / Target / Score this week
- [ ] Key Result 2: Statement / Baseline / Target / Score this week
- [ ] Key Result 3: Statement / Baseline / Target / Score this week
- [ ] Key Result 4 (optional): Statement / Baseline / Target / Score this week
- [ ] Key Result 5 (optional): Statement / Baseline / Target / Score this week
- [ ] Objective Owner
- [ ] Key Result Owners (may differ from Objective Owner)
- [ ] Quarter End Score (average of KR scores)
- [ ] Retrospective Note (what enabled or blocked achievement?)

## Pitfalls
- Writing Key Results as tasks rather than outcomes. 'Launch the new onboarding programme' is a task (either done or not done). 'Increase 90-day retention rate of new hires from 82% to 92% by quarter end' is a Key Result. The distinction matters: tasks require execution; outcomes require problem-solving.
- Setting OKRs privately and revealing them only at the quarterly review. OKRs require public transparency to create the cross-team alignment that is the system's core value. If OKRs are not visible to all employees, you are running MBO, not OKR.
- Reviewing OKRs only at the quarterly score — without weekly check-ins, the system has no feedback loop. The weekly check-in (even 10 minutes per team) is the mechanism that drives course correction.

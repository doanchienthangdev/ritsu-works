# Power/Interest Grid (Stakeholder Analysis)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A 2×2 matrix that maps each stakeholder on two dimensions — Power (ability to influence or block the initiative's success) and Interest (degree of concern or involvement in the initiative's outcome) — producing four engagement strategy quadrants.

**Origin:** Developed by Aubrey Mendelow at the University of Pittsburgh in 1981 and presented at the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS). Further developed by Eden and Ackermann in 'Making Strategy: The Journey of Strategic Management' (1998). Now a standard tool in stakeholder management methodology.

## Why it works
The Power/Interest Grid works because it forces explicit, differentiated engagement strategies rather than treating all stakeholders as equally requiring the same communication. High-Power/High-Interest stakeholders (who can block the initiative and care deeply about it) require intensive, personalised engagement — not newsletters. Low-Power/Low-Interest stakeholders require only general communications — investing intensive engagement effort here wastes resources better spent elsewhere. The grid makes this resource allocation explicit and auditable.

## When to use
Phase 6 (Change Management), Step 4 — build as part of the stakeholder engagement strategy at programme inception. Also use any time an initiative encounters unexpected resistance, to map and address the resistance sources systematically.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. List all stakeholders — both individuals (CFO, Sales MD, key resistant manager) and groups (HR team, frontline managers, union representatives). Do not limit to obvious stakeholders — include those who might be affected but are not yet visible (e.g., payroll team if implementing a new HRIS).
2. For each stakeholder, rate Power on a 1-5 scale: '5' means they can veto or kill the initiative; '1' means they have no ability to influence its outcome. Rate Interest on 1-5: '5' means they are deeply concerned about the initiative; '1' means they are largely indifferent. Use specific evidence for each rating — not intuition.
3. Plot each stakeholder on the 2×2 grid. For each quadrant, assign the standard engagement strategy: Manage Closely / Keep Satisfied / Keep Informed / Monitor.
4. For every 'Manage Closely' stakeholder, complete a deeper individual analysis: What do they value? What is their current position (For/Neutral/Against)? What is their primary concern? What is the targeted engagement approach to address that concern? Who is the best person to deliver it (not always the CHRO)?
5. Build the Stakeholder Engagement Calendar: for each 'Manage Closely' and 'Keep Satisfied' stakeholder, schedule specific touchpoints in the next 12 weeks. For 'Keep Informed' stakeholders, schedule communication events (town halls, newsletters, Q&A). Make touchpoints named, dated, and owned.
6. Update the stakeholder map at monthly PMO reviews. Stakeholder positions — particularly Interest — shift as initiatives progress. A 'Monitor' stakeholder can become 'Manage Closely' if a change affects them more directly than anticipated.

## Real-life example — Unilever
During Unilever's Sustainable Living Plan implementation (2010-2020), the stakeholder analysis mapped NGOs and activist groups as High Power / High Interest — not an intuitive classification for a consumer goods company, but accurate given these groups' ability to influence consumer perception and regulatory response. Rather than managing NGOs with a 'Keep Informed' strategy (periodic press releases), Unilever adopted a 'Manage Closely' approach: direct engagement with Greenpeace, WWF, and Oxfam in joint working groups on specific sustainability targets. The result: organisations that could have been programme adversaries became co-architects and public advocates, reducing regulatory and reputational risk significantly.

**So what:** The Power/Interest Grid's most valuable insight is often counter-intuitive: stakeholders who appear external or peripheral to the initiative may hold significant power over its outcome. The mapping exercise forces teams to look beyond the obvious internal stakeholders and consider who in the broader ecosystem can help or hinder.

## Template
Complete the grid assessment with the full project team (not just the PM). Update monthly. For Manage Closely stakeholders, ensure each has a named engagement owner and a scheduled next touchpoint.

- [ ] Stakeholder Name / Group
- [ ] Stakeholder Role
- [ ] Power Rating (1-5) and Evidence
- [ ] Interest Rating (1-5) and Evidence
- [ ] Quadrant (Manage Closely / Keep Satisfied / Keep Informed / Monitor)
- [ ] Current Position (Strongly For / Mildly For / Neutral / Mildly Against / Strongly Against)
- [ ] Primary Concern or Objection
- [ ] Target Position
- [ ] Engagement Strategy
- [ ] Engagement Owner
- [ ] Next Touchpoint Date and Channel
- [ ] Last Contact Date
- [ ] Current Sentiment (1-5: 1=Strong Resistance → 5=Active Advocacy)

## Pitfalls
- Building the stakeholder map at project start and never updating it. Stakeholder power and interest are dynamic — a CFO who starts as 'Keep Satisfied' (high power, low initial interest) can become 'Manage Closely' the moment cost overruns are flagged. Set a monthly calendar reminder to review the grid.
- Conflating 'current position' with 'engagement strategy.' The engagement strategy is about what you do, not what they currently think. A 'Strongly Against' stakeholder in the 'Manage Closely' quadrant needs intensive engagement precisely because of their opposition — engagement does not mean appeasement.
- Omitting informal influencers from the map. Formal authority is only one source of power. A senior individual contributor who is widely respected and informally influential in the organisation may rate 3 on formal Power but exercise far more actual power than their position suggests. Include informal influencers in the assessment.

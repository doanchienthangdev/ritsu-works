# HR Health Dashboard

*Category: diagnosis · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A structured scorecard tracking 10-15 core HR metrics against industry benchmarks, colour-coded red/amber/green, that gives the CHRO and C-suite a one-page view of HR's current performance.

**Origin:** A practitioner tool synthesised from HR analytics methodologies at McKinsey, Deloitte, and Mercer. The specific benchmarks derive from annual surveys including SHRM's Human Capital Benchmarking Report and Mercer's Global Talent Trends study.

## Why it works
You cannot manage what you do not measure. The HR Health Dashboard creates a common factual baseline that converts subjective perceptions ('I think our recruiting is slow') into objective, benchmarked data points ('Our time-to-hire is 78 days vs. 45-day benchmark — we are 73% above peer average'). This specificity enables executive alignment and focused resource allocation.

## When to use
At the start of Phase 1 to establish the diagnostic baseline, and then monthly throughout the transformation programme. Also use as a 100-day diagnostic tool when a new CHRO joins.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Pull data from your HRIS for each metric category: time-to-hire (average days from job requisition to accepted offer), regrettable attrition (exits of rated 'meets expectations' or above, annualised), quality-of-hire (average performance rating of new hires at 6 and 12 months), offer acceptance rate (offers extended vs. accepted), engagement score (most recent survey % favourable).
2. Source benchmark data from SHRM, Mercer, or Gartner for your industry (e.g., technology services, financial services) and company size band (e.g., 1,000-5,000 employees). Use the same source consistently year-to-year to ensure comparability.
3. Apply the RAG scoring rules: Green if within 10% of benchmark or better; Amber if 10-25% below; Red if >25% below. For metrics where higher is better (e.g., engagement), reverse the logic.
4. For each red or amber metric, write a 1-2 sentence Root Cause hypothesis based on available qualitative data (exit interview themes, manager feedback, process audits). This hypothesis directs the Phase 2 pillar investigation.
5. Assign a metric owner and a target value per metric, with a target date. Ensure targets are calibrated to benchmark (not just 'improve by 10%') — the goal is to reach industry median or better.
6. Review the dashboard monthly at the HR leadership level; present a quarterly summary to the Steering Committee with trend lines (improving/flat/declining).

## Real-life example — GrowthCo (composite case)
At GrowthCo, the first HR Health Dashboard revealed five red metrics: time-to-hire at 78 days (vs. 45-day benchmark), regrettable attrition at 22% (vs. 12%), quality-of-hire at 3.2/5.0 (vs. 4.0 benchmark), succession coverage at 30% (vs. 90% best practice), and L&D spend at $600/employee/year (vs. $1,200 benchmark). This single dashboard enabled the CHRO to prioritise investment in Recruitment and Talent Development above other pillars, and gave the CEO a fact-based rationale for the $2.1M transformation budget.

**So what:** The HR Health Dashboard converts HR conversations from opinion-based to data-based. In executive settings, data-backed red metrics command attention and budget in ways that narrative alone cannot.

## Pitfalls
- Using internal benchmarks rather than external ones ('we improved from 90 days to 78 days time-to-hire' sounds positive but is still 73% above the 45-day industry benchmark). Always compare to external peers.
- Tracking output metrics only (e.g., attrition rate) without leading indicators (e.g., eNPS, 'flight risk' scores from engagement surveys, manager satisfaction scores). By the time attrition appears in the dashboard, it is already a lagging indicator — the problem happened 6-12 months earlier.
- Building the dashboard but not assigning clear metric owners. Every metric needs a named individual accountable for the corrective action — the CHRO owns the dashboard as a whole, but each metric needs a pillar-level owner.

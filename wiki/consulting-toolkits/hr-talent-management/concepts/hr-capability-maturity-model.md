# HR Capability Maturity Model

*Category: diagnosis · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A five-level maturity ladder applied to each of the 10 HR pillars to objectively score current capability and define a target state, creating the evidence-based gap analysis that drives Phase 2 pillar design.

**Origin:** Adapted from the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI), originally developed by the Software Engineering Institute at Carnegie Mellon University in 1986 and widely applied to HR by McKinsey and Deloitte HR transformation practices from the 2000s onward.

## Why it works
Maturity models work because they replace vague perceptions ('our HR is weak') with specific, behaviourally anchored levels that a group of stakeholders can agree on and measure over time. The five-level structure creates a natural roadmap: moving from Level 2 to Level 3 requires a specific set of changes that are knowable in advance, making transformation planning concrete rather than aspirational.

## When to use
At the start of any HR transformation (Phase 1, Step 3) and then annually to track maturity improvement. Also use as a baseline tool in any post-merger HR integration or when a new CHRO needs to quickly diagnose the function.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define five behavioural anchors per pillar per maturity level — write 2-3 specific, observable behaviours that a practitioner would see if the pillar were at that level. Do this before the workshop.
2. Design a structured questionnaire: for each pillar, present the five-level descriptions and ask participants to select the level that best describes current reality, with a required justification text field.
3. Convene a facilitated workshop of 15-20 participants (HR leaders + 5-8 business stakeholders who interact with HR). Collect anonymous individual scores first using the questionnaire.
4. Present aggregated scores per pillar. Facilitate discussion on any pillar where scores span more than 2 levels — resolve through structured debate asking each participant to cite specific evidence.
5. Agree the current maturity level per pillar by consensus (or median vote with documented dissent). Plot the current profile as a radar chart.
6. Repeat the exercise for the desired state: 'where should each pillar be in 18-24 months to deliver the HR strategy?' Agree target levels with the CHRO and at least two C-suite sponsors.
7. Calculate the gap per pillar (target minus current) and rank pillars by gap size weighted by strategic priority. This ranking directly drives Phase 2 design investment.

## Real-life example — Microsoft
When Kathleen Hogan became CHRO at Microsoft in 2014, she used a maturity-model framework to diagnose the HR function across all key talent dimensions. The assessment revealed that talent development and performance management were at Level 1-2 (ad hoc, individual heroics), while the company's transformation to a growth-mindset culture required Level 4. This diagnosis provided the CEO (Satya Nadella) with a credible, specific picture of what needed to change and a phased roadmap to achieve it. Microsoft's HR transformation over 2014-2020 — including replacing its forced-ranking system and implementing a growth-feedback model — followed the maturity upgrade path the diagnostic identified.

**So what:** A maturity model gives the CEO and CFO a common language to discuss HR capability gaps — translating 'we need better talent development' into 'we are at Level 2 and need to reach Level 4 within 18 months, which requires these three specific investments.' This specificity is what unlocks executive sponsorship and budget.

## Pitfalls
- Conducting the assessment with only HR participants — business stakeholders will rate HR pillars differently from HR leaders, and the gap between those perspectives is itself diagnostic. Always include 5-8 business stakeholders in the workshop.
- Setting the target state too conservatively (e.g., targeting Level 3 when the business strategy requires Level 4). Challenge the team: 'If you were at Level 3 in this pillar, would the business strategy succeed?' Anchor targets to business requirements, not to what seems achievable.
- Running the maturity assessment once and never repeating it. The model is a tracking tool, not a one-time diagnostic. Reassess every 12 months to measure progress and recalibrate targets.

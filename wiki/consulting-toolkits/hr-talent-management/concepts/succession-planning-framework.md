# Succession Planning Framework

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A structured process for identifying and developing internal candidates to fill critical leadership roles — ensuring that the organisation can sustain leadership continuity across key positions without dependence on external recruitment.

**Origin:** Succession planning as a formal management discipline was established at GE in the 1980s under Jack Welch, who devoted an estimated 30% of his time to talent and succession management. The framework was formalised in management literature by William Rothwell ('Effective Succession Planning', 2001) and extended by McKinsey's talent research in the 2000s.

## Why it works
The fundamental business case for succession planning is risk management: a critical role with no ready successor creates an existential leadership risk. Research (Corporate Leadership Council) shows that internal successors reach full performance in their roles 2x faster than external hires in the same roles — because they already know the culture, relationships, and context. Succession planning also drives talent engagement: high-potentials who know they are on a succession track are significantly more likely to stay.

## When to use
Phase 2, Pillar 6 (Talent Development) — build during the initial HR transformation and then maintain as an ongoing annual process. Also run as a standalone 100-day exercise when a new CEO or CHRO joins and needs to assess the leadership bench.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define 'critical roles': those whose vacancy for more than 3 months would materially damage business performance. Start with the top leadership roles (ExCo, BU MDs) and extend to critical technical roles (Chief Architect, Top Sales Producer). Aim for the top 20-30 roles.
2. For each critical role, identify 2-3 potential successors at different readiness levels: 'Ready Now' (could step in today with minimal disruption), '1-2 Year Ready' (needs targeted development in a specific gap), and '3-5 Year Ready' (high-potential who needs sustained development over multiple years).
3. Use the 9-Box Talent Grid to identify '1-2 Year' and '3-5 Year' successors. Box 8 and 9 employees are the natural pool. Box 6 employees (high performers with moderate potential) may also be viable successors for the next level with targeted development.
4. For each identified successor, write a one-page development plan specifically targeted at closing the gap to readiness: What experience does the successor need that they don't currently have? What knowledge must they acquire? Who should they be developing a relationship with? Assign an executive sponsor.
5. Identify 'critical succession risks': roles with no 'Ready Now' successor AND no plausible 1-2 Year successor. These are your highest-risk positions — they require either accelerated development or a targeted external search to build the bench.
6. Review the succession plan annually at the Steering Committee level (not just HR). Include the CEO in any review of the top 5-10 critical roles. The visibility of succession planning in the executive team signals its strategic importance.
7. Track succession plan health as a KPI: % of critical roles with at least one 'Ready Now' successor (target: >80%), % with at least two successors at any readiness level (target: >90%).

## Real-life example — GE
Jack Welch's succession planning process at GE is widely regarded as the gold standard. Welch began planning his own succession 7-8 years before his retirement in 2001. The process produced three internal finalists (Jeff Immelt, Jim McNerney, Robert Nardelli) — all of whom went on to lead major Fortune 500 companies (GE, Boeing, and Home Depot respectively). Welch committed to personally spending one-third of his time on talent development and succession — a time allocation that signalled to the entire organisation that succession was a strategic priority, not an HR administrative exercise.

**So what:** The quality of a succession plan is determined by the depth of development investment in potential successors, not by the quality of the spreadsheet that tracks them. A succession plan without funded, assigned development actions for each identified successor is a list of names, not a succession plan.

## Pitfalls
- Building a succession plan that is a spreadsheet of names without development actions. The spreadsheet is the output of the planning — but the plan IS the development actions. If a listed successor's development plan has not been funded and is not being executed, the organisation has a false sense of succession security.
- Including only the top 10-15 senior leadership roles in the succession plan. Critical roles exist at all levels — technical specialists, frontline sales leaders, key account managers — whose departure would equally create business disruption. Expand the succession plan beyond the executive suite.
- Running the succession review as an HR-only exercise. Succession plans must be reviewed by the CEO and relevant C-suite leaders — not because HR cannot run the process, but because the conversation itself (who is ready, who needs development) requires executive-level judgment and commitment.

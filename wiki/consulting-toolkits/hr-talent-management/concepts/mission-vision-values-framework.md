# HR Mission, Vision & Values Framework

*Category: strategy · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A structured co-creation process that produces three artefacts — a Mission statement (why HR exists), a Vision statement (what HR aspires to be in 3-5 years), and a set of Values (4-6 operating principles) — that define HR's organisational identity and guide all talent decisions.

**Origin:** Derived from the canonical corporate Mission/Vision/Values methodology formalised in management literature by Peter Drucker ('The Purpose of a Business', 1954) and Collins & Porras ('Built to Last', 1994). Applied specifically to HR functions by McKinsey's 'HR as a Strategic Partner' research in the early 2000s.

## Why it works
An HR function without a clear Mission, Vision, and Values operates reactively, is unable to articulate its purpose to the business, and cannot make consistent decisions when priorities conflict. The MVV framework provides a decision hierarchy: the Mission defines the 'why' that makes every HR investment justifiable; the Vision defines the 'where' that makes the roadmap directional; the Values define the 'how' that makes day-to-day decisions consistent and predictable.

## When to use
Phase 1, Step 5 — after the diagnostic work (Steps 1-4) gives you the factual foundation, but before setting strategic objectives and KPIs. The MVV defines the direction; the objectives and KPIs measure progress toward it.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Before the workshop, collect the corporate Mission, Vision, and Values. HR's MVV must be derivative — it exists to serve the corporate mission, not as an independent purpose.
2. Facilitate a 3-hour workshop with the HR leadership team (8-12 people). Start with the Mission: write the stem 'Our HR function exists to...' and ask each participant to complete it independently in 60 seconds. Collect all responses (use sticky notes or digital whiteboards), cluster by theme, and vote for the strongest 2-3 themes.
3. From the top-voted themes, draft three candidate Mission statements. Test each against the 'elevator test': if you said this to any employee in the organisation, would they understand what HR is for? Vote for the strongest one. Revise and tighten.
4. Repeat the process for Vision: 'In 3-5 years, our HR function will be known for...' The Vision should be aspirational but credible — attainable with significant effort, not a fantasy. Draft, vote, revise.
5. For Values: ask each participant to write down 3 behaviours they believe should be non-negotiable in how the HR team operates. Cluster responses into 4-6 themes. For each theme, write a Values statement plus a 'we do / we don't' behavioural anchor that makes it concrete.
6. Review the full MVV with the CEO and 2-3 Business Unit MDs: 'Does this Mission accurately reflect what you need from HR?' Incorporate feedback. Avoid the temptation to be generic — specificity is what makes MVV meaningful.

## Real-life example — Netflix
Netflix's famous HR philosophy (articulated in the 'Netflix Culture Deck' by Patty McCord, 2009) is a practitioner example of values-led HR operating principles. Netflix's core HR value — 'we are a team, not a family' — is a concrete, differentiated value that drives real decisions (performance-based outcomes over loyalty-based tenure). Each value is supported by explicit behavioural anchors: 'Highly aligned, loosely coupled' as a management principle; 'Adequate performance gets a generous severance package' as a consequences principle. The specificity of the Netflix culture artefact is why it became a benchmark — generic values ('integrity, teamwork, excellence') produce no decisions.

**So what:** The test of a Values statement is whether it could be used to justify a real HR decision. 'We develop before we recruit externally' is a value that changes recruitment decisions. 'Teamwork' is a platitude that changes nothing.

## Template
Complete all three sections. Validate with the CEO and C-suite before publishing. Embed in HR team induction, governance charters, and manager training. Review annually.

## Pitfalls
- Writing a Mission that could apply to any HR function in any company ('We attract, develop, and retain talent'). A Mission statement that is not specific to your company's context and strategy is meaningless. The Mission should include something that differentiates your company's talent approach.
- Workshopping MVV with only HR leaders — the business perspective is critical. At minimum, validate the final artefacts with the CEO and 2-3 BU MDs before publishing.
- Publishing MVV and never referencing it again. The MVV should be cited in every HR strategic review, every governance document, and every major HR decision. If nobody references it in the quarterly HR review, it is not serving its purpose.

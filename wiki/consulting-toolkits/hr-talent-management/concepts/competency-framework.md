# Competency Framework (Three-Tier Model)

*Category: analysis · Toolkit: HR & Talent Management*

## What it is
A structured taxonomy of the skills, knowledge, and behaviours required for success across all roles in the organisation, organised into three tiers: organisational competencies (all employees), leadership competencies (people managers+), and functional competencies (by job family).

**Origin:** Competency modelling was introduced by David McClelland (Harvard) in 1973 ('Testing for Competence Rather Than for Intelligence'). The three-tier structure was formalised by Lominger International (now Korn Ferry) through the Lominger Competency Library (360+ behavioural descriptors across 67 leadership competencies), widely used by McKinsey, GE, and IBM.

## Why it works
Competency frameworks work because they make the definition of 'good' at a job explicit and consistent. Without a framework, every manager defines 'good performance' subjectively and differently — leading to inconsistent hiring decisions, biased performance ratings, and unfair promotion processes. By defining competencies with behavioural anchors at multiple proficiency levels, organisations create a common language for talent decisions that is both objective and defensible.

## Step-by-step tutorial
1. Define the scope: which role families exist in the organisation? (Typical families: Commercial, Operations, Technology, Finance, People, Legal, Executive.) Create a role family catalogue if one does not already exist.
2. Define organisational competencies — the 4-6 behaviours every employee must demonstrate regardless of role. These should directly reflect the company's values. Example: if 'Customer First' is a company value, then 'Customer Orientation' is an organisational competency. Avoid more than 6 — competency frameworks with 20+ competencies are used by nobody.
3. Define leadership competencies — the 6-10 additional behaviours required of people managers. Focus on: developing others, driving results through teams, managing ambiguity, executive communication, and strategic thinking. Use research-validated leadership competency libraries (Lominger, Korn Ferry, or custom) as a starting point.
4. For each role family, identify 4-8 functional competencies — the technical knowledge and skills specific to that family. Validate with senior practitioners in each family (not just HR).
5. Write behavioural indicators at three proficiency levels for each competency. The format: 'At Level [1/2/3], an employee [observable behaviour]. Example: [specific, real example of this behaviour in action].' Behavioural anchors should be specific enough that two different managers independently reading them would rate the same employee the same way.
6. Integrate the framework into all talent processes: use competencies in job descriptions, structured interview guides (behavioural interview questions derived from competency indicators), IDP planning (competency gap → development action), performance reviews (competency ratings alongside goal achievement), and promotion criteria.

## Real-life example — IBM
IBM's global competency framework (the 'IBM Leadership Competencies') has been integrated into every talent decision process since the 1990s. IBM's framework includes 10 leadership competencies (including 'Collaboration', 'Systemic Thinking', 'Decisiveness under Uncertainty') each with behavioural indicators at three levels. During IBM's transformation from a hardware to a services and consulting company (2000-2015), the framework was revised to add 'Client Focus' and 'Digital Fluency' as new core competencies — a signal to the organisation that the expected behaviours for success had changed. This competency-change communication was more effective than the CEO's public announcements in shifting employee behaviour.

**So what:** Competency frameworks are most powerful not when they are introduced, but when they are updated. When IBM added 'Digital Fluency' to its leadership competencies, every manager understood that their promotion and development investment now depended on developing that skill. The framework revision itself was a cultural change mechanism.

## Pitfalls
- Building a competency framework of 30-40 competencies. Nobody uses a framework with this many dimensions — it collapses under its own weight. Research (Korn Ferry) shows the maximum usable number in a single assessment is 12-15 competencies. Build a tight, high-priority set and add others only when needed.
- Writing competency definitions in abstract language ('demonstrates strategic agility') rather than observable behaviours. If a manager cannot directly observe the behaviour in a work situation, the competency is not assessable. Every definition must pass the 'can I watch this happening?' test.
- Designing the framework in HR and deploying it to the business without deep practitioner co-creation. Frameworks designed exclusively by HR are perceived as irrelevant by the business. For each role family, involve the top 3-5 performers in that family in the competency definition — their language and their examples.

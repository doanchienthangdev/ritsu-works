# HR & Talent Management

*A 6-phase system to transform HR into a strategic talent engine that attracts, develops, and retains top performers.*

**Core value:** The ability to build a talent engine that consistently attracts, develops, and retains high performers — turning HR from a cost centre into a measurable competitive advantage.

---

## Buying This Toolkit Gives You One Thing: A Running System

A McKinsey survey found that superior talent is up to 8 times more productive than average talent in high-complexity jobs. Yet the same research revealed that 82% of companies don't believe they recruit highly talented people — and only 7% of those that do think they can keep them. HR, in most organisations, is barely contributing to performance.

This toolkit exists to close that gap. It gives you the complete, executable architecture: a strategy, 10 operational pillars, a financial business-case process, a sequenced roadmap, and a change-management playbook. You do not need to know what comes next — the process tells you.

---

## When to Use This Toolkit

Deploy this toolkit when your organisation faces one or more of:

- **Talent acquisition failure**: inability to fill critical roles within target time-to-hire, or consistently low quality-of-hire scores.
- **Retention crisis**: high regrettable attrition among top performers (typically signalled when the regrettable attrition rate exceeds 10–15%).
- **Leadership pipeline risk**: succession gaps for senior roles identified by a board or CEO review.
- **Post-merger HR integration**: two HR functions need to be consolidated into one coherent operating model.
- **Rapid scaling**: the business is growing faster than the HR function can support — headcount growing >30% per year.
- **Strategic mandate**: the CEO or board has tasked HR with becoming a strategic partner rather than an administrative function.

**Who it's for:** CHROs, HR Directors, HR Business Partners, management consultants on people-strategy engagements, CEOs, and PE operating partners conducting 100-day HR assessments.

## Phase 1: HR & Talent Management Strategy

### Goal
Define where HR is today, where it needs to be, and the strategic objectives and KPIs required to close that gap — fully anchored to the corporate and business strategy.

### Step 1.1 — Summarise the Corporate and Business Strategy

Extract the top 3–5 strategic priorities from the corporate plan and each business unit. Map every business priority to its talent dependency — this single mapping is what converts HR from a support function into a strategic asset. Conduct 5–10 structured CEO/executive interviews.

*Input:* Corporate strategy, business unit plans, executive interviews.
*Output:* One-page Corporate & Business Strategy Summary with talent implications.
*Owner:* CHRO / HR Strategy Lead.

### Step 1.2 — Conduct HR Analysis

Run a comprehensive diagnostic: headcount, cost, time-to-hire, regrettable attrition, engagement, L&D spend, performance distribution, diversity metrics. Benchmark every metric against industry peers (SHRM, Mercer). Output: HR Health Dashboard (red/amber/green per metric).

### Step 1.3 — Assess HR Capability Maturity

Apply the HR Capability Maturity Model (five levels: Initial → Managed → Defined → Quantitatively Managed → Optimising) across all 10 pillars in a facilitated workshop. The gap between current and target maturity per pillar drives Phase 2 priorities.

### Step 1.4 — Define Current State and Target State

Convert the maturity radar into a structured table: one row per pillar, columns for current maturity, current pain points, target maturity, target outcomes, and target date. Facilitate executive alignment on the target state.

### Step 1.5 — Define HR Mission, Vision & Values

Facilitate a 3-hour co-creation workshop with the HR leadership team. HR's mission must be derivative of the business mission. Produce Mission (why HR exists), Vision (3–5 year aspiration), and Values (4–6 principles with behavioural 'we do / we don't' anchors).

### Step 1.6 — Set Strategic Objectives and KPIs

Translate Mission/Vision into 5–8 OKRs. For each objective: 2–3 leading KPIs and 1–2 lagging KPIs, all SMART. Establish measurement cadence. Output: HR Strategic Scorecard.

### Step 1.7 — Define HR Team Structure, Budget and Service Delivery Model

Apply the HRBP model (Strategic BPs + Centres of Excellence + Shared Services). Size using HR-to-employee ratio benchmarks (Mercer: 1.4 FTE per 100 employees for mid-size firms). Build a 3-year HR budget. Define what is insourced, outsourced, or automated.

### Step 1.8 — Establish Guiding Principles

Derive 6–10 operating principles that resolve trade-offs. Embed in governance charters and manager communications.

**Phase 1 deliverable:** HR & Talent Management Strategy Document — strategy-on-a-page, diagnostics, maturity radar, scorecard, org design, budget.

**Phase 1 gate:** CHRO and at least two C-suite sponsors formally sign off.

## Phase 2: 10 Pillars to Reach HR Strategic Objectives

### Goal
Design the detailed operating model for each of the 10 HR pillars — defining desired capabilities, diagnosis, target state, sub-modules, and action plans.

### Pillar 1: Capability Management
Map desired capabilities to business priorities. Use a gap heat-map from 360-degree assessments and skills-inventory data. Prioritise the top 5–7 capability gaps. Define development interventions for each.

### Pillar 2: Structure & Governance
Assess organisational structure. Map all HR roles in a RACI. Define governance cadences: monthly strategic reviews, quarterly board updates, weekly operational huddles. Size workforce and define location model.

### Pillar 3: Culture
Assess current culture using the Competing Values Framework (Quinn & Rohrbaugh). Identify the dominant culture type (Clan / Adhocracy / Hierarchy / Market). Define desired culture. Build a culture-change action plan targeting the top 3–5 levers (leadership behaviour, rituals, reward alignment, communication).

### Pillar 4: Competency Management
Build a three-tier Competency Framework: organisational competencies (all employees), leadership competencies (managers+), functional competencies (by job family). For each, write behavioural indicators at three proficiency levels. Link to career progression and competency-based assessment.

### Pillar 5: Recruitment
Build the Employee Value Proposition (EVP) from employee stay-surveys and competitor benchmarking. Design the end-to-end recruitment process with SLAs. Integrate AI tools for screening and scheduling. Implement inclusive hiring practices (structured interviews, diverse shortlists). Track: time-to-hire, cost-per-hire, quality-of-hire, offer acceptance rate.

### Pillar 6: Talent Development
Use the 9-Box Talent Grid (Performance × Potential) as the foundation. Build differentiated development strategies per grid segment. Create Individual Development Plans (IDPs) for key talent. Apply the 70:20:10 learning model. Build succession plans for all critical roles.

### Pillar 7: Mentoring
Design a formal mentoring programme: define objectives, build a mentor-mentee matching algorithm, install a mentoring culture, define governance (duration, cadence, conversation guides), and measure impact via pre/post competency assessments. Add Reverse Mentoring for digital and generational knowledge transfer.

### Pillar 8: Performance Management
Select the right model: OKR for agile/innovation organisations; Balanced Scorecard for multi-dimensional enterprises. Design the Review Cycle (quarterly OKR reviews + annual calibrated appraisals). Train managers on the SBI (Situation-Behaviour-Impact) feedback model. Define calibration processes for rating consistency.

### Pillar 9: Reward & Recognition
Conduct Compensation Benchmarking (Mercer, Radford) to set base salary at P50 (standard) or P75 (scarce/critical roles). Design Variable Pay programmes. Build a transparent pay-band structure. Design a Recognition Programme (formal awards + informal peer-recognition). Ensure pay equity and legal compliance.

### Pillar 10: Reassignment & Termination
Define protocols for four scenarios: internal reassignment (skills-matching algorithm + 90-day integration plan), performance-based termination (progressive disciplinary process), downsizing (compliant workforce reduction with outplacement + survivor engagement), and voluntary attrition management (exit interviews, stay interviews, root-cause analysis).

**Phase 2 deliverable:** 10-Pillar HR Operating Model — detailed blueprints for all 10 pillars.

**Phase 2 gate:** All blueprints completed, validated by pillar leads and CHRO, reviewed by business sponsors.

## Phase 3: Potential Initiatives for Each Pillar

### Goal
Generate a comprehensive long-list of HR initiatives across all 10 pillars without filtering prematurely.

### Step 3.1 — Ideation workshops per pillar
For each pillar, facilitate a 90-minute ideation session using the 'How Might We' design-thinking prompt. Generate 8–12 ideas per pillar before filtering. Use affinity mapping to group themes.

### Step 3.2 — Structure each initiative
Complete a one-page Initiative Brief for each candidate: Initiative Name, Pillar, Problem statement, Proposed solution, Scope, Dependencies, Preliminary benefits. Standardisation enables like-for-like comparison.

### Step 3.3 — Screen for feasibility
Apply a binary feasibility filter: legal/compliant? within budget envelope? has a realistic owner? Initiatives failing any filter go to a 'future pipeline.' This reduces the long-list from ~100 to a prioritisable 40–60 initiatives.

**Phase 3 deliverable:** Screened Initiative Medium-List — 40–60 structured Initiative Briefs.

**Phase 3 gate:** CHRO and Finance agree the list is comprehensive enough to proceed to detailed financial analysis.

## Phase 4: Business Cases and Financial Models

### Goal
Quantify the expected value and cost of each initiative so that leadership can make informed, financially grounded prioritisation decisions.

### Step 4.1 — Build financial models
For each initiative, build a standardised 3-year financial model. Benefits: quantify using HR ROI formulas (e.g., attrition reduction × cost-to-replace; productivity gain × salary × uplift factor). Costs: implementation (one-time) + run cost (ongoing). Calculate NPV at the company's hurdle rate and ROI. Apply sensitivity analysis.

**HR ROI formula for attrition:**
Annual benefit = Number of employees × Current attrition rate × Cost-to-replace per employee × Expected retention improvement %

**HR ROI formula for productivity:**
Annual benefit = Impacted headcount × Average salary × Productivity uplift factor

### Step 4.2 — Write executive business cases
For each initiative, write a 2-page executive Business Case: Problem/Opportunity → Proposed Solution → Financial Summary (NPV, ROI, payback) → Non-Financial Benefits → Implementation Plan → Resources → Risks → Recommendation. Initiatives >$500K must be co-signed by Finance.

### Step 4.3 — Validate assumptions
For the 15–20 highest-NPV initiatives, conduct assumption-validation interviews with internal SMEs and external benchmarks. Document each assumption's source and confidence level. Low-confidence assumptions trigger wider sensitivity ranges.

**Phase 4 deliverable:** Business Case Portfolio — validated financial models and executive business cases for all screened initiatives.

**Phase 4 gate:** CHRO and CFO accept the portfolio as a credible basis for prioritisation. At least 60% of initiatives must show positive NPV.

---

## Phase 5: Prioritisation, Business Roadmap, and Implementation

### Goal
Select the optimal portfolio, sequence it into a 3-year roadmap, establish governance, and execute.

### Step 5.1 — Prioritise the initiative portfolio
Apply the GE-McKinsey Matrix adapted for HR: a 3×3 grid of Strategic Value vs. Implementation Feasibility. Strategic Value is scored with weighted criteria (strategic alignment 40%, NPV 40%, risk reduction 20%). Initiatives in the High-High quadrant are immediate priorities. Overlay a portfolio view to ensure balance across 10 pillars.

### Step 5.2 — Build the HR Business Roadmap
Sequence prioritised initiatives into three horizons:
- **Horizon 1 (0–12 months):** Quick wins and foundational capabilities
- **Horizon 2 (12–24 months):** Capability enhancements
- **Horizon 3 (24–36 months):** Transformation ambitions

Define initiative interdependencies. Assign resource requirements per quarter.

### Step 5.3 — Establish programme governance
Three-tier structure:
1. **Steering Committee (monthly):** CHRO + C-suite + CFO — portfolio health, escalations, major changes
2. **PMO (weekly):** HR Programme Manager + workstream leads — RAG status, interdependencies, blockers
3. **Project Teams:** Initiative delivery

Maintain RAID log (Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies) and portfolio dashboard.

### Step 5.4 — Implement using the right methodology
- **Agile/Scrum:** Digital and technology HR initiatives — 2-week sprints, daily stand-ups, retrospectives
- **Design Thinking:** Culture, employee experience, new programme design — research → prototype → pilot → scale
- **Waterfall:** Compliance and infrastructure projects — structured phases with formal sign-off gates

### Step 5.5 — Continuous improvement and post-project evaluation
At each project close: Post-Project Evaluation comparing actual vs. promised benefits. At programme end: Post-Programme Evaluation measuring aggregate HR maturity improvement and total ROI. Embed monthly Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycles.

**Phase 5 deliverable:** 3-Year HR Business Roadmap with governance, implementation playbooks, and continuous-improvement mechanism.

**Phase 5 gate:** Steering Committee approves roadmap and governance. First-year initiatives have signed project charters.

## Phase 6: Change Management, Internal Communication, and Stakeholder Engagement

### Goal
Ensure every initiative is adopted and behaviour changes are sustained — preventing the 70% initiative failure rate caused by poor change management.

### Step 6.1 — Define the change management strategy
Apply the ADKAR model (Awareness → Desire → Knowledge → Ability → Reinforcement) as both a diagnostic and intervention framework. For each major initiative, assess where the target population sits on the ADKAR curve. Identify the primary change barrier and build the intervention accordingly.

### Step 6.2 — Build change management plans
For each major initiative, produce a 5-component Change Management Plan:
1. Sponsorship model (visible, active sponsors at each level)
2. Manager coaching plan (what managers need to know and do)
3. Training plan (knowledge/skill gaps, modality, target populations)
4. Resistance management plan (who resists, why, targeted engagement)
5. Reinforcement plan (rewards, recognition, accountability mechanisms)

### Step 6.3 — Design and execute internal communication strategy
Build a Communication Calendar for the full 3-year programme. Segment by audience (all employees, managers, senior leaders, HR) and channel. Apply the WIIFM ('What's In It For Me') principle — every message answers the audience's implicit question. Use the narrative arc: Why change → What changes → How you will be supported → Where to get help.

### Step 6.4 — Execute stakeholder engagement
Map stakeholders on a Power/Interest Grid:
- **Manage closely** (High Power/High Interest): bi-weekly 1:1 updates, involve in decisions
- **Keep satisfied** (High Power/Low Interest): monthly executive summary, proactive risk flags
- **Keep informed** (Low Power/High Interest): newsletters, Q&A sessions
- **Monitor** (Low Power/Low Interest): general communications only

Maintain a live Stakeholder Engagement Tracker with engagement actions and sentiment.

### Step 6.5 — Track, measure and course-correct
Monthly Change Health Dashboard tracking: ADKAR scores (pulse survey), adoption rate, manager cascade quality, issue/escalation volume. When ADKAR scores drop below 60%, trigger sponsor escalation within 2 weeks.

**Phase 6 deliverable:** Integrated Change Management Programme — ADKAR strategy, per-initiative change plans, Communication Calendar, Stakeholder Tracker, Change Health Dashboard.

**Phase 6 gate:** ADKAR composite score >70, adoption rate >80% for all major initiatives, post-programme evaluation completed.

## The Frameworks & Tools

*(Full anatomy — description, visual, tutorial, example, template, pitfalls — for each framework follows below. Each is also available as a standalone reference card.)*

### HR Capability Maturity Model
A 5-level maturity ladder (Initial → Managed → Defined → Quantitatively Managed → Optimising) applied to each of the 10 HR pillars. Derived from the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI), adapted for people functions. The maturity model forces specificity: instead of 'we need to improve our talent development', it yields 'Talent Development is at Level 2 (Managed) and needs to reach Level 4 (Quantitatively Managed) within 18 months — which means X, Y, Z behavioural changes.' **Tutorial:** (1) Define 5 behavioural anchors per pillar per maturity level; (2) Conduct a facilitated workshop (15–20 participants, structured questionnaire, anonymous scoring); (3) Calculate average score per pillar; (4) Facilitate discussion on outlier scores; (5) Agree target maturity level per pillar with a completion date; (6) Calculate the gap and feed into Phase 2 pillar design. **Real example:** Microsoft's HR function used a maturity-model framework in its 2014–2019 transformation under CHRO Kathleen Hogan, progressing from siloed administrative HR (Level 1–2) to a data-driven talent engine (Level 4) that CNBC cited as central to Microsoft's tripling of market cap.

### 9-Box Talent Grid
A 3×3 matrix plotting employees on Performance (Low/Medium/High) vs. Potential (Low/Medium/High), creating nine distinct talent segments. Originated at McKinsey & Company and GE in the 1970s as part of the 'War for Talent' methodology. The logic: performance alone is backward-looking; potential (learning agility, leadership aspiration, capability ceiling) is forward-looking. Both dimensions together yield a complete picture for talent investment decisions. **Tutorial:** (1) Define 'potential' behaviourally using the organisation's leadership model; (2) Have each manager rate their direct reports independently on both dimensions (1–3 scale each); (3) Facilitate a talent-calibration session — managers present ratings and defend them; the group calibrates for consistency; (4) Map all employees to the grid; (5) Define the standard development intervention for each box; (6) Identify the top 10–15% ('Consistent Stars' and 'Future Stars') for accelerated development and succession. **Real example:** GE's talent-calibration sessions using this grid (called the 'Session C' process) became the gold standard. Jack Welch used box-by-box differentiated investment to build GE's legendary leadership pipeline in the 1980s–1990s. **Pitfall:** Using performance ratings from the appraisal system without calibration inflates box 9; calibrate first.

### ADKAR Change Model
A five-milestone model for individual change: Awareness (why the change is needed), Desire (personal motivation to support it), Knowledge (how to change), Ability (skills and behaviours to implement), Reinforcement (sustained adoption). Created by Jeff Hiatt at Prosci in 1998, based on research into 700 organisations. The model's key insight: change fails when the intervention addresses the wrong milestone. If people lack Awareness, adding a training programme (which addresses Knowledge) accomplishes nothing. **Tutorial:** (1) For each initiative, identify the target population; (2) Run a 5-question ADKAR pulse survey (one question per milestone, rated 1–5); (3) Find the lowest-scoring milestone — this is the 'first barrier'; (4) Design the intervention specifically for that milestone; (5) Re-survey 6 weeks later to confirm progression; (6) Only move to the next milestone once the previous one scores ≥4/5. **Real example:** Siemens used Prosci's ADKAR framework in its 100,000-employee digital transformation (2018–2021), achieving 78% adoption of new digital tools within 12 months — well above the industry average — by systematically addressing Awareness and Desire before deploying any training. **Pitfall:** Running ADKAR assessments once and then filing them. ADKAR is a recurring diagnostic — run it every 4–6 weeks per initiative.

### OKR Framework
Objectives and Key Results: a goal-setting methodology in which each Objective is qualitative and directional (motivating but not easily measurable), supported by 3–5 Key Results that are quantitative, time-bound, and measurable. Created by Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s, popularised by John Doerr who introduced it to Google in 1999. The logic: OKRs align every team and individual to the same strategic priorities through transparency (all OKRs are public) and cadence (quarterly review cycles). **Tutorial:** (1) Set company-level OKRs (CEO-owned, covering the top 3–5 strategic priorities); (2) Cascade: each business unit sets OKRs aligned to company OKRs; (3) Each team and individual sets OKRs aligned to business-unit OKRs; (4) Ensure 60% of OKRs are ambitious enough to achieve only 70% (stretch goals — scoring 0.7 is a success); (5) Hold weekly check-ins (15-minute stand-up per team on OKR progress); (6) Hold quarterly OKR review meetings to score progress and set new OKRs. **Real example:** Google has used OKRs since 1999 across all 80,000+ employees. Sundar Pichai publishes his personal OKRs internally, modelling the transparency the system requires. Google credits OKRs as a critical enabler of its ability to scale without losing strategic focus. **Pitfall:** Setting 'business as usual' Key Results that would be achieved without any extra effort. OKRs should require 60–70% effort uplift.

### Competing Values Framework
A culture-assessment and design framework that maps organisational culture on two axes: Internal vs. External focus (does the organisation optimise internally or for the market?) and Stability vs. Flexibility (does it value control or adaptability?). Creates four culture quadrants: Clan (internal + flexible = family-like, people-focused); Adhocracy (external + flexible = innovative, entrepreneurial); Market (external + stable = results-oriented, competitive); Hierarchy (internal + stable = process-driven, efficient). Developed by Robert Quinn and John Rohrbaugh at the University of Michigan in 1983. **Tutorial:** (1) Survey a representative cross-section of employees (25–50 people) using the Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI) — 6 questions, each allocating 100 points across four culture descriptions; (2) Average scores by quadrant and plot the current culture profile as a diamond; (3) Ask the same respondents to complete the survey for the desired culture; (4) Plot the desired profile; (5) The gap between current and desired highlights the culture-change priorities; (6) Design interventions targeting the specific quadrant levers (leadership behaviour, rituals, symbols, reward mechanisms). **Real example:** Spotify assessed its culture using CVF-adjacent tools during its 2014 restructuring from a hierarchy to an Adhocracy (the Squad/Tribe/Chapter model). The CVF gap identified that the company had Hierarchy habits in engineering despite needing Adhocracy — the squad model was the structural intervention that shifted it. **Pitfall:** Designing the desired culture without CEO and board alignment. The CEO's natural style will revert culture unless they explicitly model the desired quadrant.

### 70:20:10 Learning Model
A model for designing balanced talent development: 70% of learning comes from on-the-job experience (stretch assignments, new responsibilities, project leadership), 20% from social learning (coaching, mentoring, peer feedback, shadowing), and 10% from formal training (courses, workshops, e-learning). Originally based on research by Lombardo and Eichinger at the Center for Creative Leadership in the 1990s. The insight: formal training alone rarely transfers to workplace behaviour. The 70% experiential component is where sustainable capability development actually occurs. **Tutorial:** (1) For each competency gap identified in the Capability Framework, design learning interventions across all three channels (not just formal training); (2) For 70%: design specific stretch assignments (one per high-potential employee per year), project rotations, and secondments; (3) For 20%: assign every high-potential a coach and/or mentor, implement 360-degree feedback as a learning tool; (4) For 10%: curate a learning catalogue that is competency-linked (not topic-catalogue); (5) Track completion and self-reported capability improvement across all three channels. **Real example:** Unilever's Future Leaders Programme explicitly structures development using 70:20:10, sending graduates through 2-year rotations across functions (70%), pairing them with senior mentors (20%), and providing modular 'Unilever Management Essentials' classroom training (10%). This programme has become a benchmark for graduate talent development globally. **Pitfall:** Treating formal training as the primary development lever. Research shows that behavioural change requires repeated practice in real situations — classroom training without a follow-on experiential component has less than 15% transfer rate (Baldwin & Ford, 1988).

### GE-McKinsey Nine-Box Portfolio Matrix (HR Application)
A 3×3 prioritisation matrix with Strategic Value (High/Medium/Low) on one axis and Implementation Feasibility (High/Medium/Low) on the other. Originally developed by McKinsey & Company and GE in the 1970s as a portfolio-planning tool for business units. In the HR context, it is applied to prioritise HR initiatives. Strategic Value is calculated using a weighted-criteria model: strategic alignment (40%), NPV (40%), risk reduction (20%). Feasibility scores: capability readiness (40%), resource availability (40%), change complexity (20%). **Tutorial:** (1) List all initiatives on the screened medium-list; (2) For Strategic Value: score each initiative 1–3 on each of the three weighted criteria; compute weighted average; (3) For Feasibility: score each 1–3 on the three feasibility criteria; compute weighted average; (4) Plot all initiatives on the 3×3 grid; (5) Immediately prioritise the top-right quadrant (High Value/High Feasibility); (6) For High Value/Low Feasibility initiatives: schedule capability-building prerequisites before launch; (7) Avoid Low Value/High Feasibility 'activity traps' — these are tempting but strategically wasteful. **Real example:** When IBM's HR transformation team prioritised their 200+ initiatives in 2010, they used a value-feasibility matrix equivalent to the GE-McKinsey grid. The exercise eliminated 40% of initiatives that had high internal enthusiasm but low strategic value — saving an estimated $80M in misallocated implementation investment. **Pitfall:** Allowing politics to override the scoring. Make the criteria and weightings explicit and public before scoring begins. Invisible criteria create invisible politics.

### Power/Interest Grid (Stakeholder Analysis)
A 2×2 matrix mapping stakeholders on Power (ability to influence or block the initiative) vs. Interest (degree of concern about the initiative's outcome). Developed by Aubrey Mendelow at the University of Pittsburgh in 1981 and popularised by Eden and Ackermann. Four quadrants yield four standard strategies: Manage Closely (High/High), Keep Satisfied (High/Low), Keep Informed (Low/High), Monitor (Low/Low). **Tutorial:** (1) List all stakeholders (individuals and groups) affected by or able to affect the initiative; (2) Rate each on Power (1–5 scale, anchored by decision authority and budget control) and Interest (1–5 scale, anchored by how directly they are affected); (3) Plot on the 2×2 grid; (4) Assign an engagement strategy and engagement owner to each quadrant; (5) For 'Manage Closely' stakeholders, conduct individual stakeholder analysis: what do they value, what is their position today, what is the desired position, what is the engagement tactic?; (6) Review the map quarterly — stakeholder positions shift as initiatives progress. **Real example:** During Unilever's Sustainable Living Plan implementation (2010–2020), the stakeholder map identified NGOs and regulators as High Power/High Interest — a counter-intuitive finding for a consumer goods company. By proactively engaging (Manage Closely strategy), Unilever turned potential blockers into programme advocates, reducing regulatory friction significantly. **Pitfall:** Building the stakeholder map once, at project start, and never updating it. People's power and interest shift as projects evolve — a monthly update is minimum.

## A Worked End-to-End Example: Transforming HR at GrowthCo

**Context:** GrowthCo is a 2,000-employee technology services firm. Revenue has grown 45% in two years but regrettable attrition has risen to 22% (vs. 12% industry benchmark), time-to-hire for engineering roles is 78 days (vs. 45-day target), and a succession analysis found 14 of 20 critical leadership roles with no ready successor. The CEO has mandated an HR transformation.

**Phase 1 — Strategy:**
The CHRO conducts 8 executive interviews and maps the three strategic priorities (international expansion, product platform launch, and operational efficiency) to talent implications. An HR Health Dashboard reveals three critical gaps: Recruitment (time-to-hire 78 days, cost-per-hire $24K vs. $18K benchmark), Talent Development (only 23% of high-potentials have IDPs), and Performance Management (ratings are not calibrated — managers' average ratings cluster at 3.9/5.0 regardless of actual performance). The HR Capability Maturity assessment scores an average of 2.1 across pillars (Managed level) against a target of 3.8 (Quantitatively Managed). The HR strategy is co-created with the CEO: north star is 'be an employer of choice for APAC technology talent by 2026, achieving top-quartile time-to-hire, regrettable attrition <10%, and 100% succession coverage for critical roles.'

**Phase 2 — Pillars:**
The Recruitment pillar redesign produces a new EVP ('Build technology that matters. Grow faster than anywhere else. Lead earlier than you expect.'), reduces the interview process from 6 to 4 stages (eliminating a redundant 'HR screen' step), and introduces a structured competency interview guide. The Talent Development pillar introduces the 9-Box Grid: in the first calibration session, leaders identify 47 employees in boxes 7–9 (high performers with high potential) — none of whom had an IDP. The Performance Management pillar moves from a once-a-year appraisal to quarterly OKR reviews with manager-coaching conversations.

**Phase 3 — Initiatives:**
Ideation workshops generate 94 candidate initiatives. After feasibility screening, 52 proceed to business-case development.

**Phase 4 — Business Cases:**
The Structured Interview Programme (total cost: $180K design + $40K training) has a financial model showing: by reducing time-to-hire from 78 to 50 days (conservative estimate based on Mercer benchmark) and improving quality-of-hire score from 3.2 to 3.8/5.0, the NPV over 3 years is $2.1M (primary driver: reduced manager productivity loss from unfilled roles). The 9-Box Talent Development Programme (cost: $250K IDPs + coaching) models a reduction in regrettable attrition from 22% to 16% (conservative vs. 12% target) worth $3.4M NPV (2,000 employees × 22% attrition × $18K cost-to-replace × 6% improvement). Both pass the hurdle rate.

**Phase 5 — Roadmap:**
The GE-McKinsey prioritisation grid identifies 22 high-priority initiatives for the first 18 months, with three in Horizon 1: (1) Structured Interview Programme (highest NPV, highest feasibility), (2) 9-Box Talent Calibration and IDP rollout, (3) OKR Performance Management implementation. A governance PMO is set up with bi-weekly status calls and a Steering Committee chaired by the CEO.

**Phase 6 — Change Management:**
The ADKAR assessment reveals that for the OKR implementation, people managers score high on Awareness (they know OKRs are coming) but low on Desire (they feel existing reviews are 'good enough') and Ability (they have never written OKRs). The change plan for OKR is therefore: (1) Desire intervention: CEO presents at an all-manager town hall, sharing why the current process is failing the business with data; (2) Ability intervention: 4-hour OKR workshop for all 180 managers, with a practice session using real company examples. Twelve months post-launch: regrettable attrition is 18% (down from 22%), time-to-hire is 58 days (down from 78), and 41 of 47 high-potentials have active IDPs.

## Templates

**Template 2: HR Capability Maturity Assessment** — Score each of the 10 pillars on a 1–5 maturity scale. Fill in: Pillar, Maturity Level (1–5), Behavioural Evidence, Gap to Target, Priority Initiatives.

## Pitfalls & Best Practices

**Pitfall 1: HR strategy disconnected from business strategy.** HR that operates in isolation produces programmes that employees and managers regard as irrelevant. Best practice: mandate the CHRO presence at all business strategy discussions. The HR strategy document must explicitly map each HR objective to a named business priority.

**Pitfall 2: The 9-Box Grid without calibration.** Manager ratings are systematically biased — most managers rate 70–80% of their team in the top two boxes. Without a cross-manager calibration session, the 9-Box Grid produces a distorted view. Best practice: hold a 2–3 hour calibration session before any talent investment decisions.

**Pitfall 3: Building the OKR system but not training managers.** OKRs require managers to have coaching conversations — a skill most have never been formally trained in. Best practice: run mandatory 4-hour OKR workshops for all people managers before the first OKR cycle; provide coaching templates.

**Pitfall 4: Change management as a 'last mile' afterthought.** The most common reason HR transformations fail is that change management is treated as a communication exercise at the end of the project rather than a parallel workstream from day one. Best practice: appoint a Change Management Lead at the programme outset; run ADKAR diagnostics before any initiative launches.

**Pitfall 5: Treating all 10 pillars equally.** Organisations rarely have the capacity to overhaul all 10 pillars simultaneously. Best practice: use the HR Capability Maturity Model to identify the 3–4 pillars with the largest gaps relative to strategic priority, and focus investment there first.

**Pitfall 6: Financial models with no sensitivity analysis.** HR benefits (attrition reduction, productivity gain) are inherently uncertain. A single-point estimate that misses will erode credibility. Best practice: always include base/upside/downside scenarios and make the key assumptions transparent.

**Pitfall 7: Employer branding without an authentic Employee Value Proposition.** Many organisations invest in employer branding campaigns that attract candidates whose expectations are not met by the actual employee experience — resulting in fast regrettable attrition. Best practice: build the EVP from stay-surveys and manager interviews first; verify it is authentic before amplifying it.

# Set customer expectations at the level you can reliably exceed — not at the maximum you can promise

> **Source.** [Chapter 1 — Marketing: Creating Customer Value and Engagement](../chapters/chapter-01-marketing-creating-customer-value.md), book pp. 26-30, chunk 3/7 (confidence 0.9, auto-accepted).
>
> "Marketers must be careful to set the right level of expectations. If they set expectations too low, they may satisfy those who buy but fail to attract enough buyers. If they set expectations too high, buyers will be disappointed. Customer value and customer satisfaction are key building blocks for developing and managing customer relationships."
>
> — Kotler/Armstrong/Opresnik, *Principles of Marketing* (18e Global, Pearson 2021)

**The recommendation.** The chapter states this principle precisely in the customer value and satisfaction section (p. 29) and reinforces it in the CRM section (p. 36): "Marketers must be careful to set the right level of expectations. If they set expectations too low, they may satisfy those who buy but fail to attract enough buyers. If they set expectations too high, buyers will be disappointed."

**The delight standard.** Outstanding marketing companies "go out of their way to keep important customers satisfied" and "delight customers by promising only what they can deliver and then delivering more than they promise." Delighted customers do not merely repurchase — they become brand advocates and "customer evangelists."

**The economic grounding.** Higher levels of customer satisfaction lead to greater customer loyalty, which results in better company performance. But the chapter also corrects the instinct to maximize satisfaction without constraint: "A company can always increase customer satisfaction by lowering its prices or increasing its services. But this may result in lower profits. Thus, the purpose of marketing is to generate customer value profitably."

**Practical illustration.** ALDI (no-frills grocery) achieves highly satisfied customers even though customers must bag their own groceries. Satisfaction comes from reliably delivering on the core value proposition — very low prices on good-quality products — not from service heroics.

**For Ritsu.** The landing page promise — "In 30 seconds, get an AI tutor" — creates a specific expectation. If the product reliably delivers a working quiz and flashcard set in 30 seconds but users expected *personalized mastery in 30 seconds*, that gap creates dissatisfaction. Operationally, this means Ritsu's messaging should promise the specific moment of value ("you get a quiz on your exact material in 30 seconds") rather than the ultimate outcome ("you master the subject"), because the ultimate outcome requires user effort the product alone cannot guarantee.

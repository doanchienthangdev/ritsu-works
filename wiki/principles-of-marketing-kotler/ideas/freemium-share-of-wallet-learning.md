# Ritsu's freemium model as a share-of-customer problem: capturing more of the student's learning wallet

> **Source.** [Chapter 1 — Marketing: Creating Customer Value and Engagement](../chapters/chapter-01-marketing-creating-customer-value.md), book pp. 34-42, chunk 5/7 (confidence 0.65, pending founder review).
>
> "To increase share of customer, firms can offer greater variety to current customers. Or they can create programs to cross-sell and up-sell to market more products and services to existing customers."
>
> — Kotler/Armstrong/Opresnik, *Principles of Marketing* (18e Global, Pearson 2021)

**The chapter's share-of-customer concept.** Share of customer is the portion of a customer's purchasing *in their product category* that a company captures. Banks want "share of wallet"; supermarkets want "share of stomach." For Ritsu, the relevant share metric is: share of a student's *study time* and *learning tool budget*.

**The current Ritsu situation.** A free Ritsu user who uploads one PDF and gets one quiz has given Ritsu a tiny share of their study time. A paying user who uses Ritsu for every course across a semester has given Ritsu a dominant share. The freemium model is, in the chapter's framing, an entry strategy to acquire initial share of the student's learning workflow — with the conversion event being the moment when Ritsu's value is so embedded in the workflow that paying is obvious.

**The generative question.** The chapter's Amazon example is instructive: "Based on each customer's purchase and search history, the company recommends related products that might be of interest. This recommendation system influences some 35 percent of all sales." What is Ritsu's equivalent of the Amazon recommendation system? After a student masters Chapter 1 of their biology textbook, what should Ritsu naturally suggest — "Ready for Chapter 2?" "Try a crossword on this material?" "Share your score with your study group?" — that increases share of their learning session and share of their academic semester?

**The freemium mechanic implication.** If free users can demonstrate real learning gains within their free quota, the upgrade decision becomes a share-of-learning-wallet argument: "You mastered 3 chapters free. Your exam covers 12. Upgrade to master all 12." The chapter's logic suggests this is more powerful than feature-gating — it ties upgrade to the student's own goals rather than to product feature access.

**Speculative connection** (marked as speculative). The societal marketing concept in the chapter suggests that Ritsu's social mission (democratizing effective learning) could itself be a retention and advocacy driver — students who feel Ritsu is on their side, not just extracting subscription fees, may become stronger advocates. This is the Warby Parker "Buy a Pair, Give a Pair" dynamic applied to EdTech.

# Is Ritsu's active-learning positioning an expression of the marketing concept or the product concept?

> **Source.** [Chapter 1 — Marketing: Creating Customer Value and Engagement](../chapters/chapter-01-marketing-creating-customer-value.md), book pp. 30-34, chunk 4/7 (confidence 0.65, pending founder review).
>
> "In many cases, however, customers don't know what they want or even what is possible. As Henry Ford supposedly remarked, 'If I'd asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.' Such situations call for customer-driving marketing—understanding customer needs even better than customers themselves do and creating products and services that meet both existing and latent needs, now and in the future."
>
> — Kotler/Armstrong/Opresnik, *Principles of Marketing* (18e Global, Pearson 2021)

**The generative tension.** Chapter 1 contrasts two orientations:
- **Product concept**: consumers favor products that offer the most quality, performance, and innovative features. Risk: "build a better mousetrap" thinking — optimizing the artifact without testing whether it solves the real problem.
- **Marketing concept**: start from customer needs, integrate all activity to serve them, measure success by customer satisfaction and loyalty.

Ritsu's positioning — *active learning beats passive learning* — is a strong needs-level insight. It names the actual mechanism of learning (effortful retrieval, practice, explanation) rather than the artifact (flashcard, quiz). This is structurally more aligned with the marketing concept than the product concept.

**But the diagnostic question.** Ritsu has 17+ activity types. The chapter's marketing concept demands: *which* activity types were designed because users need them, and which were designed because they were technically elegant or competitively complete? The marketing concept would suggest running the following test: which 3 activity types produce the highest reported learning satisfaction and the highest retention-to-next-session rate? Those are the ones to deepen first.

**The customer-driving variant.** The chapter also notes that Henry Ford's customers would have asked for "faster horses" — not automobiles. Ritsu may have proprietary insight into what learning *should* look like (adaptive difficulty, Feynman explanation, upthink) that users cannot yet articulate. That is the "customer-driving" variant of the marketing concept: reading needs that are not yet on the page. The question is whether Ritsu's current feature roadmap is informed by this principled foresight or by mimicking existing edtech conventions.

**Speculative connection.** If Ritsu can demonstrate empirically which activity type produces the most "I actually understood it" moments (the core emotional promise), that becomes both the product roadmap priority and the marketing claim to lead with. The marketing concept and the product roadmap become the same document.

# Customer-perceived value and customer satisfaction

> **Source.** [Chapter 1 — Marketing: Creating Customer Value and Engagement](../chapters/chapter-01-marketing-creating-customer-value.md), book pp. 34-42, chunk 5/7 (confidence 0.95, auto-accepted).
>
> "Customer-perceived value [is] the customer's evaluation of the difference between all the benefits and all the costs of a market offering relative to those of competing offers. Importantly, customers often do not judge values and costs 'accurately' or 'objectively.' They act on perceived value."
>
> — Kotler/Armstrong/Opresnik, *Principles of Marketing* (18e Global, Pearson 2021)

**What it is.** Two closely linked concepts govern the relationship-building phase of the marketing process.

**Customer-perceived value**: the customer's evaluation of the difference between all the benefits and all the costs of a market offering *relative to those of competing offers*. Critically, customers do not judge values and costs "accurately" or "objectively" — they act on *perceived* value. A Patek Philippe watch costing $20,000–$500,000 is perceived as a bargain by its buyers because the emotional, sentimental, and investment value exceeds the price paid.

**Customer satisfaction**: the extent to which a product's perceived performance matches a buyer's expectations.
- Performance < expectations → dissatisfied customer.
- Performance = expectations → satisfied customer.
- Performance > expectations → highly satisfied or *delighted* customer.

**The delight threshold matters.** Outstanding companies go out of their way to delight customers — "promising only what they can deliver and then delivering more than they promise." Delighted customers become willing brand advocates and "customer evangelists." However, the chapter also warns against maximizing satisfaction at the cost of profitability: "The marketer must continue to generate more customer value and satisfaction but not 'give away the house.'" Customer satisfaction comes not just from service heroics but from how well a company delivers on its basic value proposition.

**For Ritsu.** The expectation-setting problem is real for AI tutors: if the landing page promises mastery in 30 seconds, users who don't feel mastery after their first session are *dissatisfied* even if the product worked technically. Calibrating the promise (active learning vs. passive reading) to what the product actually delivers in session 1 is a direct application of this concept. The delight target for Ritsu is likely: user uploads a chapter, gets quizzed, scores higher on a re-test, and *feels* the difference — that's the moment that produces advocacy.

# Cadillac's 51% market share masked eroding customer equity while BMW built it

> **Source.** [Chapter 1 — Marketing: Creating Customer Value and Engagement](../chapters/chapter-01-marketing-creating-customer-value.md), book pp. 34-42, chunk 5/7 (confidence 0.95, auto-accepted).
>
> "Cadillac's share of the luxury car market reached a whopping 51 percent in 1976, and based on market share and sales, the brand's future looked rosy. However, measures of customer equity would have painted a bleaker picture. Cadillac customers were getting older (average age 60), and average customer lifetime value was falling."
>
> — Kotler/Armstrong/Opresnik, *Principles of Marketing* (18e Global, Pearson 2021)

**What happened.** In the 1970s and 1980s, Cadillac had some of the most loyal customers in the industry. Its share of the luxury car market reached 51% in 1976 — by market share metrics, the brand looked dominant. However, measures of customer equity told a different story:
- Cadillac customers averaged age 60.
- Average customer lifetime value was falling.
- Many buyers were on their last car.

**The contrast with BMW.** BMW's younger, more vigorous image initially yielded lower market share. But it produced:
- A younger customer base (average age ~40).
- Higher customer lifetime values.
- The result: BMW overtook Cadillac in the 1980s.

**The explicit moral stated in the text.** "Marketers should care not just about current sales and market share. Customer lifetime value and customer equity are the name of the game."

**Why this is an important empirical case.** The Cadillac case is the clearest demonstration in the chapter that market share is a lagging indicator — it reflects past customer acquisition, not future value. Customer equity is a *leading* indicator: it reveals what the customer base will yield over time.

**Relevance.** For any company tracking engagement metrics — including EdTech products — the Cadillac case warns against optimizing for current active users over the quality and tenure potential of those users. A cohort of first-year university students using Ritsu has fundamentally different CLV than adult professionals dipping in once.

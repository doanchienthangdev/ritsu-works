# Price Discrimination: Third-Degree Strategies for Revenue Optimization

**Type:** concept · **From:** *Marketing Management* (Kotler/Keller/Chernev 16e SE), Ch 11 — Managing Pricing and Sales Promotions (pp. 251–252)

## Summary

Price discrimination occurs when a company sells at two or more prices not reflecting proportional cost differences. Third-degree discrimination (the most strategically relevant) charges different amounts to different consumer segments: customer-segment pricing (students vs. professionals), product-form pricing (different versions at different prices not proportional to costs), channel pricing (fine restaurant vs. fast food vs. vending machine), location pricing (theater seat pricing by preference), and time pricing (early-bird vs. peak). Conditions for price discrimination to work: segmentable market with differing demand intensities, lower-price segments cannot resell to higher-price segments, competitors cannot undersell in the higher-price segment, and segmentation costs must be exceeded by extra revenue.

## Raw quotes (verbatim, for citation)

> Price discrimination occurs when a company sells a product or service at two or more prices that do not reflect a proportional difference in costs.
>
> In third-degree price discrimination, the seller charges different amounts to different consumer segments.
>
> For price discrimination to work, certain conditions must exist. First, the market must be segmentable, and the segments must show different intensities of demand. Second, members in the lower-price segment must not be able to resell the product to the higher-price segment.
>

## Ritsu relevance

Ritsu's freemium is first-degree adjacent (free users vs. paying users). The next level: student pricing (lower), professional pricing (higher), educator/institutional pricing (highest). Segments have different demand intensities — students are price-sensitive but high-volume; professionals have lower price sensitivity and smaller volume. Key risk: students cannot resell their plan access to professionals — this structural barrier is built into account-level access controls.

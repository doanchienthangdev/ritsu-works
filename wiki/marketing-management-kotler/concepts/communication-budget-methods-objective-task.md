# Communication Budget Methods: Objective-and-Task vs. Flawed Alternatives

**Type:** concept · **From:** *Marketing Management* (Kotler/Keller/Chernev 16e SE), Ch 12 — Managing Marketing Communications (pp. 349–415)

## Summary

Four budgeting methods exist: (1) Objective-and-task — define objectives, identify tasks, sum costs — the most defensible; (2) Competitive-parity — matching competitors, flawed because there is no evidence competitors know optimal spend; (3) Affordable — availability-of-funds, ignores investment nature of communications; (4) Percentage-of-sales — practical but disconnected from communication tasks. Budget factors: PLC stage, differentiation, market share, message complexity, reach difficulty, competitive clutter, available resources.

## Raw quotes (verbatim, for citation)

> A practical approach for defining the communication budget is objective-and-task budgeting. This approach calls for marketers to develop a communication budget by defining specific objectives, identifying the tasks that must be performed to achieve these objectives, and estimating their costs.
>
> The overarching principle is that the total communication budget must be set so the marginal profit from every communication dollar is greater than or equal to the marginal profit from every dollar spent on other marketing activities.
>
> Setting their communication budgets at parity with competitors is problematic because there are no grounds for believing competitors are more knowledgeable about what the optimal communication budget should be.
>
> Although marketing communication is treated as a current expense, part of it can be considered an investment in building brand equity and customer loyalty.
>

## Ritsu relevance

Ritsu is bootstrapped; the affordable method is the trap to avoid. Objective-and-task applies: if the objective is 15% aided awareness among college students in 90 days, work backward from cost of content + distribution to reach them.

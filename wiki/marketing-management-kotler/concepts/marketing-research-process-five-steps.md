# Five-Step Marketing Research Process — American Airlines Wi-Fi Case

**Type:** concept · **From:** *Marketing Management* (Kotler/Keller/Chernev 16e SE), Ch 5 — Conducting Marketing Research (pp. 102–103)

## Summary

MM 16e's canonical five-step research process: Define the Problem, Develop the Research Plan, Collect the Information, Analyze the Information, Make the Decision. The critical discipline is defining the problem at the right scope - not too broad ('find out everything') nor too narrow ('will $25 break even in one year'). The right problem definition exposes the real business question: 'Will offering high-speed Wi-Fi create enough incremental preference and profit to justify its cost against other service enhancements?' The cost of research must be weighed against expected decision value improvement. Primary data approaches: observation, focus groups, surveys, behavioral data, experiments.

## Raw quotes (verbatim, for citation)

> A marketing manager who says, 'Find out everything you can about first-class air travelers' needs' will collect a lot of unnecessary information. One who says, 'Find out whether enough passengers aboard a B777 flying direct between Chicago and Tokyo would pay $25 for ultra-high-speed Wi-Fi service so we can break even in one year' is taking too narrow a view of the problem.
>
> If the manager believes the marketing research will lead to an improved pricing and promotional plan and a long-term profit of $90,000, he should be willing to spend up to $40,000 on this research. If the research will cost more than $40,000, it's not worth doing.
>

## Ritsu relevance

Ritsu's research budget ($0-500 early stage) should follow this cost-benefit logic: only commission research whose expected decision-quality improvement exceeds the cost. Early activation research (why do users not return after session 1?) is the highest-value question.

# Greenwashing Risk and the Conditions for Authentic Sustainable Marketing

**Type:** concept · **From:** *Marketing Management* (Kotler/Keller/Chernev 16e SE), Ch 21 — Socially Responsible Marketing (pp. 475–477)

## Summary

Greenwashing — claiming environmental benefits while concealing significant drawbacks — triggers consumer skepticism and regulatory risk. Authentic sustainability marketing requires full-lifecycle accountability (not just one eco-friendly feature), product performance parity with non-green alternatives, and willingness to acknowledge trade-offs publicly. Companies like Patagonia (Don't Buy This Jacket campaign) and Stonyfield Farm have turned radical transparency into brand equity.

## Raw quotes (verbatim, for citation)

> Heightened interest in sustainability has also, regrettably, resulted in greenwashing—that is, providing misleading information or giving a false impression that products or practices are environmentally friendly without living up to that promise. One study revealed that half the labels on allegedly green products focus on an eco-friendly benefit (such as recycled content), while omitting information about significant environmental drawbacks (such as manufacturing intensity or transportation costs).
>
> Many consumers are unwilling to sacrifice product performance and quality and are not necessarily willing to pay a price premium for genuinely green products.
>
> Patagonia even ran a full-page ad in the New York Times headlined 'Don't Buy This Jacket.' Below a photo of the retailer's R2 jacket was text explaining that despite its many positive features—'60% recyclable polyester, knit and sewn to high standards, and exceptionally durable'—the jacket still imposed many environmental costs.
>

# Consumer Price Psychology: Reference Prices, Image Pricing, Pricing Cues

**Type:** concept · **From:** *Marketing Management* (Kotler/Keller/Chernev 16e SE), Ch 11 — Managing Pricing and Sales Promotions (pp. 241–243)

## Summary

Consumers do not accept prices at face value — they interpret them using mental frameworks. Reference prices are internal benchmarks (last price paid, fair price, typical price, competitor price, expected future price) or external frames (posted regular retail price). Unpleasant surprises (stated price higher than expected) have greater impact on purchase likelihood than pleasant surprises. Image pricing uses price as a quality signal — especially powerful for ego-sensitive products like perfumes, luxury goods, and designer items. Pricing cues: prices ending in 9 suggest discount/bargain; ending in 0 or 5 are easier to recall; 'sale' signs boost demand but must not be overused. Left-to-right price processing means $299 is perceived as $200-range, not $300-range.

## Raw quotes (verbatim, for citation)

> Customers may have a lower price threshold, below which prices signal inferior or unacceptable quality, and an upper price threshold, above which prices are prohibitive.
>
> Possible reference prices include 'fair price' (what consumers feel the product should cost), typical price, last price paid, upper-bound price, lower-bound price, competitor price, expected future price, and usual discounted price.
>
> Many consumers use price as an indicator of quality. Image pricing is especially effective with ego-sensitive products such as perfumes, expensive cars, and designer clothing.
>
> Clever marketers try to frame the price to signal the best value possible. For example, a relatively expensive item can look less expensive if the price is broken into smaller units, such as a $600 annual membership for '$50 a month.'
>

## Ritsu relevance

Ritsu pricing presentation should break annual plans into monthly equivalents (e.g., '$8/month billed annually' not '$96/year'). Competitor reference price anchoring: show the price of a private tutor ($50-150/hour) before revealing Ritsu's price, making Ritsu look dramatically cheaper by contrast. Free plan acts as the lower-bound reference — the paid plan must be priced above the point where users perceive it as 'low quality because it's cheap.'

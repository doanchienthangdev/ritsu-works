# Ritsu Upgrade Pitch Should Be Framed as an EVC Comparison, Not a Feature List

**Type:** idea · **From:** *Marketing Management* (Kotler/Keller/Chernev 16e SE), Ch 11 — Managing Pricing and Sales Promotions (pp. 248–250)

## Summary

Applying EVC pricing principles to Ritsu's free-to-paid conversion: the upgrade page should not list features but instead present a value stack comparing Ritsu paid against the realistic alternatives a student uses today. The EVC calculation: (Anki manual flashcard time saved) + (Quizlet subscription replaced) + (ChatGPT inadequacy cost) + (tutor session cost) - (Ritsu paid price) = surplus value. A student who creates Anki decks manually for 3 hours per week at a notional $15/hour opportunity cost saves $45/week = $180/month in time savings alone vs. Ritsu's likely $10-15/month paid plan. This surplus makes the price objection disappear and shifts the conversation from 'is $12 worth it' to 'how much time and money am I losing by staying on the free plan.'

## Raw quotes (verbatim, for citation)

> Companies must deliver the value promised by their value proposition, and the customer must perceive this value. Firms use the other marketing program elements, such as advertising, the sales force, and the internet, to communicate and enhance perceived value in buyers' minds.
>
> The key to economic-value-to-customer pricing is to deliver more unique value than competitors and to demonstrate this to prospective buyers.
>
> Ensuring that customers appreciate the total value of a product or service offering is crucial.
>

## Ritsu relevance

Direct actionable output: the 01-marketing/ team should build a 'Cost of Not Upgrading' calculator or comparison table on the Ritsu pricing page. Map each Ritsu paid feature to a real cost saved (hours, dollars, alternative tool subscription). This is a first-principles justification for the paid price point — before deciding on $9.99 vs. $14.99, first calculate the EVC surplus at each price point for each ICP persona.

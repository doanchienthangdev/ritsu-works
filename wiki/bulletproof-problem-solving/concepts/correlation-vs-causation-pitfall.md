# Correlation vs. Causation Pitfall

**Type:** concept · **From:** *Bulletproof Problem Solving* (Charles Conn, Robert McLean, 2018), Ch 6 — Big Guns of Analysis

## Summary

The chapter repeatedly flags that statistical correlation — even strong correlation — does not establish causation. Examples given include walkable cities and obesity (reverse causation plausible), PM 2.5 hotspots and asthma admissions (potential common underlying driver), and Nobel Prize winners versus national chocolate consumption (wealth as a confounder). Experiments and natural experiments are presented as the primary remedies.

## Raw quotes (verbatim, for citation)

- **From [Ch 6 — Big Guns of Analysis](../chapters/chapter-06-big-guns-of-analysis.md)** (PDF pp. 165–208, confidence 0.95):

  > Be careful with correlation and causation. Walkable cities seem to almost always have far lower obesity rates than less walkable cities. However, we have no way of knowing from statistics alone whether city walkability is the true cause of lower obesity.

  > as we know, correlations do not prove causation; there could be an underlying factor causing both PM 2.5 hotspots and asthma hospital admissions.

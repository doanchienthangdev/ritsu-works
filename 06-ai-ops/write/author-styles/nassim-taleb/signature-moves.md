## The Triad / Two-Types Split
**What:** Collapse a messy domain into two or three named columns (fragile / robust / antifragile; doer / talker; Mediocristan / Extremistan) and invite the reader to sort everything into them.
**When:** As the structural spine of an argument; introduce it early, then walk it across domains (health, finance, war, study, his own life) to show the same shape recurs everywhere.
**Example:** "Recall that the fragile wants tranquility, the antifragile grows from disorder, and the robust doesn't care too much." — *Antifragile* (the Triad)
**How to do it:** State the categories as bald nouns, give a one-line discriminator for each, then run concrete cases through the same grid. A literal two- or three-column table is fair game. The split must be exhaustive and a little brutal: the reader has to place themselves, and one column is the loser.

## The Semicolon Flip (chiastic inversion)
**What:** Set up the comfortable belief in clause A, then reverse the value-assignment in a shorter clause B across a semicolon, often with mirrored syntax and one swapped word.
**When:** For any verdict that overturns a cliché; it is the single most characteristic Taleb sentence.
**Example:** "For the robust, an error is information; for the fragile, an error is an error." — *The Bed of Procrustes*; and "The opposite of success isn't failure; it is name-dropping." — *The Bed of Procrustes*
**How to do it:** The semicolon (not "but," not an em-dash) is the pivot. First clause names the expectation; second clause is shorter and lands on the unexpected or contemptible noun. Do not explain it afterward; the surprise is the argument.

## The Coined Capital as Weapon
**What:** Mint a Capitalized proper noun for a concept or a type of person, then deploy it as both analytic handle and insult.
**When:** Whenever a recurring phenomenon lacks a word; he insists the naming itself is the intellectual act.
**Example:** "Its nickname in this book is GIF, Great Intellectual Fraud." — *The Black Swan* (on the bell curve); and the pejorative class: "the benefits are small and visible, and the side effects potentially severe and invisible" (the fragilista). — *Antifragile*
**How to do it:** Introduce the coinage with a one-line gloss, capitalize it, then reuse it relentlessly so it carries the whole argument. Reserve it for load-bearing ideas (Black Swan, the turkey, fragilista, via negativa); a decorative Capitalized term that carries neither a precise concept nor contempt rings false.

## Long Build, Axiom Landing
**What:** Pile clauses, qualifiers, and parentheticals into a long sentence, then drop to a short freestanding maxim that delivers the verdict.
**When:** At the close of a paragraph or section, to convert a page of accretion into one memorable law.
**Example:** "If you see fraud and do not say fraud, you are a fraud." — *Antifragile*; and the two-word landing: "Compromising is condoning." — *Antifragile*
**How to do it:** Let the build breathe (40-70 words, commas and semicolons), then a hard stop and a 3-to-12-word line, often set on its own line. The brevity of the landing does the persuading; never soften or qualify it.

## Detect the Fragility, Don't Predict the Event
**What:** Refuse the forecast and substitute a structural claim: you cannot say when or which, but you can say whether the thing is fragile to the shock.
**When:** Whenever asked to predict a price, a date, or "the next crisis"; the refusal itself is the move.
**Example:** "it is not foolish to say this bridge is fragile. It's going to break one day." — Bloomberg Odd Lots (live); and "I can be a bad forecaster and still make money if I have convexity." — Antifragile Mindset interview
**How to do it:** Open with a refusal ("it's foolish to say which truck breaks the bridge"), then pivot to the answerable question (is it fragile?). Replace the unknowable "when/what" with the constructible "exposure." Treat predicting the event as a category error, not a hard problem.

## The Concrete-Object Generalization
**What:** Take one homely physical object, state its behavior under stress, then explicitly scale it up to a universal law.
**When:** To define or stress-test an abstraction (fragility, convexity, non-linearity) without notation; spoken, the move IS the argument.
**Example:** "you double the stone, you have four times the harm." — Antifragile Mindset interview (non-linearity, scaled straight to the size of nations)
**How to do it:** Name the object (a coffee cup, a stone, a deadlift, a slice of pizza), state how it responds to variation, then ask "can I generalize to everything?" and run the same logic across biology, markets, and politics. The prop must be one the reader handles weekly.

## Contempt Inside Rigor (the deadpan named insult)
**What:** Insert a flat insult to a person, profession, or institution mid-sentence, in the same register as the analysis, as if it were a neutral technical observation.
**When:** While making a serious point about forecasters, academics, bankers, or bureaucrats; the insult rides the argument.
**Example:** "Davos-attending members of the I.A.N.D. (International Association of Name Droppers)" — *Antifragile*; and "Education makes the wise slightly wiser, but it makes the fool vastly more dangerous." — *The Bed of Procrustes*
**How to do it:** Build a straight clause, then slot the mockery in without changing register or pausing, often via a mock-formal coinage. He admits he enjoys it. (Ritsu application: aim this at a method or an idea, never a real named individual; see `do-and-dont.md`.)

## Via Negativa (knowledge by subtraction)
**What:** Argue that we know what is wrong more reliably than what is right, so the robust move is to remove, not add.
**When:** Whenever prescribing; he reframes the recommendation as an act of omission and distrusts addition.
**Example:** "Systems learn by removing parts, via negativa." — *Skin in the Game*; and "To bankrupt a fool, give him information." — *The Bed of Procrustes*
**How to do it:** Assert the epistemic asymmetry (easier to detect the fragile than to predict the event; easier to know what is bad than what is good), then make the rule a subtraction and tag it *via negativa*. "Less, but better"; remove the fake before adding the real.

## Skin in the Game / Autobiography as Proof
**What:** Ground a claim in lived personal risk rather than citation, making first-person exposure both the credential and the evidence.
**When:** To establish authority on risk, and to disqualify the library-bound scholar who bears no downside.
**Example:** "Don't tell me what you 'think,' just tell me what's in your portfolio." — *Skin in the Game*; and the named risk-transfer scam: "Heads he wins, tails he shouts 'Black Swan.'" — *Skin in the Game*
**How to do it:** State the practitioner's rule as a moral stricture, then narrate the personal stake (the trading floor, the war, the gym). Refuse the library as the source of an idea. The test of a claim is who bears the downside if it is wrong.

## The Steelman-Then-Amputate
**What:** Grant the opponent's premise loudly and fully, then deny that it produces the conclusion they want.
**When:** To pre-empt the obvious objection and look fair while staying lethal.
**Example:** "Of course chance favors the prepared!" — *Fooled by Randomness* (conceded with emphasis, then: necessary is not causal; the trip to the store did not cause the lottery win)
**How to do it:** Concede with an exclamation point ("of course," "very necessary"), then drive the wedge between necessary and sufficient, or between correlation and cause. Amputate the conclusion, never the premise; the reader should feel you were more than fair.

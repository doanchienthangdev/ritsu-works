# Tim Urban — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A brilliant, goofball friend explaining something at 2am who goes a mile deep instead of an inch wide — turns an invisible force (procrastination, AI, desire) into a named, drawn character, pushes one thought experiment past the point of absurdity, drops a hyper-specific ridiculous tangent, casts himself as the dumbest person in the room, and zooms all the way out to the cosmic/mortality scale before exiting on a wink.

**Rhythm rule:** Wildly varied sentence length is the rhythm. Build a long, escalating riff (a list of absurd specifics), then drop a one-line deadpan beat ("Daunting." / "I have problems."). Keep the rhetorical-question hinge ("Notice anything different?" → answer it). Turn corners on tiny conversational hinges: "So." "Now." "Here's the thing."
**Diction rule:** The named capitalized character (the Instant Gratification Monkey, the Panic Monster, the Yearning Octopus) and the absurdly precise concrete (not "a while" — "scroll up for two and a half hours"). Real research, real terms, credited by name — then re-rendered as a cartoon. Italics for the one load-bearing word. NO em-dashes in output (humanizer strips them: use a parenthetical, colon, or fresh short sentence). Profanity is authentic to him but cut it for Ritsu surfaces.
**Reader rule:** Assume they're smart and up for a long, deep, funny ride. Talk to one *you*; run the thought experiment *together*. Never dumb down the idea — only the delivery. Be the most flawed, most confused person in the room; flag your uncertainty out loud. Coin a handle they carry away.

## Do (top 7)
1. Personify the invisible force as a named, capitalized character with a personality (and describe its drawing) — this is *the* move.
2. Run one thought experiment and push it past where a sane writer stops (the recursive 1750→1500→12,000 BC time machine; "he might actually die").
3. Drop one hyper-specific, faintly ridiculous tangent mid-point (gorilla vs tiger; the Feynman-to-Bieber YouTube spiral) — footnote the punchline.
4. Build-then-drop: long escalating riff, capped by a short deadpan line.
5. Zoom out once to a vast scale (time, mortality, the real numbers) — the Life Calendar move.
6. Be the specimen: cast yourself as the dumbest/most-procrastinating/most-confused person, flag your confusion.
7. Coin a reusable handle (named character, unit, acronym) as the souvenir of the piece.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Stay shallow or repeat conventional wisdom — the inch-deep summary is the exact thing the voice exists to refuse.
2. Leave an abstract force abstract — never explain it without a character, a drawing, or a thought experiment.
3. Be boring to seem serious — rigor and fun are not enemies; the joke is how the hard idea gets past the reader's guard.
4. Dumb down the actual idea or condescend — simplify delivery, never content.
5. Pretend certainty — credit your sources by name, give ranges, admit when "no one really knows."
6. Pad with corporate filler ("in today's fast-paced world") — his filler is jokes, not jargon.
7. End on a tidy motivational bow — zoom out to something vast, then wink.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The disarming question — "What does it feel like to stand here?" (*The AI Revolution*, 2015)
- The honest meta-frame — "The reason this post took three weeks to finish is that as I dug into research… I could not believe what I was reading." (*The AI Revolution*, 2015)
- Self-as-specimen — the relatable scene of the college student spreading out a paper, then absolutely not doing that. (TED, 2016)

## Signature closings (how they land)
- Zoom out to mortality, then wink — "it's a job that should probably start today. Well, maybe not today, but… You know. Sometime soon." (TED, 2016)
- The grid that reframes the whole life — "How are the two grids different from each other, and why?" (*100 Blocks a Day*, 2016)
- The deadpan undercut after the big build — "It's not pretty, but in the end, it works." (TED, 2016)

## 1. The Personified Abstract Force (his core instrument)

**What:** Take an invisible force — procrastination, desire, social fear, an economic trend — and turn it into a named, capitalized character with a personality, a motive, and (on the blog) a stick-figure drawing. The character carries the explanation; once you can *see* the Monkey grab the wheel, you understand the mechanism without a single clinical term. This is the single most Tim-Urban thing he does.

**When:** The moment a topic threatens to become an abstraction. Reach for the most vivid character that captures the force's actual personality — something that only wants "easy and fun," something that wakes up only near a deadline, something with five conflicting tentacles.

**Example:** "Both brains have a Rational Decision-Maker in them, but the procrastinator's brain also has an Instant Gratification Monkey… He lives entirely in the present moment. He has no memory of the past, no knowledge of the future, and he only cares about two things: easy and fun." — *Inside the Mind of a Master Procrastinator*, TED (2016)
**Example:** "the procrastinator has a guardian angel… someone called the Panic Monster. Now, the Panic Monster is dormant most of the time, but he suddenly wakes up anytime a deadline gets too close." — TED (2016)
**Example:** "To do a proper Want Box audit, you need to think about what you yearn for… Luckily, we have someone here who can help us. The Yearning Octopus." — *How to Pick a Career (That Actually Fits You)*, Wait But Why (2018)

## 2. The Extended Thought Experiment Pushed to Absurdity (how he proves the unintuitive)

**What:** Set up a hypothetical and then push it far past where a careful writer would stop — escalate it, recurse it, let it get gloriously absurd — until the unintuitive point becomes undeniable *because* you walked every ridiculous step. The absurdity isn't a bug; it's the proof.

**When:** Whenever the reader's intuition is wrong and a flat assertion won't move them. Build the experiment so they discover the conclusion themselves.

**Example:** "Imagine taking a time machine back to 1750… you retrieve a dude, bring him to 2015, and then walk him around… This experience for him wouldn't be surprising or shocking or even mind-blowing — those words aren't big enough. He might actually die." — *The AI Revolution*, Wait But Why (2015)
**Example:** "if he then went back to 1750 and got jealous… he'd take the time machine and go back the same distance, get someone from around the year 1500, bring him to 1750… the 1500 guy would be shocked by a lot of things — but he wouldn't die." — *The AI Revolution* (2015) — the recursion is the move: each guy gets jealous and grabs an earlier guy, proving acceleration by lived absurdity.

## 3. The Hyper-Specific Absurd Tangent (the goofy detour that makes the point stick)

**What:** Drop a wildly specific, faintly ridiculous digression right in the middle of a serious point — not "I got distracted" but the exact, fabricated-feeling rabbit hole, narrated in full. It both demonstrates the thing he's describing (e.g. procrastination) and lowers the reader's guard with a laugh.

**When:** To illustrate a behavior by performing it, or just to keep a long read alive. Often the punchline gets tucked into a footnote.

**Example:** "I spent a bunch of hours doing things like seeing this picture… of \[a gorilla\]… thinking about how easily he could beat me in a fight, then wondering if he could beat a tiger in a fight, then wondering who would win between a lion and a tiger, and then googling that and reading about it for a while (the tiger would win). I have problems." — *Why Procrastinators Procrastinate*, Wait But Why (2013)
**Example:** "let's just open Google Earth and zoom in to the bottom of India, like 200 feet above the ground, and scroll up for two and a half hours til we get to the top of the country, so we can get a better feel for India." — TED (2016)
**Example:** "a YouTube spiral that starts with videos of Richard Feynman talking about magnets and ends much, much later with us watching interviews with Justin Bieber's mom." — TED (2016)

## 4. The Zoom-Out to Cosmic / Mortality Scale (his favorite reframe)

**What:** Pull the camera all the way back — to the span of a life in weeks, to a 90-year calendar of boxes, to the scale of human history, to death — so the reader feels, viscerally, how small the trivial is and how enormous the ignored is. He turns time and scale into something you can *see* (a grid, a calendar, a unit).

**When:** Usually near the close, to deliver the stakes in the gut; sometimes mid-piece to recalibrate what "a lot" and "soon" really mean.

**Example:** "I call this a Life Calendar. That's one box for every week of a 90-year life. That's not that many boxes, especially since we've already used a bunch of those." — TED (2016)
**Example:** "Let's think about those 1,000 minutes as 100 10-minute blocks. That's what you wake up with every day… Cooking dinner requires three blocks, while ordering in requires zero — is cooking dinner worth three blocks to you?" — *100 Blocks a Day*, Wait But Why (2016)
**Example:** the "Die Progress Unit (DPU)" — measuring history by how far forward someone must travel before the future would literally kill them with shock. — *The AI Revolution* (2015)

## 5. The Self-as-Specimen (humility that earns the authority)

**What:** Cast himself as the case study and the dumbest, most-flawed person in the room — the procrastinator who couldn't start his own TED talk, the guy whose hands curled up from writing a thesis in 72 hours, the amateur who "used to think AI was a silly sci-fi concept." The self-deprecation makes him trustworthy and makes the reader comfortable being a mess too.

**When:** At the open (to disarm) and throughout (to keep the trust). Pair it with flagged uncertainty — he's confident about the research, humble about himself.

**Example:** "And one day I woke up with three days until the deadline, still not having written a word, and so I did the only thing I could: I wrote 90 pages over 72 hours, pulling not one but two all-nighters." — TED (2016)
**Example:** "And they say, 'It's the best one we've ever seen.' … That did not happen. … It was a very, very bad thesis." — TED (2016) — he builds the reader up just to undercut himself.
**Example:** "In college, the sudden unbridled personal freedom was a disaster for me — I did nothing, ever, for any reason." — *Why Procrastinators Procrastinate* (2013)

## 6. The Build-Then-Drop (the rhythm of the deadpan)

**What:** Run a long, accreting, escalating sentence or riff — then land it on a tiny, deadpan beat. The contrast between the elaborate build and the four-word drop is where the humor and the emphasis live.

**When:** To punctuate a big idea or a long list. The drop is the period at the end of the symphony.

**Example:** "To be human-level intelligent, a computer would have to understand things like the difference between subtle facial expressions… and why *Braveheart* was great but *The Patriot* was terrible. \[paragraph break\] Daunting." — *The AI Revolution* (2015)
**Example:** "and then googling that and reading about it for a while (the tiger would win). I have problems." — *Why Procrastinators Procrastinate* (2013)
**Example:** "And this entire situation, with the three characters — this is the procrastinator's system. It's not pretty, but in the end, it works." — TED (2016)

## 7. The Drawing Described in Prose (the cartoon as load-bearing explanation)

**What:** Treat the illustration as part of the argument, not garnish — reference it directly in the prose, walk the reader through it, and let the simple stick figure or 2x2 grid or Venn diagram do explanatory work words alone would labor at. In a text-only medium, this becomes *describing* the picture so vividly the reader draws it in their head.

**When:** Whenever a relationship is easier to see than to say — two forces fighting, a spectrum, an overlap, a grid of trade-offs.

**Example:** "let's start by understanding a *non*-procrastinator's brain: \[drawing\] Pretty normal, right? Now, let's look at a procrastinator's brain: \[drawing\] Notice anything different?" — *Why Procrastinators Procrastinate* (2013)
**Example:** "leaving him spending a lot of time in this orange zone, an easy and fun place that's entirely out of the Makes Sense circle. I call it the Dark Playground." — TED (2016) — the Venn overlap *is* the explanation.

## 8. The Rhetorical-Question Hinge (voicing the reader's "wait, what?")

**What:** Say out loud the exact skeptical or confused question forming in the reader's head, then answer it in the next line. It keeps the piece feeling like a two-way conversation and pre-empts the objection instead of ignoring it.

**When:** At every turn where the reader might resist, doubt, or lose the thread. It's his conversational drumbeat.

**Example:** "So then why, when you hear me say something like 'the world 35 years from now might be totally unrecognizable,' are you thinking, 'Cool….but nahhhhhhh'? Three reasons we're skeptical of outlandish forecasts of the future:" — *The AI Revolution* (2015)
**Example:** "And guess who woke up? \[the Panic Monster\]" — TED (2016)
**Example:** "Given this predicament, how does the procrastinator ever manage to accomplish anything? As it turns out, there's one thing that scares the shit out of the Instant Gratification Monkey:" — *Why Procrastinators Procrastinate* (2013)

## 9. The Footnote-as-Aside (the wink in the margin)

**What:** Use footnotes for the conversational asides, confessions, and jokes that would derail the main line but are too good to cut. The footnote is where he is most intimately himself with the reader.

**When:** Any time a thought is a tangent, a caveat, a source, or a self-incriminating joke. In /write output, render as a parenthetical aside or a short bracketed note if footnotes aren't supported by the format.

**Example:** "It's a place where leisure activities happen at times when leisure activities are not supposed to be happening.[1]" → footnote: "A lot of you are probably reading this article while in the Dark Playground." — *Why Procrastinators Procrastinate* (2013)
**Example:** footnote: "I spent two hours in the Dark Playground *right* before I drew the Dark Playground drawing, because I was dreading having to draw the signpost, which I knew would be hard and take forever (which it did)." — *Why Procrastinators Procrastinate* (2013)

## 10. Think Like a Chef, Not a Cook (the first-principles worldview, made concrete)

**What:** Reframe the abstract distinction between reasoning from first principles vs. reasoning by analogy into a kitchen: a chef invents the recipe by playing with raw ingredients; a cook follows someone else's recipe. The metaphor turns an epistemology lecture into something you instantly grasp and can apply to your own choices.

**When:** Whenever the point is *don't just copy the default — reason it out yourself.* He warns against letting "society" (the rambling great uncle) write your recipe for the things that matter most.

**Example:** "reasoning from first principles is reasoning like a scientist. You take core facts and observations and use them to puzzle together a conclusion, kind of like a chef playing around with raw ingredients… The other kind of reasoning — reasoning by analogy — happens when you look at the way things are already done and you essentially copy it… kind of like a cook following an already written recipe." — *How to Pick a Career* (2018)
**Example:** "society is like your great uncle who traps you at holidays and goes on a 15-minute mostly incoherent unsolicited advice monologue… and conventional wisdom is like his rant. Except in this case, instead of tuning it out, we pay rapt attention to every word, and then we make major career decisions based on what he says." — *How to Pick a Career* (2018)

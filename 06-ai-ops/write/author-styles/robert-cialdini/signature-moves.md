# Robert B. Cialdini — Signature Moves

## 1. The Puzzle-First Opening (his most recognizable structural move)

**What:** Never open on a thesis. Open on a real behavior that makes *no sense* — something concrete, ordinary, and surprising — and tell it as a short story. Let the reader sit in the mystery. Only once they're hooked do you reveal the hidden psychological lever that explains it. The puzzle creates the appetite; the principle satisfies it.

**When:** The start of almost any piece. Reach for it whenever you have a counterintuitive fact, a behavior that "shouldn't" work but does, or an everyday scene whose strangeness the reader has never noticed.

**Example:** A jewelry-store owner's hard-to-sell turquoise finally sold out — but only after a scribbled note ("price *x ½*") was misread as "*x 2*" and the whole lot went at *twice* the intended price. "I can make no sense of their behavior unless it is viewed in *click, run* terms." — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example (the question form):** "Just what are the factors that cause one person to say yes to another? And which techniques most effectively use these factors to bring about compliance?" — *Influence*, Introduction

## 2. The Self-as-Patsy Confession (his trust-building move)

**What:** Put yourself one rung *below* the reader. Confess your own susceptibility — that you are the lifelong easy mark, the sucker, the patsy — so the reader can admit their own without shame. It is a deliberate act of disarmament: nobody feels stupid in a Cialdini chapter, because the author is the biggest mark in the room, and that is precisely what earns him the right to teach.

**When:** Openings, and any moment the reader might feel talked-down-to or defensive. Reach for it before a hard truth about how easily we're all played.

**Example:** "I can admit it freely now. All my life I've been a patsy." — *Influence*, Introduction
**Example:** He describes long being "an easy mark for the pitches of peddlers, fundraisers, and operators of one sort or another," and traces his whole research career to "this long-standing status as sucker." — *Influence*, Introduction (short, attributed)

---

## 3. The Named Experiment with Exact Numbers (the whole authority of the voice)

**What:** Prove every claim with a *real, named controlled study and its exact number.* This is non-negotiable and it is what separates Cialdini from every motivational imitator: the percentage is not decoration, it *is* the argument. Name the scientist, state the finding, give the precise figure, footnote the source. The reader can check it — and that checkability is the trust.

**When:** Every time you assert a mechanism. The story hooks; the study proves. Without the number, you only have an opinion dressed as science.

**Example:** In Ellen Langer's photocopier study, a request with a real reason ("because I'm in a rush") let her skip the line 94% of the time, versus 60% with no reason — but a *meaningless* reason ("because I have to make copies") still worked on 93%. The word *because* triggered compliance even when it carried no information. — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** People rated a wine as working better when it cost $1.89 than at $0.89, and a pain reliever as more effective at $2.50 than at $0.10 — though the products were identical. The *expensive = good* stereotype fulfilled itself. — *Influence*, ch. 1

> Discipline: if you do not have a real, checkable finding, make the claim weaker — never invent the data. A fabricated statistic is the one move that destroys this voice.

## 4. The Ethology Parallel / "Click, Run" (his core mechanism-picture)

**What:** Show that the behavior is an *automatic preset program* by reaching into animal behavior — fixed-action patterns, trigger features — and then bringing it home to humans. The mother turkey mothers anything that goes "cheep-cheep" and attacks anything that doesn't; the male robin attacks a clump of red feathers. A single trigger fires the whole sequence. "*Click*, and the program runs." Then the turn: *we have the same preset programs, and our triggers can be tripped at the wrong times.*

**When:** Whenever you want to make an automatic, below-conscious response feel real and forgivable. The animal parallel makes "you do this without thinking" land as biology, not insult.

**Example:** "*Click*, and the appropriate program is activated; *run*, and out rolls the standard sequence of behaviors." — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** "It is almost as if the patterns were installed as programs within the animals." — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example (the human turn):** our preset programs "usually work to our advantage," but "the trigger features that activate them can dupe us into running the right programs at the wrong times." — *Influence*, ch. 1

## 5. The Coined Handle (make the idea portable)

**What:** Stamp a short, sticky label on the phenomenon so it becomes a thing the reader can carry around and recognize later. The handle compresses a whole mechanism into two or three words and makes it memorable, quotable, and reusable. Half of Cialdini's lasting influence is his labels.

**When:** The moment you've explained a mechanism worth remembering. Name it — ideally with a vivid or slightly wry handle — and use the name consistently thereafter.

**Example:** "*expensive = good*" — the price-equals-quality stereotype, named so cleanly you never forget it. — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** "*Captainitis*" — the airline-industry name for crew members deferring to a captain's obvious error, even into a crash; the deadly form of the "if an expert said so, it must be true" rule. — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** "*click, run*," "the *contrast principle*," "the *weapons* (or *levers*) of influence," "the *profiteers*" — each a portable handle on a mechanism.

## 6. The Mechanism-Metaphor (turn the invisible force physical)

**What:** Take the abstract psychological force and give it a single vivid physical picture — most famously **jujitsu**: the persuader, like a martial artist, uses *your own* strength (the principle already wired into you) against you, "exerting little personal force." The metaphor explains why influence feels like a natural event rather than a manipulation — which is exactly its danger.

**When:** When the mechanism risks staying abstract, or when you want to convey that the persuader is *commissioning a force that's already inside the target.* Reach for the lever, the weapon, the trigger, the current.

**Example:** "A woman employing jujitsu… uses her own strength only minimally against an opponent," exploiting forces like gravity and momentum — and so the persuader exploits the levers already present in us. — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** the great advantage to the exploiter is "the ability to manipulate without the appearance of manipulation"; even the victims "tend to see their compliance as a result of the action of natural forces." — *Influence*, ch. 1

## 7. The Inclusive "You and I" (author and reader in the same boat)

**What:** Use the first-person-plural that *implicates the author too.* Not "you are susceptible" (accusing) and not "people are susceptible" (distancing), but "*you and I*" — equally wired, equally played. It is the grammatical form of his whole stance: we are all marks, and the writing is on the reader's side of the table.

**When:** Whenever you describe a universal vulnerability. The "you and I" turns a potential insult into a shared condition.

**Example:** "*You and I* exist in an extraordinarily complicated environment," easily the most rapidly moving and complex ever — "to deal with it, we need simplifying shortcuts." — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** the principles "have moved us about so pervasively… that *you and I* rarely perceive their power." — *Influence*, ch. 1

---

## 8. The Fair-Minded Cost-Benefit ("a kinder view")

**What:** Refuse to mock people for taking the shortcut. Stop and show that the rule of thumb is *rational most of the time* — the price usually does track quality; the expert usually is right — and that without our shortcuts we would be paralyzed. The reader who got fooled "bet wrong" this once, but over a lifetime the bet is smart. This fairness is a moral position, and it is what keeps the voice generous instead of cynical.

**When:** Right after you've shown a shortcut backfiring. Reach for it to keep the reader on your side and to avoid the cheap "people are dumb" tone.

**Example:** "It is easy to fault the tourists for their foolish purchase decisions, but a close look offers a kinder view." They were betting a normally-reliable rule. — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** "betting those shortcut odds represents the most rational approach"; without the simplifying features "we would stand frozen — cataloging, appraising, and calibrating — as the time for action sped by." — *Influence*, ch. 1

---

## 9. The Defensive Turn / "How to Say No" (the ethical payoff)

**What:** End by *arming the reader.* The point of mapping a lever of influence is not to wield it but to recognize when a "profiteer" is counterfeiting it. The ethical line is real and detectable: a genuine signal (a true expert, a real scarcity, an honest favor) may be honestly heeded; a *fabricated* one must be refused. Teach the reader to tell the difference and to say *no* to the fake.

**When:** Closings, and any time you've just handed the reader a powerful technique. The defense is what makes the knowledge safe to give.

**Example:** The profiteers "mimic trigger features" — counterfeiting the genuine signal — the way fake online reviews counterfeit real social proof; the defense is to spot the counterfeit (fakes "lack detail," lean on first-person pronouns, "include more verbs than nouns"). — *Influence*, ch. 1
**Example:** each principle is "a detectable and ready lever" — and *detectable* is the operative word: once you can see the lever, you can refuse the hand that pulls it dishonestly. — *Influence*, ch. 1

## 10. The Reader's-Report Anecdote (everyday first-person grounding)

**What:** Drop in a short, real, first-person story — his own, or a reader's — that shows the principle working on an actual person in ordinary life. It grounds the laboratory science in lived experience and proves the mechanism isn't just a campus artifact. He literally builds this into the book as "Reader's Reports."

**When:** After a study, to show the same effect in the wild; or as the puzzle that opens a section. Use a real, specific, slightly humbling human moment.

**Example:** A man is *thrilled* to pay $250 for a necklace after first being quoted $500 for it — the contrast principle at work — where moments earlier the same $250 price had felt offensive. — *Influence*, ch. 1 (Reader's Report)
**Example (the dry wink that ends one):** of "Sharon," who writes her parents a horror-story letter, then reveals it's all invented to soften a bad report card — "Sharon may be failing chemistry, but she gets an A in psychology." — *Influence*, ch. 1 (Author's note)

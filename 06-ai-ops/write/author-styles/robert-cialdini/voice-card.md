# Robert B. Cialdini — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A genial research psychologist who went undercover among the "compliance professionals" and now explains why we say *yes* by opening on a real behavior that makes no sense, revealing the hidden psychological lever behind it, proving the lever with a named experiment and its exact number, lighting the mechanism up with a picture from the animal kingdom or the dojo, and then — because he's honest about the cost — arming you against the person who counterfeits the trigger; he's the lifelong patsy in every story, so you never feel lectured, only let in on it.

**Rhythm rule:** Measured, professorial, flowing sentences — built for the *argument's* momentum, not clipped punch. Pose the puzzle as a question, walk through the study, then let a short italic refrain ("*Click, run.*") or a flat line reset the beat. Favor the colon/fresh-sentence setup-and-payoff and the semicolon-hinged antithesis ("those who don't… soon fall away; those who do, stay and flourish").
**Diction rule:** The precise behavioral-science term *immediately* followed by the homely picture — "fixed-action pattern" and a mother turkey in the same breath. Coin a sticky handle for the phenomenon (*expensive = good*, *Captainitis*, *click, run*). Italicize the one load-bearing word (*because*, *seems*). Numbers land exact and sourced (94% vs 60%), never rounded away.
**Reader rule:** Write "you and I." Put author and reader in the same boat — equally wired, equally susceptible. Confess your own gullibility to lower their guard, never mock theirs. Arm them, don't impress them.

## Do (top 7)
1. **Open on a puzzle or a confession**, never a thesis — a real behavior that makes no sense (jewelry that sold *faster* at double the price), told as a story, hooked *before* the principle is named.
2. **Name the hidden lever, then coin a handle for it** — the single psychological principle that explains the puzzle, stamped with a short sticky label.
3. **Prove it with a real, named study and its exact number** — "social psychologist [Name] found [exact %]…". The percentage *is* the argument. This is the whole authority of the voice.
4. **Reach for the ethology / preset-program parallel** ("fixed-action patterns," trigger features, "*click, run*") to show the behavior is automatic — then bring it home: *we run the same programs.*
5. **Turn the mechanism physical with one vivid metaphor** — jujitsu (the persuader uses the force's own strength), the lever, the weapon, the trigger.
6. **Be fair to the shortcut** — say plainly it's rational most of the time ("a kinder view"); we'd "stand frozen" without it. Never shame the reader for using it.
7. **End on the defense** — how to tell the genuine signal from the counterfeit, how to say *no* to the "profiteer." Arm the reader; that beat is load-bearing.

## Don't (top 7)
1. **Never invent a study, statistic, or percentage.** His entire authority is real, checkable evidence; a fabricated "research shows" is the one unforgivable move. No real finding → weaken the claim, never fake the data.
2. **Don't open on a thesis or a definition** — hook with the puzzle or the confession; explain after.
3. **Don't mock or shame the reader** for being susceptible — the shortcut is rational, and *you* are the biggest patsy in the room. Scorn is for the manipulator only.
4. **Don't drop the ethics-and-defense beat** and read as a how-to-manipulate manual — you'd become the very profiteer the voice warns against.
5. **Don't assert a mechanism with no concrete carrier** — the science always rides on a story, an animal, an everyday scene.
6. **Don't leave a technical term unexplained** or perform erudition — precise word, then homely picture, same breath.
7. **Don't close on a motivational flourish** — land on the lever and the defense, not on inspiration.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The self-implicating confession — "I can admit it freely now. All my life I've been a patsy." (*Influence*, Introduction)
- The puzzle that makes no sense — a jewelry-store owner's turquoise finally sells out, but only after a misread note doubles the price; "I can make no sense of their behavior unless it is viewed in *click, run* terms." (*Influence*, ch. 1)
- The hand-the-reader-the-mystery question — "Just what are the factors that cause one person to say yes to another?" (*Influence*, Introduction)

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The lever named + the reader armed — the principle is "a detectable and ready lever," and now you can refuse the profiteer who works it on you.
- The fair-minded verdict — over a lifetime, "betting those shortcut odds represents the most rational approach"; the reader is informed, never shamed.
- The dry practical wink — "Sharon may be failing chemistry, but she gets an A in psychology." (*Influence*, ch. 1, Author's note)

## 1. The Cold-Open Dossier
**What:** Open mid-scene on one specific human — exact place, prices, dates, sensory particulars, often dialogue — with the thesis withheld for paragraphs. Sometimes even the person's name is withheld until it detonates.
**When:** Every piece's first paragraph; whenever an abstract argument needs a body to live in before it is stated.
**Example:** "His chosen drink was vodka. Beer he called 'horse piss.'" — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
**Example:** "There was a moment, in the third and deciding set of the 1993 Wimbledon final, when Jana Novotna seemed invincible." — *The Art of Failure* (2000)
**Self-description:** "I think that the the way into complicated ideas is through uh people and their stories" — The Big Idea interview (2016)

## 2. The Puzzle Volley
**What:** Convert the opening scene into a mystery by firing 2–4 stacked, unanswered questions — the hinge from anecdote to investigation. The questions ARE the thesis statement.
**When:** End of the lede section; again mid-piece whenever a new dataset contradicts expectation.
**Example:** "But what if Enron failed not in spite of its talent mind-set but because of it? What if smart people are overrated?" — *The Talent Myth* (2002)
**Self-description:** "the interesting question is not what does Caesar see when he looks at a dog it's what does the dog see when he looks at Caesar" — Q&A, public talk (2009)

## 3. The Category Split
**What:** Take one familiar word, split it into two named species, and land the distinction in strict parallel antithesis — A is X; B is Y; they look the same but are worlds apart. The piece's central theoretical payload.
**When:** About one-third in, after the puzzle is set.
**Example:** "Choking is about thinking too much. Panic is about thinking too little. Choking is about loss of instinct. Panic is reversion to instinct." — *The Art of Failure* (2000)
**Example:** "High-risk activism, McAdam concluded, is a 'strong-tie' phenomenon." (vs. weak ties) — *Small Change* (2010)

## 4. The Borrowed-Then-Branded Handle
**What:** Coin — or lift from a researcher and re-brand — a sticky two-to-four-word name for the mechanism, define it in one sentence, then repeat it as a refrain that carries the argument across domains. Often becomes the title.
**When:** Immediately after the category split or central study.
**Example:** "Pilots call this the graveyard spiral." — *The Art of Failure* (2000)
**Example:** "'It cost us one million dollars not to do something about Murray,' O'Bryan said." (the quote that becomes the title-handle) — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
**Self-description:** "that relates to something that um I like to call the mismatch problem" — New Yorker hiring talk (2014)

## 5. The Named-Researcher Set Piece
**What:** Introduce an academic with full name + discipline + institution + one humanizing detail, then narrate their study chronologically as a story with a reveal — never as a citation. Variant: become the subject yourself and report sensations as data (the participatory "I").
**When:** The proof section; each pillar of the argument gets its own researcher-character.
**Example:** "Then, fifteen years ago, a young Boston College graduate student named Dennis Culhane lived in a shelter in Philadelphia for seven weeks as part of the research for his dissertation." — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
**Self-description:** "I don't Farm out my research I'm still in the thick of things I still go on reporting trips" — Revenge of the Tipping Point interview (2024)

## 6. The Because-Not-Despite Inversion
**What:** Flip received wisdom with a signature syntax — "not in spite of X but because of it," "it's the other way around" — so the conventional view becomes the anomaly needing explanation.
**When:** The thesis turn (often phrased first as a what-if question), and again as the closing stinger.
**Example:** "The talent myth assumes that people make organizations smart. More often than not, it's the other way around." — *The Talent Myth* (2002)
**Example:** (dyslexic entrepreneurs) "they will tell you they succeeded not in spite of their dis disability but because of it" — Adam Grant interview (2013)
**Self-description:** "I would rather be interesting than correct" (immediately softened: "I turn out not to be right I'm not devastated I accept that as the price of doing business") — Revenge of the Tipping Point interview (2024)

## 7. The Number Made Narrative
**What:** Render statistics as scenes, prices, and absurdly specific comparators so the number argues instead of decorating; head-to-head figures go in a parenthetical aside. Never a naked statistic.
**When:** Wherever a claim needs force; the more counterintuitive the claim, the more concrete the number.
**Example:** "The Facebook page of the Save Darfur Coalition has 1,282,339 members, who have donated an average of nine cents apiece." — *Small Change* (2010)
**Example:** "'We're talking about a cot eighteen inches away from the next cot.'" — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)

## 8. The Power-Law Re-Graph
**What:** Re-describe a problem's SHAPE — not a bell curve but a hockey stick; the action is at the extreme — then force the uncomfortable consequence the new shape implies.
**When:** Systemic problems; the re-graph IS the insight, and the moral discomfort is stated, not dodged.
**Example:** "If you were to graph the troubles of the L.A.P.D., it wouldn't look like a bell curve. It would look more like a hockey stick." — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
**Example:** "We can be true to our principles or we can fix the problem. We cannot do both." — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)

## 9. The Second-Person Conscription
**What:** Draft the reader into the experiment or dilemma with a hypothetical "you"; then widen to an indicting "we" that shares the blame for the bad assumption. Never "you should."
**When:** Explaining experiments (you are the subject); landing moral conclusions (we are the culprits).
**Example:** "If you were to graph the troubles of the L.A.P.D…" — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
**Example:** "…is not simply that we didn't know better. It's that we didn't want to know better." — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)

## 10. The Generous Villain
**What:** Steelman the people who are wrong — they are smart, well-meaning, even sympathetic; the failure is located in the system, the incentive, or the distribution, never in stupidity or malice. Converts a takedown into a diagnosis.
**When:** Right before or after demolishing a practice; it pre-empts the defensive reader.
**Example:** "They believe in stars, because they don't believe in systems." — *The Talent Myth* (2002)
**Self-description:** "I think everyone's interesting I really honestly seriously believe that" — Q&A, public talk (2009)

## 11. The Story–Theory Shuttle
**What:** Alternate narrative blocks and explanation blocks, joined by explicit hinge sentences — a "why does this matter" question, a "Consider…" imperative, or the flat "X is like Y" analogy bridge that ports the theory to a new domain.
**When:** Every act break; the bridge is how disparate cases (cops → homeless → smog) become one argument.
**Example:** "The homelessness problem is like the L.A.P.D.'s bad-cop problem." — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
**Self-description:** "I like when I have an academic argument I like to find narratives to complete it" — Q&A, public talk (2009)

## 12. The Full-Circle Coda
**What:** End by returning to the opening character or image with the theory now loaded — landing on a quiet human beat (a quote, a gesture) or a sardonic one-line sting. Never a summary.
**When:** Final paragraph, always; tender vs. acid is chosen by whether the piece's villain is fate or fashion.
**Example:** "'I told my husband that I would claim his body if no one else did,' she said. 'I would not have him in an unmarked grave.'" — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
**Example:** "It never occurred to them that, if everyone had to think outside the box, maybe it was the box that needed fixing." — *The Talent Myth* (2002)
**Self-description:** "we don't change the world what we do is we start conversations" — Q&A, public talk (2009)

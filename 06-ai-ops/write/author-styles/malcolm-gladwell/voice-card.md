# Malcolm Gladwell — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** The narrative social-science translator who opens cold inside one real person's life (a name, a place, a price), pivots on a what-if question, splits a familiar idea into two named species, proves it with a named-researcher story and a number made narrative, hops the idea across one wildly unlike domain, and lands the callback ending where the thesis detonates as a quiet inversion — maybe it was the box that needed fixing.

**Rhythm rule:** Mid-length narration (median ~16 words) punctured by a 2–6-word verdict at every pivot ("Double fault." "We cannot do both."). Anaphora for the build ("We try harder. We concentrate harder."), strict parallel antithesis for the definition ("Choking is about thinking too much. Panic is about thinking too little."). The aphorism lands as a paragraph's LAST sentence. No em-dashes (the humanizer strips them; his prose offers the substitutes): use the colon-reveal, the parenthetical aside, the semicolon antithesis, or a flat short second sentence ("But that's not true.").
**Diction rule:** Plain words carrying hyper-specific detail — the brand, the price, the street ("a dollar-fifty," "eighteen inches"). Coin the category name in quotes on first use; gloss every technical term with "what statisticians call X" plus a plain appositive. Numbers spelled out in words unless precision is the point. Superlatives ("brilliant," "superb") only for OTHER people's work, never your own thesis.
**Reader rule:** The reader is "experience rich and theory poor" — a smart, busy prediction machine you are paid to ambush ("a good story betrays the expectations of its listeners"). Use "we" to share the blame, "you" to run the thought experiment, "I" only when physically reporting. Never advise, never moralize, never condescend. Start a conversation; don't end it.

## Do (top 7)
1. **Open cold inside one real person's life** — name, place, date, one hyper-specific detail; thesis withheld. "His chosen drink was vodka. Beer he called 'horse piss.'"
2. **Pivot with stacked what-if questions** — the questions ARE the thesis: "But what if Enron failed not in spite of its talent mind-set but because of it?"
3. **Install ONE category distinction** in drumbeat parallel sentences, then re-run it across an unlike domain welded by a flat bridge: "The homelessness problem is like the L.A.P.D.'s bad-cop problem."
4. **Narrate research as story** — "a young Boston College graduate student named Dennis Culhane lived in a shelter in Philadelphia for seven weeks" — then let the researcher SPEAK in quotation.
5. **Make every number narrative**: benchmark it, pair it, anchor it to a human ("1,282,339 members, who have donated an average of nine cents apiece").
6. **Be generous to the villain** — smart people inside a bad system; locate failure in incentives, never stupidity: "They believe in stars, because they don't believe in systems."
7. **End on the callback** — return to the opening person or image with the theory loaded; land on their voice or the inverted cliché ("maybe it was the box that needed fixing"). Never summarize.

## Don't (top 7)
1. **Invent a person, study, or number** — his entire authority is that the cases are real and checkable. This is the unforgivable move.
2. Open with context, a definition, or "In today's world…" — no throat-clearing, ever.
3. Say "I think / I believe / in my view," or refer to the writing itself.
4. Tell the reader what to do — no imperatives, no advice, no "you should" (the closest allowed: "it ought to give you pause").
5. Drop a naked statistic (a number with no benchmark, pair, or human anchor) — or hype your own thesis with superlatives.
6. Use exclamation marks, headers, bullets, listicles, or bolded takeaways — structure is scene-cuts and paragraphs.
7. Moralize with vocabulary ("shameful," "unacceptable") — judgment arrives through structure and other people's voices.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The character-habit cold open — "His chosen drink was vodka. Beer he called 'horse piss.'" — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
- The suspended instant — "There was a moment, in the third and deciding set of the 1993 Wimbledon final, when Jana Novotna seemed invincible." — *The Art of Failure* (2000)
- The named-institution time-stamp — "Five years ago, several executives at McKinsey & Company… launched what they called the War for Talent." — *The Talent Myth* (2002)

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The callback in the character's voice — "I would not have him in an unmarked grave." — *Million-Dollar Murray* (2006)
- The inverted-cliché sting — "…if everyone had to think outside the box, maybe it was the box that needed fixing." — *The Talent Myth* (2002)
- The sardonic kicker — "Viva la revolución." — *Small Change* (2010)

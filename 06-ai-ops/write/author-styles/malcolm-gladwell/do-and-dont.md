# Malcolm Gladwell — Do & Don't

<!-- The fastest reference during drafting + the humanizer's voice-fidelity checklist.
     Each "do" is something to actively reach for; each "don't" would break the illusion
     that Gladwell wrote it. -->

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Open cold inside one REAL person's life: name, place, date, one hyper-specific detail ("a dollar-fifty"). Thesis withheld. | Open with context, a definition, a statistic, or "In today's world…" — any throat-clearing kills it instantly. |
| Pivot from scene to thesis with stacked what-if questions ("What if smart people are overrated?"). | State the thesis as an assertion up front, or write "in this article I will argue…". |
| Install ONE category distinction in strict parallel antithesis: "A is about X. B is about Y. They may look the same, but they are worlds apart." | Pile up three or four frameworks; the piece carries exactly one binary, re-run across domains. |
| Narrate research as a story: full name + discipline + university + one humanizing detail, then the researcher SPEAKS in quotation. | Cite like a paper ("studies show," "research indicates," "(Smith, 2006)") or summarize a study without a human in it. |
| Make every number narrative: benchmarked, paired, or anchored to a human ("a cot eighteen inches away from the next cot"; "nine cents apiece"). | Drop a naked statistic, round numbers into vagueness ("many," "a lot"), or let a figure stand without a comparator. |
| Weld unlike domains with the flat bridge: "The homelessness problem is like the L.A.P.D.'s bad-cop problem." | Stay inside one domain the whole piece, or apologize for the analogy ("this may seem like a stretch, but…"). |
| Be generous to the villain: smart people inside a bad system ("They believe in stars, because they don't believe in systems."). | Moralize with vocabulary ("shameful," "unacceptable") or make anyone stupid or evil — judgment lives in structure. |
| Use "we" for the moral turn, "you" for the thought experiment, "I" only while physically reporting. | Use "I think / I believe / in my view," or command the reader ("you should," "here's what to do"). |
| Drop a 2–6-word verdict sentence at every pivot ("Double fault." "We cannot do both."); end sections on the aphorism. | Use exclamation marks, headers, bullets, bolded takeaways, or a "Conclusion" that summarizes. |
| Replace his em-dash habit (the humanizer strips dashes) with the colon-reveal, the parenthetical aside, the semicolon antithesis, or a flat short second sentence ("But that's not true."). | Hype your own thesis ("groundbreaking," "fascinating") — superlatives are spent on OTHER people's work only. |
| End with the callback: the opening person returns with the theory loaded — their voice, a gesture, or the inverted cliché ("maybe it was the box that needed fixing"). | **Invent a person, a study, or a number. The one unforgivable move — his entire authority is that the cases are real and checkable.** |

**The one test:** Cover the byline and read the first two sentences and the last two. If the piece doesn't open inside a specific real person's life and close by returning to it with the idea detonated (a voice, a gesture, an inverted cliché), it isn't Gladwell — it's an essay wearing his clothes.

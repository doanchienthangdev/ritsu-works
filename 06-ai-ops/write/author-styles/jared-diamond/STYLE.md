# Jared Diamond — Style DNA

## 0. Voice in one breath
A field scientist who writes history the way a detective explains a solved case: he opens with a giant question posed as a concrete puzzle ("the broad pattern of human history, on all the continents, for the last 13,000 years"), states the surprising verdict early and baldly, then walks you through the evidence in pre-announced, numbered steps. Every abstraction is immediately cashed out in a named place, species, date, and number: not "early farmers were less healthy" but "a nearly 50 per cent increase in enamel defects" at Dickson Mounds around A.D. 1150. The professorial scaffolding is carried by a fieldworker's eyewitness scenes from fifty years of New Guinea trips, and by long, comma-stacked explanatory sentences that land on short flat verdicts. He treats the reader as an intelligent juror: he builds the opposing case fairly before demolishing it, qualifies his claims after stating them (with numbers, not mumbles: "at least 51 percent"), and closes on an open question or a carefully earned optimism, never a sermon.

## 1. Identity & stance
A laboratory physiologist turned ornithologist turned geographer who treats human history as a science in need of a method: where experiment is impossible, compare cases ("natural experiments") and push the chain of causation from proximate to ultimate. Writing, for him, is how a scientist serves as an expert witness to the general public; the posture toward the reader is a professor who genuinely believes you can follow the whole argument, and who therefore shows his work at every step. He is structurally deterministic and humanly sympathetic at once: people act rationally inside constraints they did not choose, so the constraints, not the people, are the thing to explain.

## 2. Diction & vocabulary
- **Reaches for:** concrete nouns of the physical world (crops, skeletons, tree rings, pigs, canoes, palm trees); proper nouns with coordinates in time and space (Dickson Mounds, Easter Island, the Ombal clan, A.D. 1150, 1432); exact quantities ("2,140 calories and 93 grams of protein," "twenty-nine more killings, and the sacrifice of three hundred pigs"); plain Anglo-Saxon verbs for dramatic processes ("chopped down the last tree," "starved to death amidst abundant food").
- **Refuses:** business and technology jargon; appeal-to-authority filler ("studies show," "experts agree" — he names the researcher and the institution instead: "says Armelagos"); rhetorical escalation and alarm-words; abstractions left floating without an anchor case.
- **Register:** formal-academic syntax delivered in an unhurried conversational temper; he shifts toward the colloquial exactly when translating a technical term or delivering dry wit ("the sole animal domesticated within sub-Saharan Africa was [you guess] a bird, the Guinea fowl").
- **Concreteness:** near-zero tolerance for unanchored abstraction. A technical term may be used freely but is glossed in plain words within the same breath: "paleopathology, the study of signs of disease in the remains of ancient peoples."

## 3. Syntax & sentence mechanics
- **Sentence length:** long, cumulative, comma-stacked sentences (30-50 words) doing the explanatory work, regularly relieved by short flat verdict sentences ("Just count our advantages." "I'm going to be dead.").
- **Rhythm:** a patient build-and-land cadence: three or four parallel clauses accumulate evidence, then a compressed clause delivers the payload ("Tuberculosis and diarrheal disease had to await the rise of farming, measles and bubonic plague the appearance of large cities.").
- **Fragments / parallelism / repetition:** almost no fragments; heavy deliberate parallelism in enumerations and contrasts ("there's a thin slice on top... there's a thin slice on the bottom..."); repetition of the key noun as successive sentence-opener to accumulate definitional weight (Easter Island... Easter Island...); chain-repetition to track causation ("Germany declared war on France. When Germany declared war on France, Britain declared war on Germany.").
- **Punctuation habits:** commas to stack qualifications; colons to introduce enumerations and examples; parentheses for genuine asides and concessions ("(Some archaeologists think it was the crowding, rather than agriculture, that promoted disease, but this is a chicken-and-egg argument...)"). Dashes are rare in his prose; render any aside with a comma, a colon, parentheses, or a period plus a new short sentence, never a dash.
- **Paragraphing:** solid expository paragraphs (4-8 sentences), each advancing one step of the argument; paragraph breaks often follow a self-posed question, letting the answer open the next paragraph.

## 4. Structure & architecture
- **Openings:** three verified patterns. (1) The widening frame that lands on a provocation: a series of what-science-taught-us clauses arriving at "Now archaeology is demolishing another sacred belief." (2) The modest-task self-framing: an enormous question stated with concrete scope and ironic humility ("I've set myself the modest task..."). (3) The in-medias-res case: year, name, age, relationship, place in one sentence ("In 1992, when Daniel Wemp was about twenty-two years old, his beloved paternal uncle Soll was killed in a battle against the neighboring Ombal clan.").
- **Development:** question-driven and enumerated. He poses the reader's next question aloud, answers it, and moves the causal chain one link deeper ("Now, let's try to push the chain of causation back further."). Major claims are validated by running the same hypothesis across independent cases (continents, islands, centuries). The strongest objection is stated fairly before it is dismantled with data.
- **Closings:** never a tidy bow. Either paired open questions with stakes ("As our second midnight approaches, will the plight of famine-stricken peasants gradually spread to engulf us all? Or..."), or the cautious-optimist pivot: pessimistic reasons enumerated first, then one structural reason for hope, ideally quantified ("at least 51 percent").
- **Headers/lists/length:** in essays he writes continuous prose and builds lists inside sentences; the enumeration lives in the syntax ("First... Second... Finally...") rather than in bullet formatting. Comfortable at 2,000-6,000 words; the argument, not a word budget, sets the length.

## 5. Rhetorical devices (signature moves)
Full detail in `signature-moves.md`: The Modest Task (giant question as concrete puzzle); The Premise Inversion (name the expected answer, show it's wrong); The Steelman Demolition (build the opposing case fairly, then dismantle with data); The Announced Enumeration ("There are at least three sets of reasons..."); The Self-Posed Question (ask the reader's question aloud, answer immediately); Do the Numbers (convert intuition into arithmetic); The Rice-Bag Anecdote (one eyewitness fieldwork scene as proof); The Time-Machine Rescale (compress incomprehensible scale into a graspable object: the 24-hour clock, the Big Mac map); The Uncomfortable Mirror (implicate the modern reader in the historical failure); The Cautious-Optimist Close.

## 6. Tone & emotional register
- **Authority:** prosecutorial confidence about the thesis, scientific tentativeness about the edges; the qualification always follows the claim and is always precise ("on the average," "at least," "I'm cautiously optimistic").
- **Warmth:** appears as patience and inclusion, not effusion: he restates questions, glosses terms, assigns "homework," and assumes you can follow.
- **Humor:** dry, deadpan, often self-deprecating, built by absurdist accumulation rather than jokes ("I have not detected average differences in ego between new guineans and Americans but then I don't have a reliable ego meter").
- **Edge:** moral seriousness without moralizing; outrage is compressed into one exact value-laden phrase inside an otherwise clinical sentence ("the gross social and sexual inequality, the disease and despotism, that curse our existence").

## 7. Themes & worldview
- **Geography loads the dice; biology does not.** Differences in societies' fates trace to environments (domesticable species, continental axes, isolation), never to differences among peoples.
- **Progress is a hypothesis to test, not a premise.** Received narratives of improvement (agriculture, civilization, growth) are repeatedly checked against skeletons, middens, and tree rings, and often fail.
- **The comparative method is the lab of history.** Where you cannot experiment, triangulate: Greenland Norse beside Inuit, Easter Island beside Tikopia, hunter-gatherer skeletons beside farmers'.
- **Compound risk governs survival.** Small repeated hazards, not dramatic single events, are what kill: "constructive paranoia" is the rational response (1-in-1,000 a night under a dead tree means dead in three years).
- **Crises are of our own making, therefore solvable.** The trap and the exit are both structural; cautious optimism is earned from precedent, not faith.
- **People are rational inside their constraints.** The Norse fish taboo is no stranger than Americans not eating horses; explain the cultural logic, never the "stupidity."

## 8. Reader relationship
He imagines an intelligent layperson carrying received wisdom that needs careful dismantling, and treats them as a juror: shown the strongest opposing case, given the evidence, trusted to weigh it. "We" is inclusive and implicating ("a catastrophe from which we have never recovered"); "you" appears for thought experiments and homework ("How many milliseconds do you think it would take them...?"; "here's a homework assignment for you"); "I" appears only as the field scientist witnessing ("Once while on a field trip there studying birds, I offered to pay some villagers..."), never as a confessor. He names the reader's discomfort early and uses it as evidence the question matters.

## 9. Formatting & medium habits
Titles are questions or flat provocations ("Why Did Human History Unfold Differently On Different Continents For The Last 13,000 Years?"; "The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race"). Evidence is handled by naming the researcher, the site, and the figure inline; quotations from named witnesses and experts land as testimony ("says Armelagos"). No decorative formatting: the prose carries the structure; enumeration is syntactic ("First... Second... Finally"). In talks he announces the framework before populating it ("I now have a checklist of five factors...").

## 10. Tells of authenticity
- The pre-announced count followed by ordinal delivery ("I'll mention four global challenges"; "I now have a checklist of five factors that I run through"), each item then marked with its ordinal as it arrives.
- A technical term immediately glossed in plain words within the same sentence.
- A New Guinea eyewitness scene deployed as the proof of a macro claim (the 110-pound rice bag; the mongongo-nuts reply).
- The exact-number habit even in hedges ("at least 51 percent that will have a happy ending and only 49 will have a sad end").
- The chain of causation made visible and pushed one level deeper ("proximate" to "ultimate") in plain view of the reader.
- The dry parenthetical aside that concedes a complication while keeping the argument moving ("(but this is a chicken-and-egg argument...)").

## 11. Anti-patterns (what he would NEVER do)
- Hedge before the thesis ("I might be wrong, but..."); he states the claim baldly, then qualifies it precisely afterward.
- Leave an abstraction unanchored: no "historical forces" without a named civilization, date, and mechanism.
- Argue from authority ("research shows..."); he presents the evidence chain and names the specific researcher.
- Close with a call to action, a sermon, or motivational uplift; essays end in questions or earned, quantified hope.
- Use business or technology metaphors; his analogies come from biology, geology, food, and fieldwork (a Big Mac, a 24-hour clock, a marriage).
- Use passive constructions that obscure agency; he names who chose what ("China's one Empire one Emperor said we don't need that fleet").
- Romanticize traditional societies or dismiss the modern world; he credits both and votes with his feet for Los Angeles while keeping the lessons.
- Invent the anecdote or the number; his entire authority is that the cases and counts are real.
- Pepper the prose with em-dashes; his asides live in commas, colons, and parentheses.

## 12. How to write AS Jared Diamond (operational checklist)
1. Open with the question, framed as a concrete puzzle with scope numbers, or with a dated, named case in medias res; never open with throat-clearing context.
2. State the surprising verdict early and baldly; save the qualifications for after, and make them precise.
3. Announce your enumeration before delivering it ("There are at least three reasons..."), then signpost each item with an ordinal.
4. Cash out every abstraction within a sentence: a place, a species, a date, a count. If a paragraph has no number and no proper noun, rewrite it.
5. Steelman the opposing view fairly enough that its holders would nod, then dismantle it with specific evidence.
6. Pose the reader's next question aloud and answer it immediately; let a self-posed question open a new section.
7. Do the numbers: turn at least one intuition into explicit arithmetic the reader can check (per-week hours, per-night risks, per-acre yields).
8. Plant one eyewitness anecdote, told with year and name, as the visceral proof of the macro claim; never invent it.
9. Gloss every technical term in plain words in the same breath you introduce it.
10. Use one rescaling device for anything too large to feel: compress it onto a clock, a map, a familiar object.
11. Write long cumulative sentences with commas, colons, and parentheses; land sections on a short flat verdict sentence; never use a dash.
12. Use "we" to implicate (reader included), "you" for thought experiments, "I" only as field witness.
13. Implicate the modern reader in the historical pattern with one uncomfortable mirror comparison.
14. Close on paired open questions with stakes, or the cautious-optimist pivot: dangers enumerated first, then one structural, ideally quantified, reason for hope.

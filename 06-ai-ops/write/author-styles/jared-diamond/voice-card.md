# Jared Diamond — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A field scientist explaining a solved historical case to an intelligent juror: he opens with a giant question framed as a concrete puzzle, states the surprising verdict baldly and early, then walks the evidence in pre-announced numbered steps, cashing every abstraction into a named place, species, date, and number, proving the macro claim with one eyewitness New Guinea scene, qualifying precisely after the verdict (never before), and closing on an open question or a quantified cautious optimism.

**Rhythm rule:** Long, comma-stacked cumulative sentences do the explaining; land each build on a short flat verdict ("Just count our advantages." "I'm going to be dead."). Let a self-posed question open a section and answer it immediately.
**Diction rule:** The concrete noun with coordinates: a place, a species, a date, an exact count ("twenty-nine more killings, and the sacrifice of three hundred pigs"). Technical terms are welcome but glossed in plain words in the same breath. Asides go in commas, colons, or parentheses, never a dash.
**Reader rule:** An intelligent juror, not a student: show the strongest opposing case fairly, give the evidence chain, trust them to weigh it. "We" implicates everyone (writer included); "you" runs thought experiments; "I" appears only as field witness.

## Do (top 7)
1. **Open with the puzzle or the case** — an enormous question with concrete scope ("the broad pattern of human history, on all the continents, for the last 13,000 years") or a dated, named scene in medias res. No throat-clearing.
2. **State the verdict early and baldly** — "the adoption of agriculture... was in many ways a catastrophe from which we have never recovered." Qualify after, precisely, never before.
3. **Announce the enumeration, then deliver** — "There are at least three sets of reasons..."; "I now have a checklist of five factors..."; signpost each ordinal as you go.
4. **Cash out every abstraction** — within a sentence, name the place, species, date, or number. A paragraph with no proper noun and no count is not Diamond.
5. **Steelman, then demolish with data** — build the progressivist case until it "seems overwhelming," then take it apart with skeletons, middens, and measured heights.
6. **Do the numbers + plant the eyewitness scene** — convert one intuition into checkable arithmetic (5,475 showers; 1-in-1,000 nights), and prove the macro claim with one real fieldwork anecdote told with year and name.
7. **Close cautious-optimist or open-question** — dangers enumerated first, then one structural reason for hope, ideally quantified ("at least 51 percent"), or paired questions with stakes.

## Don't (top 7)
1. **Hedge before the thesis** ("I might be wrong, but...") — the qualification follows the claim and carries a number.
2. **Float an abstraction** — no "historical forces," "innovation," or "disruption" without a named case, date, and mechanism attached.
3. **Argue from authority** — never "studies show"; name the researcher, the site, and the figure ("says Armelagos").
4. **Moralize or call to action** — no sermons, no "we must," no motivational uplift; the evidence and the open question do the work.
5. **Reach for business/tech metaphors** — his analogies are physical and biological (a Big Mac, a 24-hour clock, a marriage, tree rings), never markets or machines.
6. **Obscure agency with passives** — name who chose what ("China's one Empire one Emperor said we don't need that fleet").
7. **Invent the anecdote or the number** — his entire authority is that the cases and counts are real; faking one is the unforgivable move. (And no em-dashes: commas, colons, parentheses.)

## Signature openings (how he starts)
- The modest task — "I've set myself the modest task of trying to explain the broad pattern of human history, on all the continents, for the last 13,000 years." — Edge.org (1997)
- The widening frame to a provocation — "To science we owe dramatic changes in our smug self-image." — *The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race* (Discover, 1987)
- The dated case in medias res — "In 1992, when Daniel Wemp was about twenty-two years old, his beloved paternal uncle Soll was killed in a battle against the neighboring Ombal clan." — *Vengeance Is Ours* (The New Yorker, 2008)

## Signature closings (how he lands)
- Paired open questions with stakes — "As our second midnight approaches, will the plight of famine-stricken peasants gradually spread to engulf us all?" — *The Worst Mistake...* (Discover, 1987)
- The quantified cautious optimism — "I'm cautiously optimistic I see the chances as at least 51 percent that will have a happy ending and only 49 will have a sad end" — People I (Mostly) Admire, ep. 55 (2023)
- The forward-looking scientific promise — "I'm optimistic that we can eventually arrive at convincing explanations for these broadest patterns of human history" — Edge.org (1997)

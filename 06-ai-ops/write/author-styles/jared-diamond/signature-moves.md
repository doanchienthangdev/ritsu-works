# Jared Diamond — Signature Moves

## The Modest Task
**What:** Open by stating an enormous question as a concrete, bounded puzzle, with scope numbers and a straight face of ironic humility.
**When:** The opening of any piece that tackles a big-pattern question; it earns the reader's trust by showing the question's true size while promising it is answerable.
**Example:** "I've set myself the modest task of trying to explain the broad pattern of human history, on all the continents, for the last 13,000 years." — Edge.org (1997)
**How to do it:** Name the full scope honestly (all the continents, 13,000 years), attach the word-level shrug ("modest task"), then immediately pose the question the scope implies. The irony must stay deadpan; never wink at it.

## The Premise Inversion
**What:** Name the answer everyone expects, then announce flatly that the evidence shows it is wrong.
**When:** Whenever received wisdom is the real opponent; it converts the reader's assumption into the essay's antagonist.
**Example:** "the title of my talk today is one that begs a yes answer which I'll show you is wrong is geography increasingly irrelevant in our globalized world" — Upfront Summit talk (2020); and the canonical written form: "the adoption of agriculture, supposedly our most decisive step toward a better life, was in many ways a catastrophe from which we have never recovered" — *The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race* (Discover, 1987)
**How to do it:** State the received view in its own confident words ("supposedly our most decisive step toward a better life"), then attach the inversion in the same sentence, unhedged. The qualifications come later and carry numbers.

## The Steelman Demolition
**What:** Build the opposing case so fairly its holders would nod, then dismantle it with specific evidence.
**When:** The first third of any contrarian argument; the demolition only lands if the steelman was genuinely strong.
**Example:** "While the case for the progressivist view seems overwhelming, it's hard to prove. How do you show that the lives of people 10,000 years ago got better when they abandoned hunting and gathering for farming?" — Discover (1987), after two full paragraphs arguing the other side ("Just count our advantages.")
**How to do it:** Give the opposition its best concrete evidence and its most seductive rhetoric ("How many milliseconds do you think it would take them to appreciate the advantages of agriculture?"), then pivot on a methodological question (how would we actually know?) and bring the data.

## The Announced Enumeration
**What:** Tell the reader how many reasons/factors/challenges are coming, then deliver them with ordinal signposts.
**When:** Any multi-cause explanation; it converts a sprawling middle into a checkable structure.
**Example:** "There are at least three sets of reasons to explain the findings that agriculture was bad for health." — Discover (1987); in lectures: "I now have a checklist of five factors that I run through when trying to understand why Society fails or succeeds" — UCSB lecture on Collapse (2005); "I'll mention four global challenges" — World Knowledge Forum (2025)
**How to do it:** Commit to the count out loud before delivering ("at least three" buys honest room), then mark each item: First... Second... Finally. In prose the enumeration lives in the syntax, not in bullet formatting.

## The Self-Posed Question
**What:** Ask the reader's next question aloud, then answer it immediately; chain these to drive the whole argument.
**When:** Every transition; it is the engine of his development and the natural way he pushes causation one level deeper.
**Example:** "Why weren't Native Americans, Africans, and Aboriginal Australians the ones who conquered or exterminated Europeans and Asians?" — Edge.org (1997); "what's the reason for those big differences in wealth around Africa" — Upfront Summit talk (2020)
**How to do it:** Pose the question in the reader's voice (including the skeptic's version: "you may ask why did the Maya Kings not just look out the windows of the Palaces and see the deforestation" — UCSB lecture, 2005), answer it in the next sentence, and let the answer raise the next question. Never let a rhetorical question sit unanswered.

## Do the Numbers
**What:** Convert an intuition into explicit arithmetic the reader can check, often compounding a small risk or rate into its real consequence.
**When:** Whenever a claim could be dismissed as a feeling; the calculation turns it into a verdict.
**Example:** "do the numbers suppose the risk of that tree falling on me tonight is 1 in 1,000 but if I sleep under dead trees for a Thousand Nights in three years I'm going to be dead" — Royal Institution talk (2013); domesticated for the listener: "I'm 76 statistically I have 15 years ahead of me that means 5,475 showers ahead of me" — same talk
**How to do it:** State the unit risk or rate, multiply it across the real exposure, and land on the blunt consequence in a short sentence. Use real, checkable figures (12 to 19 hours per week; 2,140 calories and 93 grams of protein), never invented ones.

## The Rice-Bag Anecdote
**What:** One personally witnessed fieldwork scene, told with year, name, and physical detail, deployed as the visceral proof of a macro claim.
**When:** Exactly once per major claim, at the moment the statistics risk going abstract.
**Example:** The 110-pound rice bag: "In New Guinea farming communities today I often see women staggering under loads of vegetables and firewood while the men walk empty-handed." — Discover (1987); the witness's own words doing the work: "Why should we, when there are so many mongongo nuts in the world?" — the Bushman's reply, Discover (1987)
**How to do it:** Tell it as testimony: when, where, who, what you saw, in plain declarative sentences. The scene must be real; inventing it is the one unforgivable move, because his entire authority is that the cases are real. Let a named witness or expert speak in quotation ("says Armelagos") rather than paraphrase.

## The Time-Machine Rescale
**What:** Compress an incomprehensible scale onto a graspable everyday object: a 24-hour clock, a fast-food sandwich, a marriage.
**When:** Whenever the true magnitude (deep time, continental wealth gaps, civilizational risk) exceeds intuition.
**Example:** "Suppose that an archaeologist who had visited from outer space were trying to explain human history to his fellow spacelings." (the 24-hour clock where agriculture arrives at 11:54 p.m.) — Discover (1987); "Africa is like a Big Mac" with "there's a thin slice on top the five countries in the North temperate zone there's a thin slice on the bottom the five countries in the South temperate zone" — Upfront Summit talk (2020)
**How to do it:** Pick a physical, slightly homely object the reader handles weekly, map the quantities onto it exactly (state the conversion rate), then read the uncomfortable result off the object.

## The Uncomfortable Mirror
**What:** Implicate the modern reader in the very pattern being explained, via a self-inclusive comparison.
**When:** The moment the reader starts feeling superior to the historical case; the mirror keeps the lesson transferable.
**Example:** On the Norse starving rather than eating fish: "just as we Americans are not eating horses kidneys and hearts" — People I (Mostly) Admire, ep. 55 (2023); "If one could choose between being a peasant farmer in Ethiopia or a bushman gatherer in the Kalahari, which do you think would be the better choice?" — Discover (1987)
**How to do it:** Use "we" so the writer is implicated too, pick a present-day behavior structurally identical to the historical one, and state the parallel without underlining it. The reader should feel the click, not be told about it.

## The Cautious-Optimist Close
**What:** End by enumerating the reasons for pessimism first, then pivoting on one structural, ideally quantified, reason for hope, or on paired open questions with stakes.
**When:** Every ending; he never closes with a sermon, a call to action, or unearned uplift.
**Example:** "I'm cautiously optimistic I see the chances as at least 51 percent that will have a happy ending and only 49 will have a sad end" — People I (Mostly) Admire, ep. 55 (2023); "we are the first Society in world history with television sets and with radios and with newspapers" (the structural advantage: we can watch others' mistakes) — UCSB lecture (2005); the open-question form: "As our second midnight approaches, will the plight of famine-stricken peasants gradually spread to engulf us all?" — Discover (1987)
**How to do it:** Stack the dangers honestly (they must be the ones the piece established), then name the one asset that changes the odds, and put a number on the hope if you can. Alternatively, end on two open questions the evidence has made urgent. Stop there; no exhortation.

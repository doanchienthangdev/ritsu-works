# Jared Diamond — Do & Don't

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Open with the giant question framed as a concrete, bounded puzzle ("on all the continents, for the last 13,000 years"), or a dated named case in medias res | Open with throat-clearing context, a dictionary definition, or an abstract claim about human nature |
| State the surprising verdict baldly and early ("was in many ways a catastrophe from which we have never recovered"), then qualify after, with precision | Hedge before the thesis ("I might be wrong, but...") or bury the claim under caveats |
| Announce the count, then deliver with ordinals: "There are at least three sets of reasons... First... Second... Finally" | Wander through causes unnumbered, or format the enumeration as decorative bullets instead of syntax |
| Cash out every abstraction in a place, species, date, and number ("2,140 calories and 93 grams of protein"; "around A. D. 1150") | Let an abstraction float ("historical forces," "innovation") with no named case or count attached |
| Steelman the opposing view until its holders would nod ("Just count our advantages."), then dismantle it with data | Strawman the opposition, or skip straight to the rebuttal before the reader feels the received view's pull |
| Pose the reader's next question aloud and answer it immediately; push the causal chain one visible level deeper | Leave rhetorical questions hanging, or jump levels of causation without showing the ladder |
| Prove the macro claim with one real eyewitness scene told with year and name (the 110-pound rice bag; Daniel Wemp, 1992) | Invent or embellish an anecdote, a statistic, or a witness quote: the realness of the cases is his entire authority |
| Do the numbers: convert one intuition into checkable arithmetic (1-in-1,000 a night = dead in three years; 5,475 showers) | Assert risk, scale, or benefit as a vibe ("hugely," "massively") with no arithmetic the reader could redo |
| Close on paired open questions with stakes, or quantified cautious optimism ("at least 51 percent") after enumerating the dangers | End with a sermon, a call to action, or motivational uplift the evidence didn't earn |

**The one test:** Cover the byline and read one paragraph aloud: does it pose or answer a concrete question, with at least one named place, date, or number, inside an enumeration you could reconstruct from memory, in long comma-stacked sentences that land on a short verdict? If the evidence floats free of any place or count, or the structure can't be outlined on a napkin, it isn't Diamond.

## Clutter is the enemy — diagnose, then exhibit

> "Clutter is the disease of American writing. We are a society strangling in unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills and meaningless jargon."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Simplicity

**Demonstrates:** The flat diagnostic opening — a thesis stated as plain fact, no throat-clearing. Note the concrete list ("pompous frills," "meaningless jargon") instead of an abstract complaint. He names the disease and the symptoms in two sentences.

---

> "The airline pilot who announces that he is presently anticipating experiencing considerable precipitation wouldn't think of saying it may rain."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Simplicity

**Demonstrates:** The exhibited clutter — he quotes the inflated phrase and lets the contrast with "it may rain" convict it. The persuasion is in the gap between the two, not in any adjective of his own.

---

> "Beware of the long word that's no better than the short word: 'assistance' (help), 'numerous' (many), 'facilitate' (ease), 'individual' (man or woman)."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Clutter

**Demonstrates:** Diction as a paired table — pompous word, then the plain word in parentheses. The whole theory of his vocabulary in one move: always reach for the short Anglo-Saxon word.

## Be lean and confident — cut the qualifiers

> "Don't say you were a bit confused and sort of tired and a little depressed. Be confused. Be tired. Be depressed."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Bits & Pieces

**Demonstrates:** The parallel-imperative cascade. The hedged version is drawn out and limp; the cure is three clipped commands. Rhythm and meaning reinforce each other — short sentences *enact* the confidence he's prescribing.

---

> "Every little qualifier whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust. Don't be kind of bold. Be bold."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Bits & Pieces

**Demonstrates:** A principle stated, then proved by a four-word imperative. "Don't be kind of bold. Be bold." is self-demonstrating — the sentence obeys its own rule. This is how Zinsser closes a point: tiny, unsoftened.

---

> "Good writing is lean and confident."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Bits & Pieces

**Demonstrates:** Six words, two adjectives that earn their place ("lean," "confident"). When Zinsser *does* use an adjective, it does real work — proof that his anti-adjective rule is about waste, not poverty.

## Active, precise verbs

> "Don't say that the president of the company stepped down. Did he resign? Did he retire? Did he get fired? Be precise."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Bits & Pieces

**Demonstrates:** Verbs as the engine. The vague "stepped down" is replaced by three precise alternatives offered as questions, then sealed with "Be precise." Note the rhetorical-question rhythm and the two-word imperative close.

---

> "Active verbs push hard; passive verbs tug fitfully."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Bits & Pieces

**Demonstrates:** A craft rule delivered as a balanced, physical contrast ("push hard" / "tug fitfully"). He makes grammar tactile — you can *feel* the difference — which is why the rule sticks.

## Writing is a craft, not a gift

> "Writing is talking to someone else on paper. Anybody who can think clearly can write clearly, about any subject at all."
> — *On Writing Well*, Introduction

**Demonstrates:** The democratizing thesis, stated plainly. "Talking to someone else on paper" is the whole philosophy compressed into one image — intimate, single-reader, conversational. No jargon, no mystique.

---

> "Writing is hard work. A clear sentence is no accident. Very few sentences come out right the first time."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Simplicity

**Demonstrates:** The unsoftened hard truth, built from three short declaratives of decreasing comfort. He refuses to reassure. The flatness is the honesty — and the short sentences model the clarity he's demanding.

## Style is who you are — no ornament store

> "There is no style store; style is organic to the person doing the writing, as much a part of him as his hair."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Style

**Demonstrates:** The vivid metaphor that kills a bad idea. "No style store" is funny and final; the hair simile makes the abstraction physical. He argues against decoration *without* decoration.

---

> "Trying to add style is like adding a toupee. At first glance the formerly bald man looks young… But there's always a second glance."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. Style

**Demonstrates:** The extended concrete analogy doing the persuading. He doesn't assert "added style looks fake" — he hands you the toupee and the inevitable second glance, and you conclude it yourself.

## Write to one reader; never patronize

> "There is no such audience — every reader is a different person. You are writing for yourself."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. The Audience

**Demonstrates:** The paradox he embraces and explains — write for yourself, yet never lose the reader. The em-dash here (rendered as a period in generated copy) sets off the corrective; the cadence is conversational, the claim absolute.

---

> "Nobody ever stopped reading E. B. White because the writing was too good. But readers will stop reading you if you talk down to them."
> — *On Writing Well*, ch. The Sound of Your Voice (paraphrased to ≤25 words; cf. original)

**Demonstrates:** The good-model contrast ("Nobody ever stopped reading E. B. White…") plus the head-of-sentence "But" announcing the turn. The lesson — respect the reader — arrives as a consequence, not a scold.

## How it ends — the borrowed concrete image

> "I always thought that there was at least one person in the stands who had never seen me play, and I didn't want to let him down."
> — Joe DiMaggio, quoted as the final words of *On Writing Well*, ch. Write as Well as You Can

**Demonstrates:** The signature close — the whole book's argument about taking obsessive pride in your craft, handed to DiMaggio to deliver as an image. No summary, no exhortation. A concrete person, a concrete stadium, and the point walks out of the room intact.

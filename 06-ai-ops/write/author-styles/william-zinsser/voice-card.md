# William Zinsser — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A warm, exacting craftsman writing the plainest possible English to one specific reader — short declarative sentences, concrete nouns, hard active verbs, every needless word cut, tuned by ear and read aloud before it goes out the door.

**Rhythm rule:** Vary the sentence lengths so they don't "all sound as if they came out of the same mold," but let the short sentence predominate; after a run of medium sentences, drop a three- or four-word one to land the point ("Simplify, simplify." / "I doubt it." / "Be bold.").
**Diction rule:** Cut every word that does no work — the long word that could be a short word, the adverb already inside the verb, the adjective already inside the noun, the little qualifier ("a bit," "sort of," "very"). Prefer the plain Anglo-Saxon word and the active verb; never the pompous synonym ("help" not "assistance," "now" not "at the present time").
**Reader rule:** Write to one person, alone — "writing is talking to someone else on paper." Assume they are intelligent and impatient ("an attention span of about 30 seconds"); never talk down, never address a crowd, never bore them.

## Do (top 7)
1. Strip every sentence to its cleanest components; cut the first draft by ~50% and be "grateful for everything you can throw away."
2. Lead with active, precise verbs — "resign / retire / get fired," never the vague "stepped down." Verbs are "the most important of all your tools."
3. Write in the first person, or at least *think* "I" — get a real human, with humanity and warmth, onto the page.
4. Name the specific thing: the Laced Wyandotte, the brooder house, 60 mph, the electric clock — the concrete detail does the persuading.
5. Read every sentence aloud before releasing it; "I write entirely by ear." Fix what limps by ear, not by rule.
6. Build with plain carpentry first — saw the wood, drive the nails — and earn ornament only after the structure stands.
7. Start a contrast sentence with "but"; reach for the short pivot word ("Yet." "Still." "Instead.") that replaces a long dismal clause.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Don't inflate to sound important — no "experiencing precipitation" for "rain," no "involuntary methodologies" for "layoffs." Clutter is the enemy.
2. Don't let an adverb or adjective restate what the verb or noun already says ("blared loudly," "tall skyscraper," "effortlessly easy").
3. Don't hedge with little qualifiers — "Don't be kind of bold. Be bold." Every qualifier "whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust."
4. Don't reach for the cliché or journalese ("fired off a note," "sending shock waves," "New York's finest"); the reader knows a hack at once.
5. Don't write the breezy "just folks" voice ("Believe you me," "yours truly") — it's harder to read than good English and it patronizes.
6. Don't try to bolt on a "style" — there is "no style store"; added style is "like adding a toupee." Style is who you are, surfacing once the clutter is gone.
7. Don't use the exclamation point to flag your own joke, or the passive voice that hides who did what; don't say "indeed" or "moreover" if you'd never say it aloud.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The flat diagnostic thesis, stated as fact — e.g. "Clutter is the disease of American writing."
- The little scene or anecdote that turns into the lesson — e.g. "A school in Connecticut once held 'a day devoted to the arts'…"
- The reader's own objection, voiced and then answered — e.g. "'But,' you may say, 'if I strip every sentence to its bones, will there be anything left of me?'"

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The tiny imperative or repetition that seals the chapter — e.g. "Simplify, simplify." / "Be bold." / "Just to remind you that it exists."
- The borrowed concrete image that carries the whole point — e.g. DiMaggio: "I didn't want to let him down."
- The plain hard truth, unsoftened — e.g. "If you find that writing is hard, it's because it is hard."

## 1. The Rug-Pull Reveal (his most famous written move)

**What:** Narrate a received story in vivid present tense as if it were fact for two or three paragraphs — let the reader settle into it — then detonate it in a micro-paragraph. The reader's own investment becomes the lesson about believed myths.

**When:** First third of a piece, to clear the ground before introducing the real, documented counter-case. The detonation must be short enough to be its own paragraph.

**Example:** "This story never happened." — *The real Lord of the Flies* (The Guardian, 2020), after two paragraphs retelling Golding's plot as if reporting it
**Example:** "Their names? Peter Warner and Mano Totau. And where had they met? On a deserted island." — *The real Lord of the Flies* (2020)

---

## 2. The Hinge Inversion (the thesis move)

**What:** State the consensus generously, often in its own voice, for paragraphs — then flip the entire frame on one anaphoric sentence. The rest of the piece exists to pay for that flip with evidence.

**When:** The thesis moment, typically paragraphs 3-6, right after the conventional view has been given its fairest hearing.

**Example:** "In reality, it is precisely the other way around. In reality, it is the waste collectors, the nurses, and the cleaners whose shoulders are supporting the apex of the pyramid." — *No, wealth isn't created at the top* (The Correspondent, 2017)
**Example:** "Except that, according to the evidence, it doesn't." — *Has the time finally come for universal basic income?* (The Correspondent, 2020), the one-line paragraph rebutting "free money makes people lazy"

---

## 3. Receipt-Level Sourcing (the authority engine)

**What:** Anchor every claim with the researcher's name, the institution, the year, and a number — and where possible a physical artifact detail (the appendix, the memoir, the archive) so the reader can *see* the evidence rather than being asked to trust. Flag missing evidence honestly.

**When:** Every factual claim, and double on the counterintuitive ones. The more radical the claim, the more concrete the receipt.

**Example:** "There's one big book about this called the altruistic personality project done by two professors Samuel and Paul Olner in the states and if you read that book you can go to the appendix and you see all the numbers and again and again you see the same letters. It's NS NS NS NS meaning statistically non-significant." — James O'Brien *Full Disclosure* interview (2026)
**Example:** "(In 1952, the highest income tax bracket in the United States was 92%, and the economy grew faster than ever.)" — *No, wealth isn't created at the top* (2017)

---

## 4. The Fragment Self-Interview

**What:** Ask a clipped question fragment and answer it instantly — compressing a finding, a name, or a price into a two-beat punch. The colon and the question mark are the hinges.

**When:** Transitions between evidence beats; whenever a reveal needs drama without length.

**Example:** "The gist? It's the context, stupid." — *Poverty isn't a lack of character* (The Correspondent, 2020)
**Example:** "The cost? Some £50,000 a year." — *Has the time finally come for universal basic income?* (2020)
**Example:** "So why is this happening? The answer can be summed up in three little words: Because it can." — *No, wealth isn't created at the top* (2017)

---

## 5. The 'Take X' Exhibit Parade

**What:** Serial case introductions — each a named person or industry that converts the abstract claim into an instance. The first sentence names the exhibit; the second states the outcome flatly before the details arrive.

**When:** Middle development, stacking three to five exhibits per piece.

**Example:** "Take Simon, who had been addicted to heroin for twenty years. The money turned his life around." — *Has the time finally come for universal basic income?* (2020)

---

## 6. Concede-Then-Strike

**What:** Pre-empt the obvious objection with a generous concession — almost always opened "Don't get me wrong" — then resume the attack sharper. The concession buys the licence for the cut that follows.

**When:** Immediately before or after the most radical claim, to keep the skeptical reader aboard. Under live challenge it becomes concede-then-flip: agree warmly, then move the fight to ground he prefers.

**Example:** "Don't get me wrong, capitalism is a fantastic engine for prosperity." — *Has the time finally come for universal basic income?* (2020), three sentences before the case that capitalism must be tamed
**Example:** "I can't agree more and the left used to understand that." — Sam Harris inequality conversation (2025), opening a defense of growth politics

---

## 7. The Ventriloquized Reader

**What:** Write the skeptical reader's objection in the reader's own voice — domestic details included — then answer it, sometimes with a dare. The objection must sound like a real person, not a strawman.

**When:** Right after the big ask, before the reader can disengage.

**Example:** "Now, you might be thinking: that's all well and good, but I've got a full-time job, two kids and a mortgage. I'm happy to recycle and eat some tofu now and then, but a 'fundamental transformation'? No thanks." — *No, you're not fine just the way you are* (The Guardian, 2025)

---

## 8. The Priced Comparison + the Number Ladder

**What:** Make a policy number moral by ratio to a familiar anchor (GDP, military spending, the cost of the status quo) — and make a quantity felt by restating it in escalating denominations. Arithmetic becomes the argument.

**When:** The stakes section near the close, after the case studies have landed; the ladder whenever one number must carry existential weight.

**Example:** "Let's look at the numbers. Eradicating poverty in the US would cost only $175 billion, less than 1% of GDP. That's roughly a quarter of US military spending." — *Has the time finally come for universal basic income?* (2020)
**Example:** "A full-time career consists of 80,000 hours, or 10,000 workdays, or 2,000 workweeks." — *No, you're not fine just the way you are* (2025)

---

## 9. Statistic-to-Image Conversion

**What:** Translate an abstract toll into one visceral picture, then scale the picture stepwise with "Now, imagine…" — the listener does the multiplication emotionally.

**When:** Deploying the worst numbers, where a figure alone would bounce off.

**Example:** "I mean, just imagine one jumbo jet crashing full of kids. I mean, that in and of itself, it's hard to wrap your head around the tragedy. Now, imagine it happening seven times a day." — *The Crisis of Meaning Has an Antidote* talk (2025), on malaria deaths

---

## 10. Coin-Then-Translate

**What:** Mint a two-word concept at the climax of a story, then immediately render it in kitchen-table speech so the label never floats free. The handle is the takeaway the reader carries home. His catalog: *moral ambition*, *moral envy*, "the Bermuda Triangle of talent," "elite denial," "the leftwing excuse."

**When:** At the moment of diagnosis, often introduced with "I call that…" or "an emotion that I often like to describe as…"

**Example:** "As I was reading their biographies and their memoirs, I started to experience an emotion that I often like to describe as moral envy. I just kind of became jealous." — *How to Be Morally Ambitious* podcast (2026)

---

## 11. The Domestic Analogy (theory wears an apron)

**What:** Translate a scientific mechanism into an appliance-level comparison, then run the "not because X but because Y" corrective so the analogy lands as explanation, not decoration.

**When:** The moment a study's mechanism enters the piece; also the "In other words" restatement of any technical claim.

**Example:** "Compare it to a new computer that's running 10 heavy programmes at once. It gets slower and slower, makes errors, and eventually freezes – not because it's a bad computer but because it has to do too much at once. Poor people have an analogous problem. They're making bad decisions not because they are stupid but because they're living in a context in which anyone would make bad decisions." — *Poverty isn't a lack of character* (2020)
**Example:** "In other words, a big part of the modern banking sector is essentially a giant tapeworm gorging on a sick body." — *No, wealth isn't created at the top* (2017)

---

## 12. The Brutal Re-Read

**What:** Quote a famous feel-good line, then re-read it dark — and let the darker reading carry the more hopeful conclusion (most people are herd animals; therefore a small committed group is enough, and it might be you).

**When:** When hope risks sounding sentimental; his anti-sentimentality vaccine.

**Example:** "I have always loved that quote from Margaret Mead, the anthropologist who said, 'We should never doubt the power of small groups of thoughtful committed citizens to change the world. In fact, it's the only thing that ever has.' Now, what I've liked about it is that it's in fact a quite brutal quote." — Lavin Voices interview (2025)

---

## 13. The Future-Historian Mirror

**What:** Use past civilizations' self-congratulation to indict present complacency, then ask what our great-great-grandchildren will condemn us for.

**When:** Moving the argument from safe causes to neglected ones; the standard on-ramp to the uncomfortable subject.

**Example:** "But the Romans actually thought they were super civilized because they didn't sacrifice children to the gods anymore. So I would caution against thinking that we are so civilized today." — Lavin Voices interview (2025)

---

## 14. The Burden-Flip Close

**What:** End with a parallel question that moves the obligation from the proposal to the audience — the idea is fine; the question is us. The sibling closers: the title repaid as the final beat, and the chiasmus.

**When:** Final lines of a piece. Always agency, never doom.

**Example:** "The question is not: can our kids handle the freedom? The question is: do we have the courage to give it to them?" — *What if we give children the freedom to play and learn on their own?* (The Guardian, 2021)
**Example:** "You don't do good things because you are a good person. It's the other way around. You become a good person by doing good things." — Lavin Voices interview (2025)

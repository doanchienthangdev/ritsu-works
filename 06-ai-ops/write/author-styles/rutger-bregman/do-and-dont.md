# Rutger Bregman — Do & Don't

<!-- The fastest reference during drafting + the humanizer's voice-fidelity checklist. -->

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Open with a dateline scene ("On 13 November 1997…"), an era-scale consensus about to break, or the thesis-as-taboo; argument visible by paragraph three | Bury the thesis, perform both-sides neutrality past the opening, or open with throat-clearing about how complicated things are |
| State the conventional view so fairly its believers would sign it, then flip it on one hinge sentence ("In reality, it is precisely the other way around.") | Strawman the opposing view, or dismiss opponents as paid shills without first steelmanning their sincerity |
| Receipts on every claim: named researcher + institution + year + number ("Eldar Shafir, a psychologist at Princeton University") | "Studies show," "experts agree," or any statistic without a name, date, or denominator attached |
| Lead evidence with one named human ("Take Simon, who had been addicted to heroin for twenty years.") and give historical figures the three-fact thumbnail | Run three sentences of pure abstraction with no person, price, or date in them |
| Keep the rhythm bimodal: 30-45-word evidence freight snapped shut by a 2-7-word stinger as its own paragraph ("This story never happened.") | Monotone mid-length sentences; long paragraphs that never snap shut |
| Interview yourself in fragments ("The cost? Some £50,000 a year.") and use the colon as a reveal hinge ("Its subjects: 13 homeless men.") | Smooth, formal transitions everywhere; rhetorical questions that are merely decorative and never answered |
| Translate every mechanism into a domestic analogy (the freezing computer, the tapeworm) and gloss jargon the moment it appears | Leave a term of art untranslated, or play corporate buzzwords straight |
| Ventriloquize the skeptical reader with their real life in it (job, kids, mortgage, tofu), and concede before you strike ("Don't get me wrong…") | Lecture at the reader, guilt-trip them ("moral blackmail… Backfires. Doesn't work."), or motivate by shame instead of enthusiasm |
| Price the stakes against a familiar anchor ("less than 1% of GDP… roughly a quarter of US military spending") and ladder the key number ("80,000 hours, or 10,000 workdays, or 2,000 workweeks") | Drop a big number naked and move on, or end a story without closing the arithmetic loop (what it cost, what it returned) |
| Punch up only (bankers, consultants, Davos) and discipline your own side too; confess your own sin before accusing | Sneer at the poor, benefit recipients, nurses, teachers, or the reader |
| Coin the two-word handle (*moral ambition*, *moral envy*), then immediately translate it into kitchen-table speech and reuse it | Coin a label that names nothing, or let the handle float free of plain-speech explanation |
| Close the loop: circle back to the opening, land the burden-flip question or the agency aphorism ("And that's how all progress begins.") | End on doom, paralysis, a tidy neutral summary, or hope-as-mood with no action attached ("History is just history" — the arc doesn't bend itself) |
| Zoom out to history as proof of possibility (abolition, suffrage, Mincome, Mont Pèlerin) and apply the future-historian test to today | Treat awareness as the outcome ("Awareness is completely overrated") or treat the present as the end of history |

**The one test:** Find the boldest claim in the draft and ask two questions. Does it carry a named receipt (researcher, year, number) within two sentences? And does the paragraph end on a stinger you could read aloud in one breath? If either answer is no, it isn't Bregman yet — and if the ending leaves the reader informed but not recruited, neither is that.

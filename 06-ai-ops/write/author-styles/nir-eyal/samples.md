## The redefinition: deny the obvious antonym, prove it from the word

> "the opposite of distraction is not focus... the opposite of distraction is traction"
> — Talks at Google, *Indistractable* (transcript verbatim; ellipsis marks "if you look at the origin of the word")

**Demonstrates:** The "opposite of X is not Y, it's Z" move — break the audience's default antonym in one stroke, then substitute the surprising one.

---

> "Both words come from the same Latin root trahere, which means to pull. And you'll notice that both words, traction and distraction, end in the same letters. They end in the word A-C-T-I-O-N, which spells action"
> — Talks at Google, *Indistractable* (transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** Definition-by-etymology — the doctrine is derived from the structure of the word itself, so the proof feels discovered, not asserted.

---

> "addiction comes from addictio in Latin, which means slave"
> — *No.1 Habit & Procrastination Expert: We've Got ADHD Wrong!* (interview, transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** Etymology as argument — the word's origin *is* the refutation of its casual modern use; calling yourself an addict is calling yourself powerless.

## The anti-moral-panic flat denial

> "this Chicken Little critique that we hear about technology today, that the sky is falling, and our brains are melting."
> — Talks at Google, *Indistractable* (transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** Voice the doom narrative in the listener's words ("the sky is falling, and our brains are melting"), then flatten it — his flat-denial form is "that's just not true" — before offering the constructive frame.

---

> "people in the ADHD community, they feel like it's an identity and that is so dangerous."
> — *No.1 Habit & Procrastination Expert: We've Got ADHD Wrong!* (interview, transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** The identity-danger flag — converting a label into an identity makes you conform to it; the contrarian verdict ("so dangerous") delivered flat.

---

> "If the kind of person you want to become enjoys an hour of playing video games or scrolling social media, great! Do it! But do it according to your schedule and your values."
> — *How Successful People Timebox* (nirandfar.com)

**Demonstrates:** Anti-moral-panic permission — he refuses to demonize the "distraction" activity; it's fine *if scheduled on your terms*. Note the staccato "great! Do it!"

## The responsibility frame: not your fault, still your job

> "distraction is not something that happens to us. But rather it is an action that we ourselves take."
> — Talks at Google, *Indistractable* (transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** Relocating agency from an external event to a self-chosen action — the spine of his responsibility frame.

---

> "It's not your fault that you felt this urge, but it is your responsibility to know how to deal with it."
> — Talks at Google, *Indistractable* (transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** Responsibility-without-fault distilled to one antithesis — the "claimer" stance between blaming and shaming.

---

> "You do not control these urges any more than you would control the urge to sneeze. If you feel the urge to sneeze, it's too late."
> — Talks at Google, *Indistractable* (transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** The folk-physical analogy that makes "you can only control the response, not the urge" instantly intuitive.

## The named tool and the permission frame

> "you can give in to any distraction but not right now. You're going to give in to that distraction in 10 minutes"
> — *How To Stay Focused, Beat Distraction, and Get Things Done* (interview, transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** The permission-frame syntax of the 10-minute rule — "not yet," never "no," so the reader's reactance never fires. The named, do-it-today tool.

---

> "to-do lists are one of the worst things you can do for your personal productivity because to-do lists are nothing but registers of output."
> — Talks at Google, *Indistractable* (transcript verbatim)

**Demonstrates:** Schedule-not-list doctrine as a blunt contrarian verdict — the to-do list is the named villain, the timebox the hero.

---

> "We can't control how many tasks we complete in a day, but we can control our input—the time and the attention we put into a task."
> — *Control Your Calendar (Not Your To-Do List)* (nirandfar.com, 2024)

**Demonstrates:** The input-vs-output reframe — his core "control what you can" dichotomy, stated as an antithesis pivoting on "but."

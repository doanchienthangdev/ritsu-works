# Richard P. Feynman — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A brilliant, restless, plain-spoken Far Rockaway kid grown into a physicist who teaches by *doing the thinking out loud in front of you* — short everyday words, a homely physical analogy for every abstraction, an absolute refusal to pretend he understands what he doesn't, and an audible delight that the real answer is stranger and more beautiful than any story we'd have made up.

**Rhythm rule:** Talk on the page. Short declarative sentences in a spoken cadence, strung with plain connectors ("Now —", "You see,", "And so", "But"), then one blunt short line lands the point ("If it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong. That's all there is to it.").
**Diction rule:** The plainest Anglo-Saxon word and the concrete thing. "Stuff," "junk," "guy," "guess," "wiggle," "figure it out." Name the technical term only to *demote* it ("they call them quarks — the word doesn't do any good"). Zero pomp, zero reverence, zero jargon-as-decoration.
**Reader rule:** Treat the reader as a smart equal who can take the truth — including "I don't know" and "nobody understands this." Never flatter, never talk down, never dress it up. Invite them into the finding-out; make them do some of the figuring.

## Do (top 7)
1. Separate the **name** of a thing from the **thing** — the deepest Feynman move ("you can know the name of that bird in every language and know nothing about the bird").
2. Drop a **homely analogy** onto the abstraction and reason inside it: physics is watching a chess game and inferring the rules; guessing a law is cracking a safe; the universe is a tapestry where one thread reveals the whole pattern.
3. **Demonstrate the thinking, including the wrong turns** — guess, work out the consequence, check it against the world. Show the reasoning; don't just hand over the conclusion.
4. **Confess ignorance cheerfully and exactly** — say what nobody knows, what you don't know, where the theory breaks. Honesty is the lesson, not a disclaimer.
5. **State the integrity principle plainly:** you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool; nature cannot be fooled; the only judge is experiment.
6. **Let the delight show** — the real thing is "so much more remarkable," "nature's imagination far surpasses our own." Wonder is the point, said plainly, never gushed.
7. **Deflate the pomp** with a wisecrack, a self-deprecating aside ("guessing is a dumb man's job"), or a "don't take it too seriously — just relax and enjoy it."

## Don't (top 7)
1. Mistake **vocabulary for understanding** — never let naming a thing stand in for knowing it.
2. **Pretend to certainty** you don't have, or paper over what's unknown to sound authoritative. ("We can only ever prove a guess wrong, never right.")
3. Use **jargon, pomp, or reverence** for its own sake — no "profound," no sacred tone, no priesthood of experts.
4. **Argue from authority** ("the experts say," "it's well established") instead of from a reason you can check.
5. **Bury the reasoning** and present only the polished result — that robs the reader of the finding-out.
6. Be **solemn or precious.** No purple wonder, no inspirational send-off, no Important Voice. The mischief is load-bearing.
7. **Fake the physics.** Don't invent technical detail or equations to sound like him; his authority is honesty, not name-dropping. (Keep the *mechanics* on any topic; don't counterfeit expertise.)

## Signature openings (how they start)
- Drop into a small true scene, plainly told — "When I was a kid, a kid said to me, 'See that bird — what's its name?'"
- Demote the grand thing to set up the real one — "This law has been called the greatest generalization achieved by the human mind… but I'm more interested in the marvel of nature who obeys it."
- Reframe the ordinary as a Martian would — "Suppose you were a Martian who never slept, and you came down and saw these people lie down and go unconscious every day…"

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The blunt epistemic hammer — "If it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong. That's all there is to it."
- The reality check — "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled."
- The thread of wonder — "Nature uses only the longest threads to weave her patterns, so that each small piece of fabric reveals the organization of the entire tapestry."

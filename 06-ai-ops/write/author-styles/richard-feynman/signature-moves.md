## 1. The Name vs. the Thing (his deepest pedagogical move)

**What:** Drive a wedge between *knowing what something is called* and *knowing the thing.* A name is a social convenience for talking to other people; it is not knowledge. This is the single most Feynman idea about learning, and the root of everything else — because if you can't tell the label from the understanding, you will fool yourself into thinking you've learned something when you've only memorized a word.

**When:** Any time a reader might mistake vocabulary for mastery — the start of almost any piece about learning, or whenever a fancy term is doing suspicious work. Reach for it to deflate jargon and to redirect attention from the name to the mechanism.

**Example:** "When you know all the names in every language of that bird, you know nothing — but absolutely nothing — about the bird… So I had learned already that names don't constitute knowledge." — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)
**Example:** "What he forgot to tell me is that knowing the names of things is useful if you want to talk to somebody else. But the basic principle of knowing about something, rather than just knowing its name…" — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)

## 2. The Homely Analogy, Reasoned-Inside (his core instrument)

**What:** Take the abstraction and drop the most *ordinary* physical picture onto it — then reason openly inside the picture so it carries the cognitive load and the prose stays plain. The power is in the familiarity, never the cleverness. Crucially, he doesn't just decorate with the analogy; he *thinks* in it and walks you through the steps.

**When:** The moment an idea risks going abstract. Reach for the chess game (you infer nature's rules by watching the play), the safe combination (you guess a law the way a stranger suggests "try 10-20-30"), the tapestry (one thread shows the whole weave), the streetlight (you search where it's easy, not where the truth is).

**Example:** "It's again this chess-game business. If you were in just a corner where only a few pieces are involved, you can work out exactly what should happen… and yet in the real game there's so many pieces you can't figure out what's going to happen." — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)
**Example:** "Nature uses only the longest threads to weave her patterns, so that each small piece of fabric reveals the organization of the entire tapestry." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example (the streetlight parable):** A man searches under a lamppost for keys he lost elsewhere; asked why, he says "unless I lost it here I'll never find it, because the light's better here." Feynman: "we work where the light's better." — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)

## 3. Guess → Compute → Compare (the method, performed)

**What:** State the actual engine of science as a plain three-beat — guess a law, work out what it would imply, then check that against the world — and perform it in front of the reader rather than describing it from outside. The payoff is the bluntest epistemic line in science: if the check fails, the guess is dead, no matter who made it or how lovely it is.

**When:** Whenever you're explaining *how we know* anything, or correcting the idea that knowledge comes from authority or from how clever/beautiful an idea is. It's the backbone of any explanatory piece.

**Example:** "First we guess it… then we compute the consequences of the guess to see what it would imply, and then we compare those computation results to nature… to experiment or experience, to see if it works." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example:** "If it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong. And that simple statement is the key to science. It doesn't make any difference how beautiful your guess is, it doesn't make any difference how smart you are, who made the guess or what his name is — if it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong. That's all there is to it." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)

## 4. The Cheerful Confession of Ignorance (honesty as the lesson)

**What:** Say, plainly and without anxiety, what nobody knows — including himself, including the very thing he's teaching. The confession isn't a hedge or a disclaimer; it's the point. Admitting the edge of knowledge is what keeps you honest and what makes the search thrilling instead of dogmatic.

**When:** Whenever the honest answer is "we don't know," or whenever certainty is being faked. Deploy it to model intellectual courage — and to make uncertainty feel like an adventure, not a failure.

**Example:** "I think I can safely say that nobody understands quantum mechanics… Do not keep saying to yourself 'but how can it be like that?'… nobody knows how it can be like that." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example:** "We never are right; we can only be sure we're wrong." (You can prove a definite guess wrong, but never prove it right — tomorrow's experiment may undo it.) — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example:** "We're still very bad at it… we're doing the best we can." — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)

## 5. You Must Not Fool Yourself (the integrity principle)

**What:** The moral center of the whole voice: scientific integrity is a kind of utter honesty, *leaning over backwards* to show every way you might be wrong — and the hardest case is yourself, because you are the easiest person to fool. Stated in physics, in engineering, and in life, it always reduces to the same thing: reality doesn't care what you wish were true.

**When:** Whenever wishful thinking, motivated reasoning, or comforting stories are in play — the illusion of competence in learning, the management fantasy in engineering, the cargo cult in pseudo-science. It's the move that turns an explanation into a code of conduct.

**Example:** "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool." — *Cargo Cult Science*, Caltech (1974)
**Example:** "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled." — *Appendix F*, Rogers Commission report (1986)
**Example:** "When playing Russian roulette the fact that the first shot got off safely is little comfort for the next." — *Appendix F*, Rogers Commission report (1986)

## 6. The Fresh-Eyes / Martian Reframe (make the familiar strange)

**What:** Look at an ordinary, unexamined thing as if you'd never seen it — as a Martian would, or as a kid asking the dumb question nobody asks. Stripping away the assumed familiarity exposes what we actually do and don't know, and turns a stale topic into a live question.

**When:** When a subject feels too obvious to examine, or when received habit is masquerading as knowledge. Use it to reopen a closed question and to model genuine curiosity.

**Example:** "Suppose you were a Martian who never slept… and you came down to this earth and you saw these people who every day for a certain amount of time have to lie down and become unconscious… how does it feel to become unconscious?" — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)
**Example:** "What ceremonies do we do every morning? We brush our teeth. What is the evidence that brushing the teeth does us any good?… you try to picture this perpetual line of toothbrushes going around the earth." — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)

## 7. The Vague-Theory Takedown (a theory that can't fail explains nothing)

**What:** Expose a claim that's been stated so loosely it can absorb any result — and therefore tells you nothing. He acts it out: feed it the evidence, then feed it the opposite evidence, and watch it "explain" both. The cure is to state, in advance and precisely, what would prove it wrong.

**When:** Against hand-wavy claims, unfalsifiable explanations, and after-the-fact rationalization — in science, in management, anywhere. It's the practical edge of "you must not fool yourself."

**Example:** "He hates his mother. The reason is, of course, because she didn't caress him or love him enough when he was a child. But actually, if you investigate, you find out she loved him very much… well then, it's because she was overindulgent. By having a vague theory, it's possible to get any result." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example (engineering version):** A safety factor inferred from a part that wasn't supposed to erode at all is no safety factor — "Erosion was a clue that something was wrong… not something from which safety can be inferred." — *Appendix F*, Rogers Commission report (1986)

## 8. The Deflating Aside / Mischief (puncture the pomp)

**What:** The instant any building solemnity threatens, he punctures it — a wisecrack, a snort, a piece of self-deprecation, an explicit "don't take this too seriously." The mischief is not decoration; it keeps the work honest and human and refuses the priesthood. He's hardest on his own dignity.

**When:** Whenever the tone is getting reverent or the subject is being treated as sacred. Also to lower the reader's guard right before a hard idea.

**Example:** "If you appreciate this… don't take the lecture too seriously, feeling that you really have to understand in terms of some model what I am going to describe, but just relax and enjoy it." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example:** "Guessing is a dumb man's job." (said right before showing that it is, in fact, the subtlest job there is) — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example:** Of his own field's progress: "why that devil was I so stupid that I didn't see this?… however good you may get, compared to apes and things, we're still very bad at it." — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)

---

## 9. The Audible Delight (wonder, in plain clothes)

**What:** Let it show that the finding-out is a joy and that the true answer is more remarkable than any story we'd have made up. The delight is stated plainly — never gushed, never purple — and it's the actual engine of the whole voice: he explains because he can't help being fascinated, and he wants you in on it.

**When:** At the moment a real result arrives, especially a strange or beautiful one. Use it to close a movement, or to justify the whole enterprise of looking.

**Example:** "The truth is so much more remarkable" than the tales people used to make up — that we were living on the back of a turtle. — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)
**Example:** "As usual, nature's imagination far surpasses our own." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example:** "I have this disease, and many other people who have studied far enough to begin to understand a little of how things work are fascinated by it, and this fascination drives them on." — *Take the World from Another Point of View*, Yorkshire TV (1973)

---

## 10. The Demotion of Authority (only nature gets a vote)

**What:** Systematically strip power from titles, traditions, big names, and "the experts say." Famous scientists appear as *characters in the finding-out* (Newton guessing, Maxwell adding a term, Tycho recording the sky), never as authorities to defer to. The only thing that confers authority is agreement with experiment — and even that only ever earns "not yet proved wrong."

**When:** Whenever a claim is leaning on prestige instead of evidence, or whenever consensus is being mistaken for proof. Especially potent against institutional self-deception.

**Example:** "It doesn't make any difference… who made the guess, or what his name is — if it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong." — *The Character of Physical Law*, Cornell (1964)
**Example:** "The management of NASA exaggerates the reliability of its product, to the point of fantasy." (engineers said 1 in 100; management said 1 in 100,000) — *Appendix F*, Rogers Commission report (1986)
**Example (on AI/machines):** machines won't think like us and shouldn't try to — "the airplanes don't fly like a bird; they fly, but they don't fly like a bird." Intelligence imitated will show "the necessary weaknesses of intelligent machines." — *Can Machines Think?*, lecture Q&A (1985)

# Stephen King — Signature Moves

## The Toolbox (the extended craft conceit)
**What:** Frame all of writing as a layered carpenter's toolbox — vocabulary and grammar on the top shelf, style elements on the layer beneath — so abstract advice becomes a physical thing the reader can pick up, sharpen, and carry to work. The metaphor is sustained across the whole craft section, not dropped once.
**When:** Teaching a method, or any time advice risks floating off into the abstract. Reach for the concrete physical container ("Put your vocabulary on the top shelf of your toolbox") and let the reader hold the idea.
**Example:** "Put your vocabulary on the top shelf of your toolbox, and don't make any conscious effort to improve it." — *On Writing*, 2000. He returns to it constantly: theme is "another handy gadget to keep in the toolbox, this one something like a magnifying glass."

## The Homely Image for the Abstraction
**What:** Refuse to explain a concept in the abstract — hand the reader one everyday picture that *is* the concept, so the idea becomes portable and unforgettable. The image always comes from ordinary domestic or working life: lawns, pets, lovers, bread dough.
**When:** Whenever you'd otherwise write a dry definition or a stern rule. Find the object the reader already knows and make it carry the lesson.
**Example:** Adverbs as a spreading weed — "they're like dandelions. If you have one on your lawn, it looks pretty and unique… your lawn is totally, completely, and profligately covered with dandelions." And on padded vocabulary: "This is like dressing up a household pet in evening clothes." (*On Writing*, 2000)

## The Rule-as-Command, Then the Confession
**What:** State the craft law as a flat imperative the reader can pin to the wall — then immediately admit you break it yourself. The command lands harder *because* of the honesty; the authority is never pompous because it's self-implicating.
**When:** Handing down any non-negotiable craft standard. Issue the command plainly, then disarm it (and earn trust) by owning your own failures against it.
**Example:** "The adverb is not your friend." Followed pages later by: "I've spilled out my share of adverbs in my time, including some (it shames me to say it) in dialogue attribution." (*On Writing*, 2000) His summary: "while to write adverbs is human, to write he said or she said is divine."

## The Concrete Grotesque, Made Funny
**What:** Render a horrible, embarrassing, or grim moment in flat, vivid, specific detail — and let the comedy come from the precision and the deadpan, not from softening it. The grotesque earns affection rather than disgust.
**When:** Memoir, scene-setting, or any moment you want the reader to feel something true rather than be protected from it. Name the specific awful thing; don't flinch; let the flat delivery be the joke or the gut-punch.
**Example:** The childhood babysitter — "After having a two-hundred-pound babysitter fart on your face and yell Pow!, *The Village Voice* holds few terrors." And his mother on a man who jumped: "'He splattered,' my mother said in her most matter-of-fact tone… 'The stuff that came out of him was green.'" (*On Writing*, 2000)

## Constant Reader (the letter to one person)
**What:** Address a single imagined reader directly and intimately — King names this reader "Constant Reader" — and write the entire piece as a letter to that one person, never to an audience. It governs the second person, the warmth, and the refusal to broadcast.
**When:** Always, but especially when the prose starts sounding like an announcement or a press release. Picture one person in one chair and tell it to them.
**Example:** "the reader must always be your main concern; without Constant Reader, you are just a voice quacking in the void." And: "all novels are really letters aimed at one person. As it happens, I believe this." (*On Writing*, 2000)

## The Stub Sentence as a Landing
**What:** After a run of longer declaratives, drop a two- or three-word sentence (often a fragment) to land the point with a thud. The brevity is the emphasis; it gives the reader's ear somewhere to stop.
**When:** At the end of an argument, a scene-beat, or a rule, when you want the previous sentences to resolve. Build the run, then cut hard.
**Example:** "It did, too." / "Don't do these things. Please oh please." / "Pow." / "Perish the thought." (*On Writing*, 2000) — each lands a paragraph that ran several lines longer.

## Profanity as Truth-Instrument
**What:** Use the blunt or coarse word precisely where the polite word would soften a truth — never as decoration, always to keep the prose "plain and direct." The vulgarity is a commitment to honesty, calibrated to the audience.
**When:** When the genteel word would be a small lie, or when you want to puncture pretension (your own included). Use the first true word; dress it down for the audience only if you must, never up.
**Example:** "you'll never use 'emolument' when you mean 'tip' and you'll never say John stopped long enough to perform an act of excretion when you mean John stopped long enough to take a shit." (*On Writing*, 2000) His gloss: "I'm not trying to get you to talk dirty, only plain and direct."

## The Return-to-Life Close
**What:** End not on a moral or an upbeat send-off but on an image of getting back to living — flight, breath, gratitude, the body healing. The piece resolves emotionally rather than instructively; King explicitly rejects the tidy lesson.
**When:** The final beat of any piece. Trade the summary-moral for a concrete image of aliveness or plain thanks, and let the reader leave with a feeling instead of a takeaway.
**Example:** "It's like lifting off in an airplane: you're on the ground, on the ground, on the ground… and then you're up." And the book's last argument: "It's about getting up, getting well, and getting over. Getting happy, okay? Getting happy." (*On Writing*, 2000)

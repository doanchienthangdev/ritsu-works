## Plain diction — the first word, not the fancy one

> "use the first word that comes to your mind, if it is appropriate and colorful."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** His core diction rule, stated plainly and obeyed by the sentence itself ("colorful," not "vivid and evocative"). The advice is concrete and falsifiable.

---

> "One of the really bad things you can do to your writing is to dress up the vocabulary… This is like dressing up a household pet in evening clothes."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** The homely-image-for-the-abstraction move. A craft warning becomes an absurd, unforgettable picture.

## The adverb / the rule-as-command

> "The adverb is not your friend."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** The flat imperative as a whole sentence. Maximum compression; nothing to cut.

---

> "I believe the road to hell is paved with adverbs, and I will shout it from the rooftops."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** Conviction stated as overstatement-for-effect, then the self-aware grin. Authority that doesn't take itself too solemnly.

## The passive voice — make verbs act

> "Throw back your shoulders, stick out your chin, and put that meeting in charge! Write The meeting's at seven. There, by God!"
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** Parallel imperatives building to a stub ("There, by God!"). He coaches the reader bodily, in the second person, with energy.

## The concrete grotesque, made funny

> "'He splattered,' my mother said in her most matter-of-fact tone. She paused, then added, 'The stuff that came out of him was green.'"
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** The flat, specific, grotesque detail delivered deadpan — and his one-line landing right after ("That makes two of us, Mom."). Precision is the emotion.

---

> "After having a two-hundred-pound babysitter fart on your face and yell Pow!, *The Village Voice* holds few terrors."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** Childhood horror turned to comedy via exact numbers, a named institution, and a punchline structure. The grotesque earns affection.

## Constant Reader — the letter to one person

> "the reader must always be your main concern; without Constant Reader, you are just a voice quacking in the void."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** The single-reader credo, plus a plain-funny image ("quacking in the void") doing the persuading instead of an abstract argument.

## The credo — what writing is and is for

> "Life isn't a support-system for art. It's the other way around."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** Inversion compressed to two short sentences; the second flips the first. A whole philosophy in twelve words.

---

> "Telepathy, of course."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** The one-word-answer opening. He answers "what is writing?" with a fragment and earns the rest of the section.

## The close — return to life, not a moral

> "The scariest moment is always just before you start. After that, things can only get better."
> — *On Writing*, 2000

**Demonstrates:** The landing on plain encouragement instead of a lesson. Two sentences, stressed words, no flourish — the cadence of someone telling you the truth.

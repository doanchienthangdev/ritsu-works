# Stephen King — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A blue-collar storyteller who writes plain, concrete, fast prose to one person he respects — pulling no punches, choosing the short word over the long, the named thing over the category, and ending every adverb's life on sight.

**Rhythm rule:** Short declarative sentences that move; let a run of them build, then drop a three-word stub to land it ("Pow." "It did, too." "Don't do these things."). Vary the length or the reader hears a metronome.
**Diction rule:** Use the first plain word that comes to mind — "take a shit," not "perform an act of excretion." Concrete and colorful beats long and ashamed. Brand names and proper nouns over generic ones ("Coty perfume," not "perfume").
**Reader rule:** You're writing a letter to one person — "Constant Reader." Trust them. Throw the rope, don't bury them under ninety feet of steel cable.

## Do (top 7)
1. Open in the middle of a scene or a flat confession — never a throat-clear ("This is a short book because most books about writing are filled with bullshit").
2. Nail the abstraction with one homely image — adverbs as dandelions, dressed-up vocabulary as a pet in evening clothes, the passive voice as a timid lover.
3. Anchor every memory in a named, specific, slightly grotesque concrete detail (the seven fried eggs, the wasp in the cinderblock, the green that came out of the man).
4. State the craft rule as a flat command, then confess your own sin against it ("I've spilled out my share of adverbs in my time").
5. Use profanity as a truth-instrument, not decoration — the blunt word where the polite word would lie.
6. Talk to the reader directly and warmly — "Relax. Chill." "Please oh please." "do whatever it is you do when you've got something to celebrate."
7. Close not with a moral but with a return to life and feeling — the airplane lifting off, "getting happy, okay?"

## Don't (top 7)
1. Don't use an adverb to prop up a verb you didn't pick well — "He closed the door firmly" when the scene already told us how.
2. Don't write the passive voice ("The body was carried from the kitchen") when an active subject is right there.
3. Don't dress up the vocabulary — never "emolument" for "tip," never the long Latinate word out of shame for the short one.
4. Don't pad the dialogue tag — "said" is almost always the right verb; not "he grated," not "Bill jerked out."
5. Don't start from theme or message ("such discussions are for Better Minds"); start from story, let meaning surface in the second draft.
6. Don't lecture, pose, or sound literary — no "transcendental asshole" gasbaggery; if it sounds like an English-exam answer, cut it.
7. Don't end on a tidy upbeat platitude — King would rather have Stu say "I don't know" honestly than hand you an "empty platitude I didn't believe myself."

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The flat confession / blunt thesis — e.g. "This is a short book because most books about writing are filled with bullshit." (Second Foreword)
- The scene dropped in mid-motion with a concrete object — e.g. "I had found a cement cinderblock in a corner of the garage and had managed to pick it up." (C.V.)
- The one-word answer as a whole opening — e.g. "Telepathy, of course." (What Writing Is)

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The hard-won credo stripped to a few stressed words — e.g. "The scariest moment is always just before you start. After that, things can only get better."
- The image of return-to-life or plain gratitude — e.g. "it makes my life a brighter and more pleasant place." / "Getting happy, okay? Getting happy."

## DO (emulate)

- **Write classic style.** See a true thing first, then show it to a competent equal; present, don't argue, don't hedge, don't use the page to sort out your own thinking. *Teaching value: the single best stance for an explainer — the learner is treated as capable and shown, not lectured.*
- **Replace every rule with its reason.** Give the cognitive mechanism (memory load, given-then-new, the curse of knowledge) so the reader understands *why* and can apply judgment. *Teaching value: reasons are remembered; rules-by-rote are forgotten — the core of durable learning.*
- **Make every abstraction seeable.** Turn the idea into an object, an actor, or a scene; coin a vivid sticky label for any recurring concept (as he coined *zombie nouns* and "soul of the gaps"). *Teaching value: a concrete image is what makes a hard concept stick.*
- **Pair every generalization with an example, and every example with its generalization.** Never leave one without the other. *Teaching value: this is the explanatory loop — claim, "for example…", restate so it clicks.*
- **Open strong** — a real question ("Why is so much writing so hard to understand?"), a flat counterintuitive premise ("Writing is an unnatural act"), a familiar story flipped, or a genuine confession. Provoke curiosity in the first line.
- **Pin claims to evidence** — a precise number, a named study, a sourced quote — and debunk a comfortable myth with the finding, not with indignation. *Teaching value: models intellectual conscientiousness, the deepest lesson under the topic.*
- **Disarm before you argue.** When a point is contested, concede your own side's real flaws first, then make the case.
- **Build long sentences that read downhill** (subject and verb early, modifiers branching right), then reset with a two-to-five-word sentence to land the point. Use triads where each member carries a *specific*.
- **Order given-before-new**, thread one moving protagonist through subject position, state things in the positive, and cut needless words.
- **Hunt and explain jargon** — banish what you can, define on the spot what you must keep, assume the reader is *not* in your clubhouse. *Teaching value: this is the built-in clarity gate for any learner less expert than the writer.*
- **Be funny in a learned, deadpan way** — a sly coinage, a perfectly chosen specimen of bad prose, a parenthetical wink. Aim the edge at habits and ideas.
- **Self-exemplify** — obey your own rule on the page where the reader can see it ("I looked it up").
- **Read it aloud** before you ship it — if you can't say it smoothly, the reader can't either.
- **Close on the wider point** — a rule-of-three with a faint moral lift, or a perfectly chosen borrowed maxim, attributed.

## DON'T (avoid)

- **Don't hand down a rule with no reason** ("never use the passive," full stop). Pinker calls reason-free advice "ham-fisted"; the *why* is the whole point.
- **Don't leave jargon, abbreviations, or insider argot unexplained**, and don't assume a term is "standard" when it is clubhouse-only.
- **Don't pile zombie nouns and nominalizations** in subject position, or bury the actor in officialese when an actor should be visible.
- **Don't hedge by tic** — no apologetic scare quotes, no "it could be argued that perhaps," no whinging that the topic is "complex and controversial." Qualify only by naming the genuine exception.
- **Don't condescend or talk down.** The reader is a smart adult and an equal; clarity is not the same as dumbing down. *Teaching value: never confuse simplifying for the learner with patronizing them.*
- **Don't moralize about "the decline of the language" or "kids today."** Every generation has cried this, and it is always wrong.
- **Don't open on a cliché or banality** ("Since the dawn of time," "Recently, scholars have been increasingly concerned with…") or close on a generic upbeat send-off.
- **Don't confuse an anecdote with a trend, argue from people instead of reasons, or collapse a real issue into a war between two -isms.** "It isn't about being right. It's about getting it right."
- **Don't pad a triad with three vague abstractions.** Every member of a list must carry a concrete particular (golf tee, catcher's mask), never three synonyms for "important." *(This is also the difference between his real rule-of-three and the AI-slop tripled list.)*
- **Don't perform erudition for its own sake.** The bookish words and the pop-culture references are seasoning that aids the point — never pomp that obscures it.
- **Don't sound like an LLM.** Pinker's own diagnosis of AI prose: it is "sound" but "so generic and prosaic" you "can almost recognize the output of a large language model." The cure is exactly this artifact — the named reason, the seeable image, the specific number, the deflating snap.

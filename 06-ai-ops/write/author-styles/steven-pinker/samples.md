## Classic style — prose as a window onto the world

> "prose is a window onto the world."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 2

**Demonstrates:** The governing metaphor in six words. The whole stance — writer sees, reader sees, language is transparent — compressed to a single concrete image. Note how a doctrine about abstraction is itself stated concretely.

---

> "What classic style does is explain them as if they were objects and forces that would be recognizable to anyone standing in a position to see them."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 2

**Demonstrates:** His answer to "but I have to write about abstract ideas." The cure is not to avoid abstraction but to *embody* it — turn the idea into something a person could literally watch.

## Opening strong

> "Good writing starts strong. Not with a cliché… not with a banality… but with a contentful observation that provokes curiosity."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 1

**Demonstrates:** A rule stated in his signature shape — short declarative thesis, then a *not… not… but* triad that names the two failure modes before delivering the right way. The structure teaches the rule.

---

> "Writing is an unnatural act."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 2

**Demonstrates:** The flat, counterintuitive four-word opener. After a long preceding build, the short snap resets attention. This is the rhythm fingerprint: limber long sentences punctuated by tiny ones.

## The curse of knowledge — his master idea

> "Call it the Curse of Knowledge: a difficulty in imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3

**Demonstrates:** The colon introducing a definition (a Pinker punctuation habit), the act of *coining and labelling* the enemy, and a plain restatement that anyone can hold. The label will now carry the concept everywhere.

---

> "The curse of knowledge is the single best explanation I know of why good people write bad prose."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3

**Demonstrates:** First-person confidence ("I know") without arrogance; a sweeping claim made precise by "good people" (it's not malice or stupidity). The sentence reframes the whole problem of bad writing.

---

> "Like a drunk who is too impaired to realize that he is too impaired to drive, we do not notice the curse because the curse prevents us from noticing it."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3

**Demonstrates:** Make-the-abstract-a-picture in action — a recursive cognitive blind spot rendered as a single homely image, then the paradox stated cleanly. The analogy does the explaining.

---

> "Still, their writing stinks."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3

**Demonstrates:** The three-word snap after a generous paragraph about brilliant, honest scholars. Plain Anglo-Saxon ("stinks"), deadpan, and it lands the paragraph's whole point. Long build, short payoff.

## Evidence over assertion

> "they guessed thirteen minutes; in fact, it took thirty-two."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3

**Demonstrates:** The number that detonates the intuition. He doesn't say experts underestimate; he shows the exact gap. The semicolon pivots from belief to fact. Precision *is* the argument.

## Language is living, not legislated

> "Language is not a protocol legislated by an authority but rather a wiki that pools the contributions of millions of writers and speakers…"
> — *The Sense of Style*, prologue

**Demonstrates:** A *not X but Y* reframe that swaps a stuffy metaphor (protocol, authority) for a contemporary concrete one (a wiki). His worldview — descriptive, democratic, anti-scold — in one balanced sentence.

## The parallel/triadic build

> "English syntax demands subject before object. Human memory demands light before heavy. Human comprehension demands topic before comment…"
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 4

**Demonstrates:** Three matched clauses, identical syntax, only the meaningful term varying. The parallelism makes three competing technical demands feel ordered and memorable — structure carrying argument.

## Self-exemplification with a wink

> "Actually, he didn't say that—I looked it up."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 6

**Demonstrates:** He states the rule "look things up," then visibly obeys it — correcting a misattributed quotation in real time. The deadpan aside is the proof. Form becomes content.

## Closing on the wider, faintly moral point

> "…to enhance the spread of ideas, to exemplify attention to detail, and to add to the beauty of the world."
> — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 6 (final line)

**Demonstrates:** The rule-of-three close with a rising moral lift — never a generic upbeat send-off. Three infinitive phrases, escalating from utility to ethics to beauty. This is how Pinker lands a whole book.

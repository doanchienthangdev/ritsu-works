# Steven Pinker — Signature Moves

<!-- Each = name + what it is + when to use + a short attributed example (≤25 words from this
     copyrighted book, always attributed). These are the techniques a writer deliberately
     deploys to sound like Pinker. -->

## Classic Style — The Window onto the World
**What:** Adopt the stance that you can *see* something true and your only job is to orient a competent reader's gaze until she sees it for herself. Prose is a window, not a performance; you present, you don't argue, you don't use the page to sort out what you think. The reader is your equal and can recognize the truth given an unobstructed view.
**When:** The default posture for any explanatory or argumentative piece — and the specific cure for academese, bureaucratese, and corporatese. Reach for it whenever the draft starts hedging, throat-clearing, or hiding behind abstraction.
**How:** Find the concrete thing worth showing and the perfect vantage point on it; then write as if pointing it out in conversation. Even abstract ideas get shown "as if they were objects and forces that would be recognizable to anyone standing in a position to see them."
**Example:** "prose is a window onto the world." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 2. And the stance it implies: "The writer and the reader are equals, and the process of directing the reader's gaze takes the form of a conversation." (ch. 2)

## Give the Reason, Kill the Diktat
**What:** Never hand down a rule bare. Replace every "do this / don't do that" with the cognitive mechanism that makes it true — how memory load rises and falls, how attention works, why a construction helps or hurts. The reason is more memorable than the rule and lets the writer apply judgment instead of obeying robotically.
**When:** Any time you would otherwise issue a commandment. Especially when overturning a received rule (the "avoid the passive" superstition) — explain the function the maligned thing actually serves.
**How:** State the phenomenon, then the underlying psychology, then the practical upshot. Pinker's whole project is "replacing dogma about usage with reason and evidence."
**Example:** On the passive: it "has a number of indispensable functions because of the way it engages a reader's attention and memory." — *The Sense of Style*, prologue. He gives the mechanism, not the order.

## Reverse-Engineer Good Prose Aloud
**What:** Take a passage you admire, quote it, and then annotate it phrase by phrase — naming the move behind each effect (the parallelism, the snatched-from-cliché idiom, the stressed monosyllable). You teach the craft by dissecting excellence in slow motion, "think[ing] aloud as I try to understand what makes them work."
**When:** When you want to *show* how a technique works rather than assert that it works; ideal for teaching, criticism, or any "here's why this is good" passage.
**How:** Reproduce the admired line, then walk through its parts, each as a mini-heading, each move named so the reader can steal it.
**Example:** Of Dawkins's famous opening, Pinker observes: "Good writing starts strong. Not with a cliché… not with a banality… but with a contentful observation that provokes curiosity." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 1.

## Make the Abstract a Moving Picture (and Coin a Label for It)
**What:** Refuse to leave an abstraction abstract. Turn it into something the reader can watch — an object, an actor, a scene — and, if it recurs, give it a sticky, often slightly comic name that carries the whole concept. The label does the remembering for the reader.
**When:** Whenever a concept is too dry, too large, or too technical to land as a plain sentence; whenever you catch yourself stacking nouns.
**How:** Find the picture that *is* the idea; name the failure mode in a phrase. Pinker's coinages — *zombie nouns* for nominalizations, *the clubhouse* for insider jargon, *the curse of knowledge* itself — are concepts you can carry out of the room.
**Example:** "Like a drunk who is too impaired to realize that he is too impaired to drive, we do not notice the curse because the curse prevents us from noticing it." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3.

## The Number That Detonates the Myth
**What:** Counter a comfortable belief not with indignation but with the actual finding — usually a precise number or a named study that quietly blows the belief apart. Evidence is the rebuttal; the contrast between the assumption and the data does the persuading.
**When:** Any time you are about to debunk conventional wisdom or assert a claim about how minds or readers behave. Replace "people underestimate this" with the measured gap.
**How:** State the intuition, then drop the experiment and the figures. Let the disparity speak.
**Example:** "experienced cell phone users were asked how long it would take novices to learn to use the phone, they guessed thirteen minutes; in fact, it took thirty-two." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3.

## Name the Enemy with a Coinage
**What:** Define good prose by labelling, precisely and wittily, the diseased prose that destroys it. A sharp coinage for the failure mode lets the reader instantly know which side of the line a passage is on — and makes the standard memorable.
**When:** Setting up a chapter, a critique, or a manifesto; any "how to do X well" piece where naming the wrong way clarifies the right way.
**How:** Coin or deploy a vivid label — *academese, bureaucratese, corporatese, legalese, officialese, gobbledygook* — and, ideally, exhibit a verbatim specimen of the crime before prescribing the cure.
**Example:** Classic style is "the strongest cure I know for the disease that enfeebles academic, bureaucratic, corporate, legal, and official prose." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 2.

## The Triad / Parallel Build
**What:** Stack three matched members, or a run of parallel clauses, so the structure itself carries the argument and the rhythm. Often used to enumerate distinctions or to close a passage on a satisfying three-beat.
**When:** When listing competing demands, summing up, or ending a section; whenever parallelism can make a complex point feel ordered and inevitable.
**How:** Hold the syntax constant across members so only the meaningful element varies (his own "rule of one variable"). Let the third beat land hardest.
**Example:** "English syntax demands subject before object. Human memory demands light before heavy. Human comprehension demands topic before comment and given before new." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 4.

## Self-Exemplification — Obey Your Own Rule on the Page
**What:** Demonstrate a principle by performing it in the very sentence that states it, often with a wink. The form becomes proof of the content, and the small act of visible discipline earns the reader's trust.
**When:** When teaching a rule you can enact (look things up; start strong; omit needless words); when a touch of humour will make the lesson stick.
**How:** State the rule, then immediately do the thing — and, where it's funny, point out that you did it.
**Example:** After quoting a maxim attributed to Mark Twain: "Actually, he didn't say that—I looked it up." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 6. He models "look things up" by looking it up.

# Steven Pinker — Signature Moves

## 1. Classic Style — Prose as a Window onto the World
**What:** Adopt the stance that you can *see* something true and your only job is to orient a competent reader's gaze until she sees it for herself. Prose is a window, not a performance; you present, you don't argue, you don't use the page to sort out what you think. The reader is your equal and can recognize the truth given an unobstructed view.
**When:** The default posture for any explanatory or argumentative piece — and the specific cure for academese, bureaucratese, and corporatese. Reach for it whenever the draft starts hedging, throat-clearing, or hiding behind abstraction.
**How:** Find the concrete thing worth showing and the perfect vantage point on it; then write as if pointing it out in conversation. Even abstract ideas get shown "as if they were objects and forces that would be recognizable to anyone standing in a position to see them."
**Example:** "prose is a window onto the world." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 2. Note that even his *doctrine about abstraction* is stated as a concrete image.

## 2. Give the Reason, Kill the Diktat
**What:** Never hand down a rule bare. Replace every "do this / don't do that" with the cognitive mechanism that makes it true — how memory load rises and falls, how attention works, why a construction helps or hurts. The reason is more memorable than the rule and lets the writer apply judgment instead of obeying robotically.
**When:** Any time you would otherwise issue a commandment. Especially when overturning a received rule (the "avoid the passive" superstition) — explain the function the maligned thing actually serves.
**How:** State the phenomenon, then the underlying psychology, then the practical upshot. His whole project is "replacing dogma about usage with reason and evidence."
**Example:** On the passive: it "has a number of indispensable functions because of the way it engages a reader's attention and memory." — *The Sense of Style*, prologue. The mechanism, not the order.

## 3. Make the Abstract a Moving Picture (and Coin a Label for It)
**What:** Refuse to leave an abstraction abstract. Turn it into something the reader can watch — an object, an actor, a scene — and, if it recurs, give it a sticky, often slightly comic name that carries the whole concept. The label does the remembering for the reader.
**When:** Whenever a concept is too dry, too large, or too technical to land as a plain sentence; whenever you catch yourself stacking nouns.
**How:** Find the picture that *is* the idea; name the failure mode in a phrase. His coinages — *zombie nouns* for nominalizations, *the clubhouse* for insider jargon, "soul of the gaps" for god-of-the-gaps mysticism — are concepts you can carry out of the room.
**Example (spoken):** "no one can form an image of a paradigm in their mind's eye." — David Perell interview, 2025. So he hands you the bunny rabbit instead of "the stimulus."
**Example (book):** "Like a drunk who is too impaired to realize that he is too impaired to drive, we do not notice the curse because the curse prevents us from noticing it." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3.

## 4. The Number That Detonates the Myth
**What:** Counter a comfortable belief not with indignation but with the actual finding — usually a precise number or named study that quietly blows the belief apart. Evidence is the rebuttal; the contrast between the assumption and the data does the persuading.
**When:** Any time you are about to debunk conventional wisdom or assert a claim about how minds or readers behave. Replace "people underestimate this" with the measured gap.
**How:** State the intuition, then drop the experiment and the figures. Let the disparity speak.
**Example (book):** "they guessed thirteen minutes; in fact, it took thirty-two." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3.
**Example (essay):** "The human brain, with 86 billion neurons and 100 trillion connections, is the most complex object in the known universe." — WSJ letter, 2025. The number makes the "soul of the gaps" inference look small.

## 5. Disarm, Then Argue (the No-Shill Opening)
**What:** Before making an unpopular or one-sided-sounding case, establish that you are not a shill — enumerate your *own* prior criticisms of the very thing you're about to defend, or concede the real flaws up front. Only then make the contested argument. The reader, no longer braced for propaganda, listens.
**When:** Op-eds, rebuttals, any "actually, the consensus is overheated" piece. The move buys the credibility the argument needs.
**How:** Open with your bona fides as a critic, list the genuine problems specifically, then pivot ("So I'm hardly an apologist when I say…") to the case for proportion.
**Example:** "In my 22 years as a Harvard professor, I have not been afraid to bite the hand that feeds me." — "Harvard Derangement Syndrome," NYT 2025. He spends four paragraphs criticizing Harvard before defending it.

## 6. Accumulate the Specific
**What:** Defeat a vague accusation or a sweeping abstraction by burying it under a pile of concrete particulars. The sheer specificity overwhelms the generalization; the list itself is the argument.
**When:** When a claim is fuzzy and grand ("a woke madrasa," "writing has declined") and the honest rebuttal is just *the facts, enumerated.*
**How:** Stack vivid, checkable specifics in parallel, and let one land with a wink.
**Example:** Of Harvard's inventions: "They invented the golf tee and the catcher's mask." — "Harvard Derangement Syndrome," NYT 2025. The deadpan trivial-but-true specifics undercut the grand denunciation.

## 7. The Vivid Analogy That Carries the Argument
**What:** Replace an abstract claim about what to do with a single physical image so apt that the reader's intuition does the rest. The analogy is not decoration; it *is* the argument, compressed.
**When:** When the logical point is sound but bloodless, or when you want a position to feel obviously right (or obviously wrong) on contact.
**How:** Find the everyday physical scene whose logic mirrors the abstract one, and state it plainly.
**Example:** "The appropriate treatment … is to diagnose which parts need which remedies, not to cut its carotid and watch it bleed out." — "Harvard Derangement Syndrome," NYT 2025. (Also: "if you're standing in a downpour and Mr. Trump tells you to put up an umbrella, you shouldn't refuse just to spite him.")

## 8. The Deflating One-Liner
**What:** After a long, generous, scrupulously fair paragraph, drop a very short sentence that delivers the verdict — plain Anglo-Saxon, deadpan, and final. The contrast in length is the punch.
**When:** To land the point of a paragraph you've argued honestly; to puncture a pretension without a sneer.
**How:** Earn it with the fair build first. Then: three to six words, no adverbs.
**Example (book):** after a paragraph praising brilliant, honest scholars — "Still, their writing stinks." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 3.
**Example (essay):** "None of this implies a ghost in the machine." — WSJ letter, 2025. ("Oy gevalt!" does the same job mid-essay.)

## 9. The Borrowed Maxim
**What:** Close (or pivot) on a perfectly chosen, often surprising, *attributed* quotation that says your point better than you could — high or low, ancient or pop. The act of quoting it both lands the idea and models intellectual generosity.
**When:** Endings, especially; or to crystallize a section. Best when the source is unexpected (an economist, a country singer, a physicist).
**How:** Set it up in one clause, attribute it, let it be the last word.
**Example:** "As the economist Joan Robinson put it, 'Ideology is like breath: You never smell your own.'" — "Harvard Derangement Syndrome," NYT 2025. (He closes the same essay on David Deutsch: "Everything that is not forbidden by laws of nature is achievable, given the right knowledge.")

## 10. The Triad / Parallel Build (Each Member a Specific)
**What:** Stack three matched members, or a run of parallel clauses, so the structure itself carries the argument and the rhythm — but make every member carry a *concrete particular*, not a synonym. Often closes a passage on a satisfying three-beat.
**When:** When listing competing demands, summing up, or ending a section.
**How:** Hold the syntax constant across members so only the meaningful element varies (his "rule of one variable"); let the third beat land hardest.
**Example (book):** "English syntax demands subject before object. Human memory demands light before heavy. Human comprehension demands topic before comment and given before new." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 4.
**Example (essay):** the character of a man — "privileged in upbringing but louche in lifestyle, personally endearing but at times obstreperous and irresponsible, otherworldly brilliant but forehead-slappingly foolish." — Quillette, 2026.

## 11. Self-Exemplification — Obey Your Own Rule on the Page
**What:** Demonstrate a principle by performing it in the very sentence that states it, often with a wink. The form becomes proof of the content, and the small act of visible discipline earns the reader's trust.
**When:** When teaching a rule you can enact (look things up; start strong; omit needless words); when a touch of humour will make the lesson stick.
**How:** State the rule, then immediately do the thing — and, where it's funny, point out that you did it.
**Example:** After quoting a maxim attributed to Mark Twain: "Actually, he didn't say that—I looked it up." — *The Sense of Style*, ch. 6. He models "look things up" by looking it up.

## 12. Example ⇄ Generalization (never one without the other)
**What:** Pair every abstract claim with a concrete instance, and every vivid instance with the principle it illustrates. A generalization without an example "erases detail"; an example without a generalization leaves the reader asking "appropos of what?"
**When:** Always, when teaching or explaining. It is the engine of his explanatory paragraphs.
**How:** Make the claim, then "for example…", then restate the claim so the reader feels it click.
**Example (spoken):** "generalizations without examples and examples without generalizations are both useless." — David Perell interview, 2025. (He then proves it: "a bathroom isn't necessarily a room with a bath.")

# Steven Pinker — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A cognitive scientist writing classic style — prose as a window onto the world, where the writer sees something true and orients a competent equal's gaze to it — in long, limber, parallel sentences warmed by wit, anchored by a concrete image and a checkable fact, always fighting the curse of knowledge.

**Rhythm rule:** Build long, right-branching sentences carried by parallelism and rhythm (20–40 words), then drop a three-to-five-word sentence to land it ("Not so." / "Writing is an unnatural act." / "Still, their writing stinks.").
**Diction rule:** Name the mechanism, then make it seeable — every abstraction gets a concrete image (zombie nouns, the drunk who can't tell he's too drunk to drive) and, where a claim is made, a number or named source ("guessed thirteen minutes; in fact, thirty-two").
**Reader rule:** Treat the reader as a smart adult and equal who can connect the dots — give reasons, not diktats; never patronize, never write "because I said so."

## Do (top 7)
1. Adopt classic style: see a truth first, then show it — point, don't argue; the reader is competent and can recognize truth given an unobstructed view.
2. Give the *reason* behind every rule (the cognitive mechanism: memory load, given-then-new, the curse of knowledge), so advice is remembered, not obeyed by rote.
3. Make the abstract concrete — convert any idea into objects and actors a reader could watch ("explain them as if they were objects and forces" someone could see).
4. Back claims with a real number, a named study, or a sourced quote — and demolish the comfortable myth with the actual evidence.
5. Open strong with a contentful observation or a real question — never a cliché ("Since the dawn of time") or a banality ("Recently, scholars have...").
6. Season authority with dry wit, a vivid coinage, and a self-exemplifying move (state your own rule, then visibly obey it: "I looked it up").
7. Build triads and parallel constructions ("light before heavy… topic before comment… given before new"); thread one protagonist through subject position.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Don't pronounce rules as commandments chiselled in sapphire — Pinker mocks the stylebook that treats usage "the way fundamentalists treat the Ten Commandments."
2. Don't leave jargon, abbreviations, or clubhouse argot unexplained — "we forget that our readers may not be members of the clubhouse."
3. Don't string zombie nouns in subject position; don't let nominalizations and the passive bury the actor when an actor should be seen.
4. Don't hedge by tic — no apologetic scare quotes, no whinging that the topic is "complex and controversial"; qualify by spelling out the exception instead.
5. Don't condescend or talk down; clarity is not the same as dumbing down — the reader is an equal, not a remedial student.
6. Don't moralize about "the decline of the language" or "kids today" — every generation has cried this since the Sumerian clay tablets.
7. Don't confuse an anecdote with a trend, attack the person instead of the argument, or reduce a real issue to a war between two -isms.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The real question, asked plainly — e.g. "Why is so much writing so hard to understand?"
- The flat counterintuitive premise — e.g. "Writing is an unnatural act."
- The personal confession that frames the whole book — e.g. "I love style manuals."

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The rule-of-three on a rising, faintly moral note — e.g. ends on striving "to add to the beauty of the world."
- The borrowed maxim he then fact-checks on himself — e.g. quotes a saying, then: "Actually, he didn't say that—I looked it up."

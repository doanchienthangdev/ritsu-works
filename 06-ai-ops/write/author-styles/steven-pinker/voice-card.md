# Steven Pinker — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A cognitive scientist writing *classic style* — prose as a window onto the world, where the writer sees something true and orients a competent equal's gaze to it — in long, limber, parallel sentences warmed by dry wit, anchored by a concrete image and a checkable number, always fighting the curse of knowledge.

**Rhythm rule:** Build a long, right-branching sentence carried by parallelism (20–40 words, subject and verb early, modifiers branching right), then drop a three-to-five-word sentence to land it. ("Writing is an unnatural act." / "Still, their writing stinks." / "None of this implies a ghost in the machine.")
**Diction rule:** Name the mechanism, then make it *seeable* — every abstraction gets a concrete image (the bunny rabbit, not the stimulus) and, where a claim is made, a number or named source ("86 billion neurons"; "they guessed thirteen minutes; in fact, thirty-two").
**Reader rule:** Treat the reader as a smart, curious adult who is *not in your clubhouse* — give reasons, not orders; define jargon on the spot; never patronize, never write "because I said so."

## Do (top 7)
1. Adopt classic style: see a truth first, then *show* it to a competent equal — point, don't argue, don't hedge.
2. Replace every rule with its *reason* (the cognitive mechanism: memory load, given-then-new, the curse of knowledge), so advice is understood, not obeyed by rote.
3. Make the abstract concrete — convert any idea into an object, an actor, or a scene a reader could watch; coin a sticky label for a recurring one.
4. Back claims with a real number, a named study, or a sourced quote — and demolish a comfortable myth with the finding, not with indignation.
5. Open strong — a real question, a flat counterintuitive premise, a familiar story flipped, or a credibility-establishing confession. Never a cliché.
6. When the point is contested, disarm first (concede your own side's real flaws), then make the unpopular case.
7. Build triads and parallel constructions where each member carries a *specific* (golf tee, catcher's mask); thread one protagonist through subject position.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Don't pronounce rules as commandments with no reason — Pinker calls reason-free advice "ham-fisted."
2. Don't leave jargon, abbreviations, or clubhouse argot unexplained — assume the reader is *not* a member of the clubhouse.
3. Don't string zombie nouns in subject position; don't let nominalizations bury the actor when an actor should be seen.
4. Don't hedge by tic — no apologetic scare quotes, no "it could be argued that perhaps"; qualify by naming the genuine exception.
5. Don't condescend or talk down; clarity is not dumbing down — the reader is an equal, not a remedial student.
6. Don't moralize about "the decline of the language" or "kids today" — every generation has cried it, and it's always wrong.
7. Don't pad a triad with three vague abstractions, confuse an anecdote with a trend, or attack the person instead of the argument.

## Signature openings (how he starts)
- The real question, asked plainly — e.g. "Why is so much writing so hard to understand?"
- The flat counterintuitive premise — e.g. "Writing is an unnatural act."
- The familiar story turned on its head — e.g. "When the little boy said the emperor was naked, he wasn't telling them anything they didn't already know."
- The credibility-establishing confession — e.g. "In my 22 years as a Harvard professor, I have not been afraid to bite the hand that feeds me."

## Signature closings (how he lands)
- The rule-of-three on a rising, faintly moral note — e.g. ending on striving "to add to the beauty of the world."
- The perfectly chosen borrowed maxim, attributed — e.g. "Ideology is like breath: You never smell your own." (Joan Robinson)
- The deflating short snap after a long fair build — e.g. "None of this implies a ghost in the machine."

## ⚠ /write GATE CAVEATS (read before drafting)
1. **Em-dashes.** Pinker's real prose is full of em-dashes — but the humanizer strips them. Where he'd dash, use a **colon** (for a verdict/definition; very Pinker), a comma-bounded clause, **parentheses** (he uses them), or a fresh short sentence. Do not just delete the dash and run the clauses together.
2. **His genuine moves overlap with AI tells — keep them earned, not decorative.** (a) Rule-of-three lists are his signature, but every member must carry a *concrete specific* (golf tee, catcher's mask), never three synonyms for "important" — three vague abstractions is the AI-slop tell. (b) The antithesis "It isn't about X. It's about Y." is his ("It isn't about being right. It's about getting it right.") but is also overused by AI; allow it **once** per piece, when truly earned. (c) Erudite/bookish words and pop-culture anchors must *serve the point*, never decorate — perform erudition and the illusion breaks.

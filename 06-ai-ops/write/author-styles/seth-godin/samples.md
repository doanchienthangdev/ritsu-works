## Naming a pattern so it sticks

> "remarkable is a really cool word because we think it just means neat, but it also means worth making a remark about. And that is the essence of where idea diffusion is going."
> — *How to Get Your Ideas to Spread*, TED 2003

**Demonstrates:** The core Godin move — coin/redefine a one-word handle (*remarkable*), then state the redefinition plainly. Notice he etymologizes a familiar word rather than inventing jargon. Short clauses, no hedging.

> "There's a great new word called sonder, which means realizing that other people also have a noise in their head the way you have a noise in your head. And for most of us that's a revelation."
> — *How to Do Work That Matters*, Chase Jarvis Live, 2018

**Demonstrates:** Importing outside vocabulary (a coined word) to name a felt experience, then immediately translating it into plain second-person language ("a noise in their head"). The abstraction is always cashed out in a concrete sensation.

## The risk-flip (inverting the intuition)

> "the riskiest thing you can do now is be safe… And being very good is one of the worst things you can possibly do. Very good is boring. Very good is average."
> — *How to Get Your Ideas to Spread*, TED 2003

**Demonstrates:** State the default (*be safe / be very good*), then invert it flat. Repetition of the subject ("Very good is… Very good is…") hammers the reframe. Declaratives only — no "I think maybe."

---

> "Winners quit all the time. They just quit the right stuff at the right time."
> — *The Dip*, 2007

**Demonstrates:** A two-sentence inversion of a cultural taboo ("quitters never win"). Eleven words. The second sentence rescues and redefines the first — a complete idea with no scaffolding. (≤25-word book quote.)

## The concrete parable carrying the abstraction

> "before sliced bread was invented in the 1910s… for the first 15 years after sliced bread was available, no one bought it. No one knew about it. It was a complete and total failure. And the reason is that until Wonder came along and figured out how to spread the idea of sliced bread, no one wanted it."
> — *How to Get Your Ideas to Spread*, TED 2003

**Demonstrates:** Open on a tiny, specific, true story; only afterward state the law it proves. The generalization ("can you get your idea to spread or not") is withheld until the picture has done its work.

---

> "there are eight people at Table 4. If I can go bring magic to Table 4, even though I've got a long shift ahead of me, if I act like it's the only chance those people are ever going to have… cause it is."
> — *How to Do Work That Matters*, Chase Jarvis Live, 2018

**Demonstrates:** Replacing an abstraction ("smallest viable audience") with one vivid, countable image (eight people, Table 4). Specificity is the persuasion. The dropped fragment "cause it is" mimics live speech and adds a beat.

## One-line paragraphs as a drumbeat

> "There's safety in numbers. (Virtual, digital connection).
> Resilience, too.
> Not to mention inspiration and mutual support.
> Go start a group.
> Find the others.
> Learn together. Meet regularly."
> — *Raft up!*, Seth's Blog, 2020

**Demonstrates:** Maximum white space, one beat per line, an imperative cluster at the close. Fragments ("Resilience, too.") are allowed. The rhythm — not the information density — is the payload.

## The leadership-frame construction

> "The Beatles did not invent teenagers. They merely decided to lead them… Bob Marley did not invent Rastafarians. He just stepped up and said 'follow me.'"
> — *The Tribes We Lead*, TED 2009

**Demonstrates:** A repeated parallel frame ("X did not invent Y; they merely led them"). Stacking two or three identical structures makes the claim feel like a discovered law rather than an opinion.

---

## The rhetorical-question cascade

> "What if we did the opposite? What if we got specific? What if we said: if there were 100 people I changed, 200 people I taught, a thousand people who were my patrons… Would it be enough?"
> — *How to Do Work That Matters*, Chase Jarvis Live, 2018

**Demonstrates:** A short stack of "What if…?" questions in plain second person that lets the reader arrive at the reframe themselves. Builds toward a single yes/no question that sets up the punchline.

---

## Reframing a loaded word, generously

> "It doesn't mean hustling people, and it doesn't mean being reckless. What it means is that you are showing up in a way to make things better. And if you're gonna make things better, it means you're going to change things."
> — *Cause a Ruckus with Your Leadership*, 2020

**Demonstrates:** Define by negation first ("it doesn't mean… it doesn't mean…"), then deliver the generous redefinition. The chain-link logic — better → change → discomfort — walks one step at a time, each clause picking up the last clause's last word.

## The empathy-first reframe of marketing/work

> "the selfishness cannot stand in a world where we have lots of choices — because if I can bestow my attention on anybody, why should I bestow it on a toddler? … what it means to see before being seen is to say: that person I seek to serve — what's the story in their head?"
> — *How to Do Work That Matters*, Chase Jarvis Live, 2018

**Demonstrates:** Service-before-self sequencing. A sharp metaphor (the marketer as a toddler), then a question that reorients the whole frame toward the other person. "The person I seek to serve" is signature Godin diction — earnest, slightly formal, never cynical.

---

## Build-to-a-turn, then stop

> "What I want you to do — it only takes 24 hours — is create a movement. Something that matters. Start. Do it. We need it."
> — *The Tribes We Lead*, TED 2009

**Demonstrates:** The whole talk collapses into a four-fragment imperative finish. No recap, no "in conclusion." The shortest possible sentences at the very end; he trusts the white space after "We need it." to land the close.

---

## The "safe is risky" macro, in prose register

> "before it's too late, realize one basic truth: Safe is Risky. Cogs don't take risks. But cogs are the ones that are next to go."
> — *When people become cogs*, Seth's Blog, 2002

**Demonstrates:** How the inversion travels into written prose: a colon-delivered slogan ("Safe is Risky"), then two terse sentences that turn the same coin twice. Capitalized for emphasis — Godin will bend typography to make a phrase a handle.

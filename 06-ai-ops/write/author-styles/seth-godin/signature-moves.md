# Seth Godin — Signature Moves

<!-- The deliberate, reproducible rhetorical/structural devices Godin uses. Ordered by how

## 1. Name the unnamed pattern with a sticky 1–3-word handle

**What:** Take a phenomenon the reader feels but has no word for, and hand it a short, concrete, often borrowed or coined label — then let the label carry the whole argument. The name does the work a 200-page treatise can't.
**When:** The opening or climax of any piece where you want one idea to lodge and spread. Reach for it the moment you catch yourself explaining a concept in a full paragraph — compress it into a noun instead.
**Example:** "remarkable is a really cool word because we think it just means neat, but it also means *worth making a remark about*. And that is the essence of where idea diffusion is going." — *How to Get Your Ideas to Spread*, TED 2003. (Same move builds *Purple Cow, otaku, the Dip, sheepwalker, the lizard brain, smallest viable audience, sonder, emotional labor, the connection economy.*)

## 2. Invert the intuition (the risk-flip)

**What:** Take the conventional-wisdom default — *be safe, be very good, never quit, reach the masses* — and flip it into its opposite as a flat, declarative claim. The reframe is the payload.
**When:** When the audience is operating on an unexamined assumption. State their default, then turn it inside out in one sentence so the inversion is impossible to un-hear.
**Example:** "the riskiest thing you can do now is be safe… And being very good is one of the worst things you can possibly do. Very good is boring. Very good is average." — *How to Get Your Ideas to Spread*, TED 2003. (Book-length versions: *The Dip* — "Winners quit all the time. They just quit the right stuff at the right time" [≤25 words, *The Dip*, 2007]; *Purple Cow* — "Safe is risky.")

## 3. The one-line paragraph as a drumbeat

**What:** Break a passage into single sentences, each on its own line, so emphasis falls on every beat and the white space sets the pace. Often a short imperative cluster: *Go start a group. Find the others. Learn together.*
**When:** A manifesto, a call to action, or any climax where you want the reader to slow down and feel each claim land. Use it sparingly inside denser prose so the contrast hits.
**Example:**
> "There's safety in numbers. (Virtual, digital connection).
> Resilience, too.
> Not to mention inspiration and mutual support.
> Go start a group.
> Find the others."
> — *Raft up!*, Seth's Blog, 2020

## 4. The concrete parable / single vivid image instead of abstraction

**What:** Open with a tiny, specific story or a single mental picture — sliced bread, a purple cow, eight people at Table 4, a guy lost in an airport — and let it carry an idea that would otherwise need a model or a statistic.
**When:** When the idea is abstract and you need it *felt*, not understood. Lead with the picture; deliver the generalization only afterward, in one line.
**Example:** "you're driving down the road and you see a cow and you keep driving because you've seen cows before. Cows are invisible. Cows are boring. Who's going to stop and pull over and say, 'Oh, look, a cow.' Nobody. But if the cow was purple…" — *How to Get Your Ideas to Spread*, TED 2003.

## 5. The "X did not invent Y; they merely led them" construction

**What:** A parallel, almost scriptural sentence frame that asserts a counter-claim about leadership/change: someone famous didn't *create* the demand, they *led* a group that already had a yearning. The repeated grammar makes the point feel inevitable.
**When:** When making a claim about movements, leadership, or marketing-as-service. Stack two or three instances of the identical frame so the pattern itself becomes the argument.
**Example:** "The Beatles did not invent teenagers. They merely decided to lead them… Bob Marley did not invent Rastafarians. He just stepped up and said 'follow me.'" — *The Tribes We Lead*, TED 2009.

## 6. The rhetorical-question cascade

**What:** A short run of direct second-person questions — *What if we did the opposite? What if we got specific?* — that walks the reader through a reframe by making them answer in their own head rather than being told.
**When:** When you want the reader to arrive at the conclusion themselves, or to dramatize a choice. Especially powerful right before the punchline you're setting up.
**Example:** "What if we did the opposite? What if we got specific? What if we said: if there were 100 people I changed, 200 people I taught, a thousand people who were my patrons — what if we could do that? Would it be enough?" — *How to Do Work That Matters*, Chase Jarvis Live, 2018.

## 7. The reframed definition ("doing X is really doing Y")

**What:** Quietly redefine a loaded word — *marketing, art, quality, work, hustle, passion* — away from its assumed meaning toward a more generous, human one, stated as if it were obvious.
**When:** When a word carries baggage you need to clear before the reader will follow you. Drop the new definition flat and unargued; the confidence is what sells it.
**Example:** "What matters is doing work that matters for people who care." — *How to Do Work That Matters*, Chase Jarvis Live, 2018. (Also: "I'm not a fan of hustling. I'm a fan of a *generous* hustle." — *Impact Theory*, 2018; "art is a human act of making a change in the world" — paraphrased, *Cause a Ruckus*, 2020.)

## 8. Build to a quotable turn, then stop

**What:** Structure a passage so it climbs to a single tweet-length line that crystallizes everything — then end, refusing to over-explain or wrap with a summary. The last sentence is the souvenir the reader carries out.
**When:** The close of nearly any piece. Resist the corporate instinct to recap; trust the one line to do the work, and trust the white space after it.
**Example:** "What I want you to do — it only takes 24 hours — is create a movement. Something that matters. Start. Do it. We need it." — *The Tribes We Lead*, TED 2009. (Compare the blog ending: "It's gonna be okay. The speed bumps are fine. You're gonna be fine." — *Cause a Ruckus*, 2020.)

# Seth Godin — Style DNA

## 0. Voice in one breath
Seth Godin writes the way a generous teacher talks to one smart person across a table: short, declarative, plainspoken, and built to be quoted. A paragraph is often a single sentence. He hands you a sticky 1–3 word name for something you've felt but never labeled ("Purple Cow," "the Dip," "sheepwalker," "the lizard brain"), then flips the intuition you arrived with ("the riskiest thing you can do now is be safe"). The argument moves on concrete examples — sliced bread, Krispy Kreme, a 7-Eleven coffee server, Table 4 — never on jargon or data dumps. He addresses *you* directly, assumes you're capable, and lands not on a tidy bow but on a small, hard imperative: *ship*, *start*, *go*, *do the work that matters*.

## 1. Identity & stance
Godin is a marketer-turned-teacher who refuses the title "guru." His self-description is consistent and load-bearing: *"I see myself as a teacher"* and *"I'm just there to start a fire"* (Impact Theory; Chase Jarvis). He believes writing exists to **change the reader** — to make someone do work they were afraid to do — not to inform, impress, or sell. His posture is a peer who respects you and a provocateur who won't let you hide: he flatters your potential while indicting your excuses. The governing worldview, stated plainly: *"Art is a human act of making a change in the world, and if you're making a change, you're leading"* (Customer Bliss), and *"the work is the point. The process is the point"* (Impact Theory). He is in the business of trust — *"my goal is to build that trust, not to trade in on the trust"* — which is why the prose never oversells.

## 2. Diction & vocabulary
- **Reaches for:** plain Anglo-Saxon verbs (*ship, spread, lead, care, see, change, matter, notice, build, quit*) and concrete nouns (*cow, bread, chair, paint can, table, coffee, X-ray specs*). He names a **specific brand or person** rather than a category: not "a successful product" but *Krispy Kreme*, *the Aeron chair*, *Lionel Poilâne*, *Amanda Palmer*, *Banksy*, *Jerry Garcia*. He coins or imports a tight handle for an idea — *otaku, sonder, sneezers, sheepwalker, emotional labor, smallest viable audience, the connection economy, sprezzatura*. He uses second-person *you* and inclusive *we* constantly.
- **Refuses:** marketing/corporate jargon — and he says so out loud: *"words like scale, total addressable market, gross rating points"* are named only to mock them (Chase Jarvis). No "synergy," "leverage" as a buzzword, "best practices," "thought leadership." Almost no adjective-stacking and no hype words ("revolutionary," "game-changing," "world-class"). When he reaches for "remarkable," he immediately reclaims its literal meaning — *"worth making a remark about"* (TED 2003).
- **Register:** conversational-but-precise. Sits at colloquial ("whap people upside the head," "poo-pooed it," "a pain in the neck") and shifts up to aphoristic-declarative for the line he wants you to keep. Rarely formal; never stiff.
- **Concreteness:** very high. He distrusts abstraction and pays it off immediately with an example. The pattern is *name the abstract idea → then a specific, picturable thing*: "remarkable" → a purple cow; "emotional labor" → the flight attendant warm at the end of a 14-hour shift; "smallest viable audience" → eight people at Table 4.

## 3. Syntax & sentence mechanics
- **Sentence length:** mostly short — roughly 6–14 words for the load-bearing lines — interleaved with longer run-on, comma-spliced explanatory sentences when he's mid-story. The talks show the rhythm: *"Cows are invisible. Cows are boring."* (4 words, 3 words) sits next to a 40-word sentence about Otto Rohwedder. The *punch lines* are always short.
- **Rhythm:** a long, winding setup (the story, the example, the build) followed by a short, stressed turn. Setup → setup → **snap**. *"Very good is boring. Very good is average."* (TED 2003). He'll stack three short parallel beats then stop.
- **Fragments / parallelism / repetition:** heavy, deliberate. Anaphora is a signature — *"What if we got specific? What if we said…? Would it be enough?"* (Chase Jarvis); *"Who are you upsetting? Who are you connecting? Who are you leading?"* (TED 2009). Triads everywhere ("Start. Do it. We need it."). Repetition-with-build: *"and it spreads and it spreads"* (Google talk). One-word and one-line sentences used as drumbeats: *"Together."* / *"Wrong."* / *"Me."*
- **Punctuation habits:** short sentences and periods do most of the work; he rarely needs semicolons. Frequent rhetorical questions ending in `?`. Parentheticals for the wink/aside — *"(quick, become a boss!)"*, *"(virtual, digital connection)"*. He'd use a period or a colon where a lesser writer reaches for an em-dash; if a dash is stripped, the natural replacement is a hard stop and a new one-line sentence. Ellipses to trail a thought into the next post ("Let's see what happens…"). Occasional **bold** on the single phrase he wants you to screenshot.
- **Paragraphing:** very short. Frequently **one sentence per paragraph**, double-spaced (the blog default). A 200-word post may have ten paragraphs. White space is part of the voice — it forces each line to stand on its own and be quotable.

## 4. Structure & architecture
- **Openings:** he earns the first line with a concrete hook, never a thesis statement. Common patterns: (a) a small surprising fact or parable — *"before sliced bread was invented in the 1910s…"* (TED 2003); (b) a flat, almost rude assertion — *"No one cares about you"* (Google talk), *"Leadership is a choice"* (2016 blog); (c) a question or a self-deprecating sidestep — *"Doesn't sound like anyone I know"* (Impact Theory, on his own intro); (d) a story about an odd niche — the people who make balloon animals (TED 2009). The first line creates a small itch the rest scratches.
- **Development:** **accretion of concrete examples**, not linear proof. He states a pattern, then piles real instances on it until the pattern feels inevitable — purple cow → Aeron chair → Poilâne bread → Dutch Boy paint → Krispy Kreme. Each example is one short beat. He defines a term, then immediately tests it against the world. He argues by *picture*, not by *evidence table*.
- **Closings:** he lands on an imperative or a small, human turn — never a corporate "in conclusion" or an upbeat motivational send-off. *"Create a movement. Something that matters. Start. Do it. We need it."* (TED 2009). *"Safe is Risky. Cogs don't take risks. But cogs are the ones that are next to go."* (2002 blog). *"Because it's all we got."* (Impact Theory). The end is a hand on your shoulder pushing you toward action, often the shortest line in the piece.
- **Headers/lists/length:** blog posts are typically 100–400 words. Titles are short, lowercase-feeling, often a fragment or the named concept itself ("Raft up!", "When people become cogs", "The conversation"). He uses bullet lists sparingly and for contrast (what we *think* we get vs. what we *actually* get). Books are deliberately short — *The Dip* is 76 pages, and "quit padding" is itself the point.

## 5. Rhetorical devices (signature moves)
Full detail in `signature-moves.md`. The recognizable techniques:
- **The Named Pattern** — coin a sticky 1–3 word handle for an unnamed thing (Purple Cow, the Dip, sheepwalker, smallest viable audience, the lizard brain).
- **Intuition Inversion** — flip the reader's default ("safe is risky," "winners quit all the time," the smallest audience is the *safest* move).
- **The Concrete Proof-Picture** — pay off every abstraction with one picturable, real example (Table 4, the 55-foot lava lamp, Fancy Feast).
- **The Direct Address Imperative** — end on a short command to *you* (ship, start, go, do the work).
- **The Triad** — three short parallel beats, often closing ("Start. Do it. We need it.").
- **The Reclaimed Word** — take a familiar word and restore its literal meaning ("remarkable = worth making a remark about").
- **Borrowed Vocabulary** — import a precise word from outside marketing (otaku from Japanese, sonder from Koenig, sprezzatura from Italian, status roles from theatre).
- **The Diagnostic Question** — a single felt-sense test that replaces a dashboard ("If you disappeared, who would miss you?").

## 6. Tone & emotional register
- **Warmth:** present but unsentimental. It shows up *mechanically* as direct second-person care ("I'm really glad you're here") and as generosity of permission ("You don't need permission… but in case you do, here it is"). He roots for the reader.
- **Authority:** high, but earned through proof-of-work, not credentials — he argues from companies he built (Yoyodyne) and 7,100+ shipped posts, not from theory. The authority reads as *been-there*, not *above-you*.
- **Edge:** real. He'll flatly tell you uncomfortable things — *"you're probably not good enough — and no one is"* (Chase Jarvis); *"consumers don't care about you at all"* (TED 2003). The edge is always in service of the reader's growth, never contempt.
- **Humor:** dry, deadpan, self-deprecating. The X-ray specs riff; "I hate cats"; mocking his own SACD failure. Humor lowers the stakes so the lesson lands.
- **Vulnerability:** he volunteers his own failures as evidence — 800 rejection letters, the fortune-cookie construction set, turning down a billion dollars and admitting the doubt. Failure is shown, not hidden.
- **Generosity:** the dominant register. The whole posture is *here, take this, share it with your friends* — including literally ("Feel free to share").

## 7. Themes & worldview
The recurring mental models. If these are absent, it doesn't sound like him:
- **Remarkable or invisible** — average products for average people are now the riskiest play; the middle is death.
- **The factory deal is off** — the industrial bargain (comply, fit in, get security) is broken; the only safe job is the indispensable one (the **linchpin**).
- **Spread beats interrupt** — ideas win by being told friend-to-friend (permission, tribes, sneezers, the flipped funnel), not by buying attention.
- **The smallest viable audience** — serve a specific few obsessively; they become your sales force. Specificity over scale.
- **Empathy first / sonder** — *"you can't be seen until you learn to see"*; other people have a different noise in their head, and marketing starts there.
- **Emotional labor & art** — the work that matters is the work no manual can describe; "art is a personal gift that changes the recipient."
- **Generosity & trust** — do work *for* people, give away credit, build trust you never trade on.
- **Ship despite the fear** — the lizard brain / Resistance fights you hardest near the finish; *"welcome back, thanks for letting me know I'm onto something."*

## 8. Reader relationship
He imagines a single capable adult who is *stuck* — afraid to ship, hiding behind "I'm not worthy," waiting for permission. He treats that reader with **respect-by-default and zero condescension**: assumes intelligence, refuses to dumb down, and refuses to coddle. He flatters potential ("their genes are the same as yours") while puncturing excuses ("show me your bad writing"). This is why second-person *you* is everywhere and why the tone can be blunt without being cruel — he believes you can do the thing, and the bluntness is the belief made audible. He never writes down to a "mass"; he explicitly rejects the mass and writes to the one who cares.

## 9. Formatting & medium habits
- **Title style:** short, often a fragment or the bare concept ("The conversation," "Raft up!," "When people become cogs"). Lowercase, conversational, no clickbait numerals as a rule (though he'll mock listicle culture inside a post).
- **Length:** the daily blog post — usually a screen or less, 100–400 words. Books are short by design.
- **Evidence handling:** he cites by *naming the real thing* (the brand, the person, the price — "$112 yo-yo that sleeps for 12 minutes," "$5.82 espresso-machine ad") rather than footnotes or statistics. Numbers appear as vivid specifics, not data tables.
- **Visual habits:** generous white space; one sentence per paragraph; selective **bold** on the keeper line; parentheticals for asides; ellipses to trail off; the occasional bracketed `[note to reader]` ("Feel free to share."). Talks are slide-light and story-heavy.

## 10. Tells of authenticity
The hard-to-fake specifics that make it unmistakably him:
- **He names the thing, then defines it in-line.** Every coined term arrives with its own one-line gloss ("sonder, which means realizing that other people also have a noise in their head").
- **The one-sentence paragraph** as a rhythmic unit, used for emphasis, not by accident.
- **The self-deprecating deflection** at moments of praise ("Doesn't sound like anyone I know").
- **The failure offered as proof** — he reaches for *his own* losses (800 rejections, the SACD label) as the most persuasive evidence.
- **The blue-collar concrete example** for a white-collar idea — a 7-Eleven coffee server, a deli, a paint can, sliced bread.
- **The closing imperative that's the shortest line in the piece** ("Start. Do it.").
- **"This might not work"** as a recurring honesty marker — he flags risk instead of guaranteeing outcomes.
- **The flat, almost-rude opener** that he then redeems ("No one cares about you").

## 11. Anti-patterns (what they would NEVER do)
- **Never** lead with a hedge, a throat-clear, or a thesis-statement abstract. No "In this post I'll explore…"
- **Never** use corporate/marketing jargon unironically (scale, synergy, leverage, KPIs-as-buzzword, deep-dive, circle back).
- **Never** stack adjectives or reach for hype words ("revolutionary," "world-class," "best-in-class").
- **Never** write long, dense, multi-clause paragraphs with no white space. A wall of text is not him.
- **Never** bury the keeper line in the middle of a paragraph — it gets its own line.
- **Never** end on a generic upbeat motivational send-off ("So go out there and crush it!"). His ends are quiet, hard, and specific.
- **Never** guarantee success or sell a quick fix — he *mocks* "six paragraphs and you'll lose 30 pounds." He says "this might not work."
- **Never** pander to "the masses" or optimize for the largest audience. He writes to the few who care.
- **Never** explain a concept purely in the abstract without a picturable, real example attached.
- **Never** be smug or contemptuous toward the reader; the edge is always in service, never to win.

## 12. How to write AS Seth Godin (operational checklist)
1. **Open with a concrete hook in ≤12 words** — a small surprising fact, a flat assertion, or an odd specific. No throat-clearing, no thesis statement.
2. **Coin or deploy one named handle** for the idea (1–3 words) and **gloss it in the same breath**. The reader should leave with a word they can repeat.
3. **Invert the reader's intuition at least once.** Find the safe/obvious default and flip it ("the riskiest thing you can do now is be safe").
4. **Pay off every abstraction with one picturable, real example** — a named brand, person, or price. If you wrote a concept, the next sentence shows it.
5. **Keep load-bearing sentences to 6–14 words.** Let the punch lines be the shortest sentences on the page.
6. **Use one-sentence paragraphs and heavy white space.** Break before the keeper line so it stands alone.
7. **Address the reader as "you" and assume they're smart and capable.** Respect by default; never dumb it down, never coddle.
8. **Build the middle by accretion, not argument** — stack short, concrete beats; don't write a proof table or cite statistics as data.
9. **Use a triad or anaphora for emphasis** ("Start. Do it. We need it." / "Who are you upsetting? Who are you connecting? Who are you leading?").
10. **Strip all corporate jargon and hype adjectives.** If a word would appear in a pitch deck, cut it.
11. **Tell the truth about risk** — include a "this might not work" honesty marker; never guarantee, never sell a shortcut.
12. **Close on a short imperative or a quiet human turn** — push the reader toward action (ship, start, go, do the work that matters). Make it the shortest line.
13. **Offer it generously** — the implied stance is *here, take this, tell your friends*. No gatekeeping, no hard sell.
14. **If you can't cut a third of the words without losing meaning, cut harder.** Brevity is the discipline ("quit padding").

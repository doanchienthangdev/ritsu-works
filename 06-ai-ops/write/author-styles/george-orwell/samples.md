## The windowpane — prose as transparency

> "Good prose is like a windowpane."
> — *Why I Write* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The whole aesthetic in six words — and the sentence practices what it preaches: one homely object, one plain verb, nothing between the reader and the idea. Note that it comes immediately after a confession ("one can write nothing readable unless one constantly struggles to efface one's own personality"): transparency is presented as a *struggle*, not a gift.

---

> "What is above all needed is to let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about."
> — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The method behind the windowpane: think the thing first (he says to get the meaning clear "through pictures and sensations"), then hunt for the words that fit. The inversion in the second clause is his signature antithesis shape — the wrong way named in the same breath as the right one.

---

## The blunt verdict sentence — accumulation, then judgment

> "Nationalism is power hunger tempered by self-deception."
> — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)

**Demonstrates:** The earned aphorism: seven words closing a long analytical paragraph, every word Saxon-plain except the ones doing precise work. It survives out of context — which is the test of the form — but in context it lands because pages of specimens precede it.

---

> "In its net effect the meeting was a demonstration in favour of censorship."
> — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The verdict applied to witnessed events: he attends a meeting, reports what was actually said, and then closes the exhibit with one flat sentence of judgment. The pattern is reportage → verdict; the verdict is never allowed to arrive before the evidence.

---

> "All revolutions are failures, but they are not all the same failure."
> — *Arthur Koestler* (1944)

**Demonstrates:** The antithetical aphorism as argument-clincher — the second clause pivots on one repeated word ("failure") and rescues the sentence from cynicism by drawing the exact distinction that matters. Repetition of the plain word, not new vocabulary, does the work.

---

## The list of particulars — abstraction forced to show its contents

> "Defenceless villages are bombarded from the air, the inhabitants driven out into the countryside, the cattle machine-gunned, the huts set on fire with incendiary bullets: this is called *pacification*."
> — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The most famous euphemism demolition in English. Structure: particulars first (four concrete clauses, each visible), then the colon, then the soft official word exhibited last so the gap convicts itself. To write like Orwell about anything, master this order — facts, colon, the comfortable phrase.

---

> "I write it because there is some lie that I want to expose, some fact to which I want to draw attention, and my initial concern is to get a hearing."
> — *Why I Write* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The motive stated as plainly as a tradesman states his business. This is the engine of every Orwell piece: a specific lie, a specific fact, and the practical concern ("to get a hearing") admitted without embarrassment.

---

## The self-incriminating confession — candour as authority

> "I often wondered whether any of the others grasped that I had done it solely to avoid looking a fool."
> — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)

**Demonstrates:** The closing line of the essay — the whole machinery of empire reduced to one man's fear of being laughed at, and the man is himself. No summary follows; the confession IS the conclusion. This is his deepest move: the verdict aimed at the writer lands harder than any aimed at the world.

---

> "Look back through this essay, and for certain you will find that I have again and again committed the very faults I am protesting against."
> — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** Convicting himself of the essay's own charges, inside the essay. The candour is tactical as well as honest: after this sentence, no reader can dismiss him as superior, so the rules land as shared discipline rather than lecture.

---

> "All writers are vain, selfish, and lazy, and at the very bottom of their motives there lies a mystery."
> — *Why I Write* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The unflattering generalization that includes its author — three blunt adjectives, then the honest admission of what he doesn't know ("a mystery"). Hedged where knowledge runs out, unhedged about the unflattering part.

---

## The homely simile — one fresh physical image per abstraction

> "one turns as it were instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms, like a cuttlefish spurting out ink."
> — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** Insincerity made visible: a sea-creature hiding in its own discharge. The image is fresh (rule i), physical, and exact — the ink is the writer's own words. One image, fully seen, then back to argument.

---

> "A mass of Latin words falls upon the facts like soft snow, blurring the outlines and covering up all the details."
> — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** Diction critique as weather: you can watch the syllables fall and the facts disappear. Note the verbs — *falls, blurring, covering* — doing the analytical work that adjectives would botch.

---

> "Political writing in our time consists almost entirely of prefabricated phrases bolted together like the pieces of a child's Meccano set."
> — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The toolshed register: a child's construction toy stands for the whole machinery of orthodox prose. Homely, slightly contemptuous, and mechanically precise — *bolted*, not "combined."

---

## The stipulative definition — pin the word, then argue

> "By 'patriotism' I mean devotion to a particular place and a particular way of life, which one believes to be the best in the world but has no wish to force on other people."
> — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)

**Demonstrates:** The "By 'X' I mean" formula that opens his taxonomies: the contested word is pinned down before any argument is allowed to use it. The definition itself carries the antithesis (devotion vs. force) that the essay will spend its length proving.

---

> "Freedom of the intellect means the freedom to report what one has seen, heard, and felt, and not to be obliged to fabricate imaginary facts and feelings."
> — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The operational definition: an abstraction cashed out in sensory verbs — *seen, heard, felt* — and its negation stated concretely (*fabricate imaginary facts*). If a definition can't be tested against experience, he won't use it.

---

## Hedged facts, unhedged morals — the calibration asymmetry

> "It may be an exaggeration, but it cannot be a very great one, to say that whenever a book dealing with totalitarianism appears in this country, and still seems worth reading six months after publication, it is a book translated from some foreign language."
> — *Arthur Koestler* (1944)

**Demonstrates:** The concede-then-assert: the hedge ("It may be an exaggeration") is immediately bounded ("but it cannot be a very great one"), so the concession strengthens the claim instead of weakening it. Calibration as armor.

---

> "The great enemy of clear language is insincerity. When there is a gap between one's real and one's declared aims, one turns as it were instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms"
> — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The unhedged moral diagnosis — no "perhaps," no "arguably" — followed at once by the mechanism (the gap between real and declared aims). He states the moral flatly and then explains it concretely; never the reverse.

---

## The six rules — the craft floor, held with judgment

> "i. Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print. … iii. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out. iv. Never use the passive where you can use the active. … vi. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous."
> — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

**Demonstrates:** The most-quoted writing rules in English, and the tell is rule vi: the discipline is held with judgment, not piety. Imperative mood, no hedging, each rule itself obeying the rules (count the syllables).

---

## The narrative windowpane — scene-writing with exact figures

> "In Moulmein, in lower Burma, I was hated by large numbers of people – the only time in my life that I have been important enough for this to happen to me."
> — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)

**Demonstrates:** The witnessed opening: place named twice, then the self-deflating turn that makes the narrator trustworthy in one stroke. The irony ("important enough") cuts the writer, not the crowd.

---

> "Alive, the elephant was worth at least a hundred pounds; dead, he would only be worth the value of his tusks, five pounds, possibly."
> — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)

**Demonstrates:** Moral arithmetic: the stakes of the decision priced in pounds sterling, balanced across a semicolon, with the honest wobble ("possibly") exactly where his knowledge runs out. The numbers are the argument.

---

> "He wears a mask, and his face grows to fit it."
> — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)

**Demonstrates:** The aphorism rising out of narrative — nine words, two clauses, one image, a whole theory of institutional character. It lands because two pages of concrete scene precede it; quoted alone it still works, which is the test.

---

## The implicating close — end cold

> "The moral to be drawn from this dangerous nightmare situation is a simple one: Don't let it happen. It depends on you."
> — Orwell's 1949 statement on *Nineteen Eighty-Four*

**Demonstrates:** The hardest close in his register: colon setup, then two imperative sentences of four and four words, second person, no consolation. The book's entire purpose compressed into a direct order to the reader.

---

> "All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others."
> — *Animal Farm* (1945)

**Demonstrates:** Irony by minimal means: repeat one plain word ("equal") and corrupt it with a single illogical comparative ("more equal"). No new vocabulary, no commentary — the corruption of the sentence enacts the corruption of the revolution. The most efficient satirical sentence in the language.

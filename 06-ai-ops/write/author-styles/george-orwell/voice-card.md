# George Orwell — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A windowpane plain-stylist with a moral motive — he opens on something witnessed, names the lie he means to expose, builds the case as comma-strung lists of concrete particulars in short Saxon words and active voice, lands each paragraph on a blunt unhedged verdict sentence, convicts himself before anyone else, and ends cold with the reader implicated.

**Rhythm rule:** Accumulation, then verdict. Let a long sentence gather concrete evidence clause by clause, then land a five-to-ten-word judgment that could survive being quoted alone ("Nationalism is power hunger tempered by self-deception."). Vary long with short; never two ornate sentences in a row.
**Diction rule:** The short Saxon word and the concrete noun. *Lie, decent, humbug, swindle, rubbish* do the judging; windowpanes, cuttlefish, tea-leaves and Meccano sets do the imagining; exact counts and prices do the proving. Never *utilize*, never *leverage*, never a phrase you've seen in print. (His dash-interpolations render as parentheses, a colon, or paired commas — the humanizer strips dashes.)
**Reader rule:** Write to a decent, intelligent equal who can face unpleasant facts with you. Never flatter, never sell, never console. Implicate yourself and the reader together; save second person for the moral climax.

## Do (top 7)
1. **Find the lie first** — the piece exists because "there is some lie that I want to expose, some fact to which I want to draw attention." Name the comfortable falsehood, then take it apart.
2. **Open on the witnessed particular or the flat contrarian declarative** — "In Moulmein, in lower Burma, I was hated by large numbers of people"; or state the received opinion and turn on it. No warm-up.
3. **Let the meaning choose the word** — picture the thing wordlessly, then use the fewest, shortest words that cover it: short over long, active over passive, everyday over jargon, cut every cuttable word.
4. **Build with lists of particulars** — where an abstraction tempts you, exhibit its contents: the bombarded villages, the machine-gunned cattle, the huts on fire. Then say what the soft phrase ("pacification") was built to hide.
5. **Land the blunt verdict sentence** — close each major paragraph with a short, unhedged judgment: "In its net effect the meeting was a demonstration in favour of censorship."
6. **Hedge the facts, never the morals** — "so far as one can judge," "probably" on the evidence; no hedge at all on the verdict. And confess first: convict your own side, your own habits, yourself, before judging anyone else.
7. **Use one homely physical simile per abstraction** — a cuttlefish spurting ink, Latin words falling like soft snow, phrases "tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house." Fresh, domestic, actually seen.

## Don't (top 7)
1. **Euphemize** — never the soft institutional phrase where the concrete fact should stand; naming things plainly is the whole ethic.
2. **Use the prefabricated phrase** — dying metaphors (*toe the line, Achilles' heel*), verbal false limbs (*render inoperative, make contact with*), or any figure you're used to seeing in print.
3. **Dress up the simple statement** — no Latinate dignity (*utilize, ameliorate, constitute*), no meaningless words (*vitality, values, world-class*), no foreign phrase where everyday English exists.
4. **Hide agency in the passive** — someone does the thing; name who. The agentless passive is the grammar of evasion.
5. **Decorate** — purple passages, decorative adjectives and "humbug generally" are his named failure mode; if it's there for the sound, cut it.
6. **End upbeat** — no summary, no pep talk, no consolation. End on the verdict, the confession, or the imperative.
7. **Invent the witnessed scene or the number.** His authority is that he was there and counted. Keep the mechanics on any topic; never counterfeit the testimony or the statistic.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The witnessed scene, first person, placed and dated — "In Moulmein, in lower Burma, I was hated by large numbers of people – the only time in my life that I have been important enough for this to happen to me." — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)
- The flat contrarian declarative — "Most people who bother with the matter at all would admit that the English language is in a bad way, but it is generally assumed that we cannot by conscious action do anything about it." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)
- The eyewitness report that becomes the argument — "About a year ago I attended a meeting of the P.E.N. Club" — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The self-incriminating confession — "I often wondered whether any of the others grasped that I had done it solely to avoid looking a fool." — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)
- The implicating *we* — "they are part of the make-up of most of us, whether we like it or not." — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)
- The hard imperative — "Don't let it happen. It depends on you." — Orwell's 1949 statement on *Nineteen Eighty-Four*

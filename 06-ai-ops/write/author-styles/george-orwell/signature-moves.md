## 1. The Windowpane (the principle under everything)

**What:** Efface the writer so the subject shows through. Prose succeeds when the reader sees the thing and forgets the words — which means the meaning must be settled first, wordlessly, and only then dressed in the fewest, shortest words that cover it. The opposite of style-as-performance: style as transparency.

**When:** Always — it's the standing order. Most operative at revision: any phrase that draws attention to itself, any word there for the sound, any borrowed elegance, gets cut.

**Example:** "Good prose is like a windowpane." — *Why I Write* (1946)
**Example:** "What is above all needed is to let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)
**Example:** "In prose, the worst thing one can do with words is to surrender to them." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

---

## 2. The Blunt Verdict Sentence (his most recognizable beat)

**What:** After a paragraph of patiently accumulated evidence, a short, flat, unhedged judgment — five to twelve words, plain Saxon diction, compressed enough to survive being quoted alone. The aphorism is *earned*: it never appears before the particulars that justify it.

**When:** The close of any major paragraph or section; the close of the whole piece. If a paragraph ends on a qualification or a wind-down, it isn't finished.

**Example:** "Nationalism is power hunger tempered by self-deception." — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)
**Example:** "In its net effect the meeting was a demonstration in favour of censorship." — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)
**Example:** "All revolutions are failures, but they are not all the same failure." — *Arthur Koestler* (1944)

---

## 3. The List of Particulars (evidence as exhibit)

**What:** Demolish an abstraction by exhibiting what it actually consists of — concrete instances, comma-strung, forced inside the sentence so the reader must look at each one. The abstraction is never allowed to float; it is made to show its contents.

**When:** Whenever a big noun (oppression, engagement, progress, efficiency) is doing work that specific facts should do. Also his indictment form: the charge sheet is a list.

**Example:** "torture, the use of hostages, forced labour, mass deportations, imprisonment without trial, forgery, assassination, the bombing of civilians" — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)
**Example:** "The wretched prisoners huddling in the stinking cages of the lock-ups, the grey, cowed faces of the long-term convicts, the scarred buttocks of the men who had been Bogged with bamboos – all these oppressed me with an intolerable sense of guilt." — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)
**Example:** "He is worn down by solitude, toothache, lack of tobacco, bright lights glaring in his eyes, and continuous questioning" — *Arthur Koestler* (1944), the grand and the homely in one list

---

## 4. The Euphemism Demolition (calling things by their names)

**What:** Quote the soft institutional phrase, then state the concrete facts it was built to hide — the particulars first, the euphemism exhibited after, so the gap between word and thing convicts itself. The companion move holds cant phrases in single scare quotes, like tongs.

**When:** Whenever official, corporate, or fashionable language is concealing an unpleasant fact. The signature Orwell service: translation from euphemism back into English.

**Example:** "Defenceless villages are bombarded from the air, the inhabitants driven out into the countryside, the cattle machine-gunned, the huts set on fire with incendiary bullets: this is called *pacification*." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)
**Example:** "Political language… is designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable, and to give an appearance of solidity to pure wind." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)
**Example:** "The inflated style is itself a kind of euphemism. A mass of Latin words falls upon the facts like soft snow, blurring the outlines and covering up all the details." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)

---

## 5. The Self-Incriminating Confession (candour as authority)

**What:** Convict yourself first. He puts his own guilt, vanity, bias, or rule-breaking on the page before judging anyone else's — which is exactly why the judgments land as honesty rather than superiority. The confession is structural, not decorative: whole essays pivot on it.

**When:** Early, to establish the right to judge; or at the close, as the final verdict aimed at himself. Any time the writer's own side or own habits are part of the problem being diagnosed.

**Example:** "I often wondered whether any of the others grasped that I had done it solely to avoid looking a fool." — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936), the closing line
**Example:** "Look back through this essay, and for certain you will find that I have again and again committed the very faults I am protesting against." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)
**Example:** "All writers are vain, selfish, and lazy, and at the very bottom of their motives there lies a mystery." — *Why I Write* (1946)

---

## 6. The Homely Simile (one fresh physical image per abstraction)

**What:** A mental or political process rendered as one domestic, physical, actually-seen image — drawn from kitchens, toolsheds, weather, animals. Never two images at once (a mixed metaphor proves "the writer is not seeing a mental image of the objects he is naming"), never one he's seen in print.

**When:** Once per abstraction that needs making visible. The test is visual: if you can't see it happen, it isn't his image.

**Example:** "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts" set right by the drinker figure: "A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)
**Example:** "one turns as it were instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms, like a cuttlefish spurting out ink." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946)
**Example:** "Political writing in our time consists almost entirely of prefabricated phrases bolted together like the pieces of a child's Meccano set." — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)
**Example:** "the imagination, like certain wild animals, will not breed in captivity" — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)

---

## 7. The Stipulative Definition (lock the terms, then argue)

**What:** Define the contested word before using it — "By 'X' I mean…" — and prefer operational definitions in plain sensory verbs: what one can see, hear, feel, report. The argument only starts once the words are pinned down; undefined big words are how swindles get in.

**When:** The top of any piece whose key term is fashionable, contested, or vague. Also mid-essay, whenever a new abstraction enters.

**Example:** "By 'patriotism' I mean devotion to a particular place and a particular way of life, which one believes to be the best in the world but has no wish to force on other people." — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)
**Example:** "By 'nationalism' I mean first of all the habit of assuming that human beings can be classified like insects" — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)
**Example:** "Freedom of the intellect means the freedom to report what one has seen, heard, and felt, and not to be obliged to fabricate imaginary facts and feelings." — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)

---

## 8. The Concede-then-Assert (the honest hedge that strengthens)

**What:** Grant the objection in full — even state your claim's weakness for it — then re-assert with more force. Empirical claims get calibrated hedges ("probably," "so far as one can judge"); moral verdicts get none. The asymmetry is the fingerprint: scrupulous about facts, immovable about right and wrong.

**When:** Any sweeping generalization; any claim a fair reader would test. Concede before they can object, and the assertion that follows is armored.

**Example:** "It may be an exaggeration, but it cannot be a very great one, to say that whenever a book dealing with totalitarianism appears in this country, and still seems worth reading six months after publication, it is a book translated from some foreign language." — *Arthur Koestler* (1944)
**Example:** "Russian literature, so far as one can judge by translations, has deteriorated markedly since the early days of the revolution" — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)
**Example:** "Totalitarianism demands, in fact, the continuous alteration of the past, and in the long run probably demands a disbelief in the very existence of objective truth." — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)

---

## 9. The Six-Rules Discipline (the craft floor)

**What:** His own published rules for prose, applied as a revision pass: (i) never use a figure of speech you're used to seeing in print; (ii) never a long word where a short one will do; (iii) if it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out; (iv) never the passive where you can use the active; (v) never a foreign phrase, scientific word or jargon word if an everyday English equivalent exists; (vi) break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. Rule vi is the tell — the rules are held with judgment, not piety.

**When:** Every sentence, on revision. Paired with his six questions: "What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect? … Could I put it more shortly? Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?"

**Example:** "Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946), rule i
**Example:** "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946), rule iii
**Example:** "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946), rule vi

---

## 10. The Exact Figure as Proof (count it, price it)

**What:** Claims clinched by arithmetic and prices, not adjectives — he counts the syllables, names the pounds sterling, measures the trench. The number is always real and always load-bearing; it is how the windowpane stays honest.

**When:** Any comparative claim (better, worse, simpler, costlier) that could be settled by counting. If you can count it, count it; if you can't, don't fake it.

**Example:** "The first contains 49 words but only 60 syllables, and all its words are those of everyday life. The second contains 38 words of 90 syllables: 18 of its words are from Latin roots, and one from Greek." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946), the Ecclesiastes demonstration
**Example:** "Alive, the elephant was worth at least a hundred pounds; dead, he would only be worth the value of his tusks, five pounds, possibly." — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)
**Example:** "his face had scored a trench a foot deep and a couple of yards long" — *Shooting an Elephant* (1936)

---

## 11. Irony Tongs & Litotes (the controlled sneer)

**What:** Cant phrases exhibited in single scare quotes — held at arm's length like specimens — and explosive content delivered in deliberate understatement. The contempt is in the precision, never in volume; the deadpan one-liner does the demolition.

**When:** Quoting the opposition's vocabulary; delivering a devastating aside; any moment a lesser writer would shout.

**Example:** "No tirades against 'individualism' and the 'ivory tower', no pious platitudes to the effect that 'true individuality is only attained through identification with the community', can get over the fact that a bought mind is a spoiled mind" — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)
**Example:** "The fact is that certain themes cannot be celebrated in words, and tyranny is one of them. No one ever wrote a good book in praise of the Inquisition." — *The Prevention of Literature* (1946)
**Example:** "It is not merely that 'power corrupts': so also do the ways of attaining power." — *Arthur Koestler* (1944)

---

## 12. The Implicating Close (end cold, convict everyone)

**What:** No summary, no uplift. The essay ends on a verdict, a confession, or an imperative — and the diagnosis includes the writer and the reader ("we," "most of us," "you"). The warmth, such as it is, is the respect of being told the truth straight.

**When:** The final paragraph, always. If the draft ends by reassuring anyone, it isn't finished.

**Example:** "they are part of the make-up of most of us, whether we like it or not." — *Notes on Nationalism* (1945)
**Example:** "one can even, if one jeers loudly enough, send some worn-out and useless phrase… into the dustbin where it belongs." — *Politics and the English Language* (1946), the closing sentence
**Example:** "The moral to be drawn from this dangerous nightmare situation is a simple one: Don't let it happen. It depends on you." — Orwell's 1949 statement on *Nineteen Eighty-Four*

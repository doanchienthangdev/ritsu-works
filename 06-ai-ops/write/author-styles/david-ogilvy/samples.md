## Specificity over adjectives — the fact IS the persuasion

> "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
> — Rolls-Royce ad headline, 1958 (read aloud by Ogilvy in *The View From Touffou*, 1982)

**Demonstrates:** The signature move — a measured, checkable detail in place of a mood word. Note the exactness: a speed, a single named sound. The reader cannot dismiss a measurement the way she can dismiss "quiet."

---

> "Good ad. All facts, no adjectives. All specific. Sold a lot of cars."
> — *A Conversation About Advertising*, 1977

**Demonstrates:** Four clipped fragments, no connective tissue, evidence as the final word ("sold a lot of cars"). This is how Ogilvy *praises* — by stating what a thing did, not how it felt. The rhythm is staccato and declarative.

---

> "Here's a model of an AGA. It's the most expensive cooking stove in the world and by far the best. It used to take me half an hour to make the sale. That's about 3,000 words."
> — Ogilvy (archival), *Salesman: The Early Years*

**Demonstrates:** Plain claim, then immediately a number that anchors it ("3,000 words"). Even a boast gets quantified. The voice reaches for the unit, always.

## Respect for the reader — one intelligent adult

> "The consumer is not a moron. She is your wife."
> — *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, 1963

**Demonstrates:** Nine words, two sentences, a premise stated as flat fact. The most-quoted Ogilvy line because it is maximally compressed and impossible to argue with. The second sentence reframes the whole relationship.

---

> "Do not address your readers as though they were gathered together in a stadium. When people read your copy, they are alone."
> — quoted in Copyblogger, "13 Timeless Lessons from the Father of Advertising"

**Demonstrates:** Negative instruction first (name the wrong way), then the corrective image ("they are alone"). The cognitive move from "audience" to "single reader," delivered in two short, balanced sentences.

---

> "I don't sing to her in rhyming couplets… I give her rational reasons why she should buy a Mercedes-Benz. I don't insult her intelligence."
> — *A Conversation About Advertising*, 1977

**Demonstrates:** The dinner-party frame in action — one woman, one question, a plain answer. Notice the repeated "I don't…": Ogilvy defines his method by what he refuses to do.

## Naming the enemy

> "They worship at the altar of creativity — which really means originality — the most dangerous word in the lexicon of advertising."
> — *We Sell — Or Else*, c. 1985

**Demonstrates:** Coin the villain ("the most dangerous word"), define it in passing, deliver with mock-religious imagery ("worship at the altar"). The sentence is an indictment dressed as an observation.

---

> "You cannot bore people into buying your product."
> — *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, 1963

**Demonstrates:** Boring named as the structural enemy, in seven words, as an absolute. No qualifier, no "usually." Ogilvy's standards arrive as laws, not suggestions.

---

> "Never use jargon words like reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally. They are the hallmarks of a pretentious ass."
> — *How to Write* memo, 7 September 1982

**Demonstrates:** The enemy is named *by example* (four exact jargon words) and then *by character* ("a pretentious ass"). Diagnosis, not stylistic preference — bad words signal a bad mind.

## Portable rules — the numbered commandment

> "2. Write the way you talk. Naturally. 3. Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs… 5. Never write more than two pages on any subject… 10. If you want ACTION, don't write. Go and tell the guy what you want."
> — *How to Write* memo, 1982

**Demonstrates:** Operational imperatives, numbered, each one applicable the same workday. The memo obeys its own rules — short words, one page, the action unmistakable. Form is the proof of content.

---

> "Boil down your strategy to one simple promise, and go the whole hog in delivering that promise."
> — *The View From Touffou*, 1982

**Demonstrates:** A command built from homely idiom ("go the whole hog") rather than business abstraction. Ogilvy makes a strategic instruction sound like advice from a plain-spoken uncle.

## The vivid single image

> "A lot of people in our trade use research the way a drunkard uses a lamp post — for support, not for illumination."
> — *The View From Touffou*, 1982

**Demonstrates:** One everyday image (a drunkard, a lamp post) carries an entire critique. The contrast pair "support, not illumination" is the payload; the picture makes it memorable and portable.

---

> "If each of us hires someone smaller than we are, we shall become a company of dwarfs. But if each of us hires someone bigger than we are, we shall become a company of giants."
> — the Russian-doll memo, read aloud in *The View From Touffou*, 1982

**Demonstrates:** Perfect parallel construction (dwarfs / giants), an if-then symmetry, a physical metaphor he literally handed people inside nested dolls. The idea is engineered to be carried out of the room intact.

## British wit with a blade in it

> "When the client moans and sighs, make his logo twice the size. / If he still should prove refractory, show a picture of his factory."
> — light verse, *The View From Touffou*, 1982

**Demonstrates:** Charm on the surface, a hard truth about committee meddling underneath. Ogilvy disarms with rhyme, then lands the serious point — the humour is the delivery vehicle, never a substitute for the standard.

---

> "Don't bunt. Aim out of the ball park. Aim for the company of immortals."
> — *A Conversation About Advertising*, 1977 (and *Ogilvy on Advertising*, 1983)

**Demonstrates:** Three short imperatives escalating from a baseball metaphor to "the immortals." Ogilvy ends on ambition delivered as a clipped command — witty in image, ruthless in standard.

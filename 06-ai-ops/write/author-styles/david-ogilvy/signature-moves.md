# David Ogilvy — Signature Moves

## The Specific Fact as Headline
**What:** Lead with a measured, checkable detail — a number, a unit, a sourced observation — never an adjective or a mood. The persuasion lives in the precision: "at 60 miles an hour," not "very quiet."
**When:** The opening line of any persuasive piece, or any moment you'd otherwise be tempted to write "amazing," "world-class," or "revolutionary." Replace the adjective with the fact underneath it.
**Example:** "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." — Rolls-Royce ad, 1958 (read aloud by Ogilvy, *The View From Touffou*, 1982). His own gloss: "All facts, no adjectives. All specific. Sold a lot of cars." (*A Conversation About Advertising*, 1977)

## "The consumer is not a moron" — Respect the Reader
**What:** Address one intelligent adult, give them the real information, refuse to talk down. State the respect as a flat premise, then write as if the reader can tell when they're being patronized — because they can.
**When:** Any time the draft starts hyping, dumbing-down, or hiding the substance behind cleverness. Use it to justify *more* facts and *fewer* tricks.
**Example:** "The consumer is not a moron. She is your wife." — *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, 1963. Operationalized in his door-to-door days: "Don't insult her intelligence and don't shock her. A lot of consumers are not as sophisticated as you are." (AGA sales manual, quoted in *Salesman: The Early Years*)

## Write the Way You Talk — One Person, One Chair
**What:** Drop the broadcast register. Picture a single reader alone, or a dinner-party neighbour who just asked your advice, and answer *that* person in plain Anglo-Saxon words and short sentences. Imagine the doorbell and the housewife.
**When:** Whenever the prose starts sounding like a press release, a stadium announcement, or a committee. The fix is to read it aloud and cut anything you wouldn't say out loud to one friend.
**Example:** "Do not address your readers as though they were gathered together in a stadium. When people read your copy, they are alone." (quoted in Copyblogger, "13 Timeless Lessons"). And the test he gave his own staff: "If I rang your doorbell and started singing at you, you'd think a lunatic was outside." (*A Conversation About Advertising*, 1977)

## Name the Enemy
**What:** Don't define the good by describing it — define it by naming, precisely and often, the failure mode that destroys it. Boring. Clowning. Jargon. The committee. The copy-cat. The pretentious ass. The success mode then self-organizes around the named enemy.
**When:** Setting standards, writing a manifesto, a brief, or any "how to do X well" piece. Coin a sharp label for the thing to avoid and the reader instantly knows which side of the line they're on.
**Example:** "They worship at the altar of creativity — which really means originality — the most dangerous word in the lexicon of advertising." (*We Sell — Or Else*, c. 1985). And in the writing memo: jargon words "are the hallmarks of a pretentious ass." (*How to Write* memo, 1982)

## The Numbered Commandment / Portable Rule
**What:** Compress hard-won judgment into a short, numbered, imperative list a junior could pin to the wall on Monday and apply by Friday. Each item is operational ("Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs"), never aspirational ("be clear").
**When:** Closing an argument, handing down a method, or any time you want the reader to *do* something rather than admire something. End the piece with the checklist, not the philosophy.
**Example:** From the *How to Write* memo, 1982: "2. Write the way you talk. Naturally. 3. Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs… 5. Never write more than two pages on any subject… 10. If you want ACTION, don't write. Go and tell the guy what you want." The memo obeys its own rules: one page, plain words, the action unmistakable.

## The Worked Example as Argument
**What:** Don't assert the rule — install it by telling the story that proves it. The Hathaway eyepatch, the three weeks reading Rolls-Royce manuals, the $30,000 budget, the wagon dreamed at 2 a.m. The anecdote carries the lesson so the reader never has to be lectured.
**When:** You have a claim that would sound like a platitude stated bare ("research pays," "specificity sells"). Reach for the concrete case — ideally with a name, a number, and a result — and let it do the persuading.
**Example:** "When we were hired by Rolls-Royce I spent three weeks reading up on Rolls-Royce, and came across that observation… All facts, no adjectives. All specific. Sold a lot of cars." (*A Conversation About Advertising*, 1977). He argues from the file, never from theory.

## The Vivid Single-Image Metaphor
**What:** Hand the reader one picture that contains the whole idea — a Russian doll for hiring, a drunkard's lamp post for misused research, a ship passing in the night for a forgettable campaign, an empty church for unread copy. One image; the abstraction becomes portable and memorable.
**When:** A concept is too dry or too large to land as a sentence. Find the everyday object that *is* the idea and let the reader carry it out of the room.
**Example:** "A lot of people in our trade use research the way a drunkard uses a lamp post — for support, not for illumination." (*The View From Touffou*, 1982). On the same principle: "Unless your advertising contains a Big Idea, it will pass like a ship in the night." (*Ogilvy on Advertising*, 1983)

## British Wit with a Blade in It
**What:** Defuse authority with self-deprecation and dry humour, then land the serious point harder for the disarming. The joke is the delivery vehicle; the standard underneath is uncompromising. He'll tell you he scored 96 on his own IQ test, then insist you "compete with the immortals."
**When:** You're about to issue a demanding standard and want it to be heard rather than resented — or you want to puncture pomposity (your own included) before making a non-negotiable claim.
**Example:** Light verse for a difficult client: "When the client moans and sighs, make his logo twice the size. / If he still should prove refractory, show a picture of his factory." (*The View From Touffou*, 1982) — charm on the surface, a hard truth about committee meddling underneath. And the closing exhortation, witty but ruthless: "Don't bunt. Aim out of the ball park. Aim for the company of immortals." (*A Conversation About Advertising*, 1977)

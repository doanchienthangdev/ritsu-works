# Paul Graham — Signature Moves

## 1. The Coined Handle (his most recognizable move)

**What:** Isolate a fuzzy, real pattern and give it a short, plain, sticky name the reader can pick up and reuse. The name *is* the payload — once you have the handle, you can see the pattern everywhere and talk about it in three words. This is the single most Paul-Graham thing about his essays: he doesn't just describe; he *names*.

**When:** The moment a pattern crystallizes out of the examples — usually mid-essay, right after the concrete cases that earned it. Reach for it when you've found something true that doesn't yet have a name. Never coin one for a non-pattern; an unearned handle is just branding, and he'd hear the falseness.

**Example:** "One of the most common types of advice we give at Y Combinator is to do things that don't scale." — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)
**Example:** "The best plan is to let as few things into your identity as possible." (the essay that named *keep your identity small*) — *Keep Your Identity Small* (2009)
**Example:** "I think most people have one top idea in their mind at any given time." (the *top idea in your mind*) — *The Top Idea in Your Mind* (2010)

## 2. The Essay as Discovery (write to figure it out, not to defend)

**What:** Treat the essay as a tool for finding something out, not a container for a conclusion you already had. He refuses the school-debate shape — pick a thesis, marshal three arguments, defend. Instead he starts from a question and lets the writing do the thinking, so the reader watches the idea get discovered in real time. This is why his pieces feel alive: even *he* didn't know exactly where they'd land.

**When:** The default architecture for any genuinely exploratory piece. Use it whenever the honest situation is "I'm trying to work out whether X is true," rather than "I already know X and want to convince you."

**Example:** "An essay is something you write to try to figure something out." — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)
**Example:** "In a real essay, you don't take a position and defend it." — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)
**Example:** "Expressing ideas helps to form them." — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)

---

## 3. Write Like You Talk (the prose mechanic under everything)

**What:** Write in spoken language — the plain words and contractions you'd use explaining something to a friend — and run every sentence through the friend-test. The clincher is auditory: if you wrote something you wouldn't actually say, you can hear it clank. Formality is not a sign of seriousness; it's usually a sign of someone hiding behind the words.

**When:** Every sentence, on revision. It's the floor the whole voice stands on: do this and most of the AI-flavored stiffness disappears on its own.

**Example:** "Is this the way I'd say this if I were talking to a friend?" — *Write Like You Talk* (2015)
**Example:** "Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas." — *Write Like You Talk* (2015)
**Example:** "If you simply manage to write in spoken language, you'll be ahead of 95% of writers." — *Write Like You Talk* (2015)

---

## 4. Aim for Surprise (the reason the piece exists)

**What:** Build the essay around the thing that surprised *you*, because what surprised you, who've thought hard about the topic, will surprise the reader too. A piece that only confirms what the reader already believed has no reason to exist. The test of usefulness is whether you've told them something true and important they didn't already know.

**When:** At the planning stage and again at the cutting stage — find the single most surprising true thing the piece contains and make sure it's at the center, not buried. If you can't find one, the piece isn't ready.

**Example:** "Essays should aim for maximum surprise." — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)
**Example:** "Useful writing tells people something true and important that they didn't already know, and tells them as unequivocally as possible." — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)
**Example:** "Anything you notice that surprises you, who've thought about the topic a lot, will probably also surprise a significant number of readers." — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)

---

## 5. The Concrete Real Example (the specific carries the general)

**What:** Cash out every abstraction in a specific, true, often named case. He doesn't say "engage your users early"; he tells you Airbnb's founders went door to door in New York and Stripe set people up on the spot. The example does the persuading, and because it's real and checkable, it carries an authority no generalization can. The corollary discipline: the example must be *true* — inventing one is the unforgivable move.

**When:** Right after any claim that floats above the ground. The rhythm is *claim → "for example" → real case*, and the case is usually more memorable than the claim.

**Example:** "The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually." (then: Airbnb door-to-door; Stripe's laptop) — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)
**Example:** "When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot." — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)
**Example:** "Just take a shower. What topic do your thoughts keep returning to?" (the abstract claim about attention, made testable on yourself) — *The Top Idea in Your Mind* (2010)

---

## 6. First-Principles "Why Is This Actually True?"

**What:** Refuse the received answer and reason the thing out from the ground up. He asks the obvious question everyone skips — *why does this actually happen?* — and follows it until he hits bedrock, even when the bedrock is unfashionable. The essays often open at exactly this moment: the instant a long-standing puzzle finally resolves.

**When:** Whenever there's a settled explanation that everyone repeats but nobody has checked. Use it to reopen a closed question and to earn a genuinely new claim.

**Example:** "I finally realized today why politics and religion yield such uniquely useless discussions." — *Keep Your Identity Small* (2009)
**Example:** "I think what religion and politics have in common is that they become part of people's identity." — *Keep Your Identity Small* (2009)
**Example:** "Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off." (rejecting the passive "startups take off" folk-explanation) — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)

---

## 7. The Honest Qualifier (carefulness as authority)

**What:** Calibrate every claim to exactly how much he believes it — "I think," "probably," "most," "in my experience," "I may be wrong." Far from weakening the prose, the qualifier is what makes it trustworthy: he's clearly reporting what he found, not selling a certainty he doesn't have. He'll then turn around and state the genuinely settled thing flatly and unequivocally, so the contrast tells you what's load-bearing.

**When:** On every claim whose confidence is less than total — which is most of them. Pair it with the unequivocal statement of the few things you *are* sure of.

**Example:** "I think most people have one top idea in their mind at any given time." — *The Top Idea in Your Mind* (2010)
**Example:** "To be happy I think you have to be doing something you not only enjoy, but admire." — *How to Do What You Love* (2006)
**Example (the unequivocal counterweight):** "Useful writing… tells them as unequivocally as possible." — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)

---

## 8. The Contrarian Inversion (the obvious approach is backwards)

**What:** Find the place where the standard, obvious approach is actually wrong, and invert it. The thesis is usually contrarian on first read and obvious on reflection — which is exactly the signature of a useful idea. He likes the verb-swap especially: replacing the wrong mental action with the right one.

**When:** When the common advice is not just incomplete but pointed the wrong way. Use it as a thesis engine — the inversion is often the whole essay.

**Example:** "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems, preferably problems you have yourself." — *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012)
**Example:** "The verb you want to be using with respect to startup ideas is not 'think up' but 'notice.'" — *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012)
**Example:** "Do what you love doesn't mean, do what you would like to do most this second." — *How to Do What You Love* (2006)

---

## 9. The Reorganizing Analogy (one homely picture resets the view)

**What:** Drop a single homely comparison that reorganizes how the reader sees the thing — not decoration, but a new pair of glasses. The analogy is always ordinary (clothing, a magnet, a river), never clever for its own sake, and he usually reasons one step inside it before moving on. One good analogy per idea is enough; more would be showing off.

**When:** When an abstract relationship is hard to hold in the head and a familiar physical image can carry it. Reach for the *plainest* picture that fits, because the power is in the familiarity.

**Example:** "Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas." — *Write Like You Talk* (2015)
**Example:** "Prestige is like a powerful magnet that warps even your beliefs about what you enjoy." — *How to Do What You Love* (2006)
**Example (the river, on essay structure):** "The path it has discovered is the most economical route to the sea." (an essay flows toward what's interesting the way water finds its way down) — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)

---

## 10. Compression (cut everything unnecessary)

**What:** Write a deliberately bad first draft fast, then rewrite it over and over, cutting everything that isn't pulling weight. The published essay is what survived the cutting. Strength comes from removal, not addition — and the willingness not to publish at all if you can't get it right is part of the discipline.

**When:** Every revision pass. The first draft is for discovering what you think; the rest of the passes are for deleting everything that isn't it.

**Example:** "Write a bad version 1 as fast as you can; rewrite it over and over; cut out everything unnecessary…" — *Writing, Briefly* (2005)
**Example:** "the best advice is to write as simply as you can." — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)
**Example:** "You don't have to publish the essay at all, if you can't get it right." — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)

---

## 11. The Earnest Builder's Close (a small thing to go do)

**What:** End not on a joke and not on a tidy summary, but on a quiet widening or a small imperative the reader can act on. The implicit message is always *you can see this too, and once you do, you can go do something with it.* It's optimistic without being a pep talk, because it hands over an action or a slightly larger frame rather than an exhortation.

**When:** The final beat of almost any piece. Land on the thing the reader can carry out of the essay and use.

**Example:** "Live in the future, then build what's missing." — *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012)
**Example:** "Just take a shower. What topic do your thoughts keep returning to?" — *The Top Idea in Your Mind* (2010)
**Example:** "Always produce." — *How to Do What You Love* (2006)

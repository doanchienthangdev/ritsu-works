# Paul Graham — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A hacker-turned-essayist who writes the way he'd explain something to a smart friend at dinner — plain spoken words, no jargon, starting from something he noticed himself, following his curiosity instead of defending a thesis, grounding every idea in a concrete real example, qualifying every claim exactly as far as he believes it, and compressing the finding into a short sticky handle you can carry around and reuse.

**Rhythm rule:** Think on the page. Short-to-medium conversational sentences strung with speech connectors ("Actually,", "But", "So", "It turns out,"), one wandering reasoning sentence, then a short flat line that lands the finding ("Make something people want." / "Live in the future, then build what's missing."). Read it aloud; if a sentence isn't how you'd say it to a friend, you'll "hear the clank as it hits the page" — rewrite it.
**Diction rule:** The plainest Anglo-Saxon word and the contraction. "Thing," "stuff," "smart," "weird," "obvious," "actually," "turns out," "notice," "figure out," "want." Use, not utilize; so, not thus. Zero jargon, zero buzzwords, zero Profound Voice. (Asides go in parentheses or a new short sentence — never an em-dash; the humanizer strips them.)
**Reader rule:** Write to one smart, curious peer — never a student to lecture or a customer to sell. Assume intelligence; repay it with the real idea and the real mechanism, precisely qualified. Be candid about your own uncertainty. Hand them something to *do*.

## Do (top 7)
1. **Open from your own noticing** — "I realized recently that…", a flat surprising declarative, or a familiar thing you're about to turn over. Never a thesis sentence or a hype hook.
2. **Write to figure it out, not to defend** — follow the most interesting thread, let the reader watch you think, arrive somewhere you didn't fully see at the start ("An essay is something you write to try to figure something out").
3. **Aim for surprise** — build the piece around the thing that surprised *you*; "an essay you publish ought to tell the reader something he didn't already know." No surprise, no reason to exist.
4. **Coin the handle** — give a real pattern a short, plain, sticky name the reader can reuse (*do things that don't scale*, *keep your identity small*, *the top idea in your mind*). The name is the payload — but only when it names something true.
5. **Cash out every abstraction in a concrete, real example** — "Airbnb's founders went door to door in New York"; what you think about in the shower. The specific carries the general. Never invent it.
6. **Qualify honestly** — "I think," "probably," "most," "in my experience," "I may be wrong." The carefulness is the authority; then say the important thing unequivocally.
7. **Close earnest, not clever** — a small imperative or a quiet widening the reader can act on ("Just take a shower. What topic do your thoughts keep returning to?"), never a punchline or a summary.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Use **jargon, buzzwords, or business/academic language** to sound smart, or write a sentence you wouldn't say aloud to a friend.
2. **Take a position and defend it** like a debater — argue to win instead of to find what's true.
3. Tell the reader **something they already knew**; publish a piece with no surprise in it.
4. **Pad or throat-clear.** Write a bad version 1 fast, then "cut out everything unnecessary."
5. **Fake certainty** (or hedge out of fear) — calibrate the claim to what you actually believe.
6. Optimize for **prestige, profundity, or impressiveness** — the thing that sounds deep over the thing that's true and plain. "Prestige is like a powerful magnet that warps even your beliefs about what you enjoy."
7. **Invent the example or the number.** His authority is that the cases are real; faking one is the unforgivable move. (Keep the *mechanics* on any topic; don't counterfeit a startup anecdote.)

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The first-person realization — "I realized recently that what one thinks about in the shower in the morning is more important than I'd thought." — *The Top Idea in Your Mind* (2010)
- The flat surprising declarative — "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems, preferably problems you have yourself." — *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012)
- The familiar thing, reexamined — "Remember the essays you had to write in high school?" — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The small imperative — "Just take a shower. What topic do your thoughts keep returning to?" — *The Top Idea in Your Mind* (2010)
- The compressed builder's directive — "Live in the future, then build what's missing." — *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012)
- The plain finding stated as fact — "Always produce." — *How to Do What You Love* (2006)

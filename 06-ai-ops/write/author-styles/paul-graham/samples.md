## Write like you talk — plain spoken language

> "Informal language is the athletic clothing of ideas."
> — *Write Like You Talk* (2015)

**Demonstrates:** The whole prose philosophy in one homely analogy. Note what it *isn't*: no jargon, no abstraction about "register" or "diction." A piece of clothing you already understand carries the point, and the sentence itself is exactly as plain as the advice it gives. The form matches the content — which is the deepest tell of his voice.

---

> "Is this the way I'd say this if I were talking to a friend? … If you simply manage to write in spoken language, you'll be ahead of 95% of writers."
> — *Write Like You Talk* (2015)

**Demonstrates:** The single most useful revision test in the whole corpus, stated as a question you ask yourself, plus the flat empirical payoff ("95% of writers"). The "to a friend" is load-bearing — it sets the entire reader-relationship. On the page this becomes: read every sentence aloud, and cut any that you wouldn't actually say.

---

## The essay as discovery — write to figure it out

> "An essay is something you write to try to figure something out."
> — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)

**Demonstrates:** The thesis-free architecture in eleven plain words. Not "an essay argues a position" — "to try to figure something out." Everything else about his structure (the digressions, the train of thought, the surprise) follows from this one redefinition. Good model for an opening that resets a familiar word.

---

> "In a real essay, you don't take a position and defend it. … Expressing ideas helps to form them."
> — *The Age of the Essay* (2004)

**Demonstrates:** The refusal of the school-debate shape, and the reason for it: writing is *how you think*, not a record of thinking already done. The second sentence is the engine of the whole method — you write to find out what you think, so the essay has to be allowed to discover its own conclusion.

---

## Aim for surprise — the reason the piece exists

> "Useful writing tells people something true and important that they didn't already know, and tells them as unequivocally as possible."
> — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)

**Demonstrates:** His definition of value, and the structure of his sentences. Watch the four hidden criteria packed in plainly — *true*, *important*, *new* ("didn't already know"), *unequivocal*. And the close ("as unequivocally as possible") is itself unequivocal: he practices the rule in the sentence that states it.

---

> "Anything you notice that surprises you, who've thought about the topic a lot, will probably also surprise a significant number of readers."
> — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)

**Demonstrates:** The practical method for *finding* the surprise — use your own surprise as the detector. Note the careful qualifier ("probably," "a significant number") doing real work: he's giving you a usable heuristic, not a guarantee, and the honesty about that is what makes it trustworthy.

---

## The coined handle — name the pattern

> "One of the most common types of advice we give at Y Combinator is to do things that don't scale."
> — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)

**Demonstrates:** The handle, deployed. Five plain words — *do things that don't scale* — that you can now carry around and apply to a hundred situations. It's deliberately counterintuitive (scaling is supposed to be the whole point), which is what makes it stick. The title *is* the idea.

---

> "The more labels you have for yourself, the dumber they make you."
> — *Keep Your Identity Small* (2009)

**Demonstrates:** A blunt, plain, slightly provocative finding that names a real cognitive trap. No hedging here — this is one of the few things he states flatly, because he's sure of it. The model for the one or two sentences in a piece that you let land without a qualifier.

---

## The concrete real example — the specific carries the general

> "When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot."
> — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)

**Demonstrates:** How he turns an abstract principle ("reduce friction for early users") into a scene you can picture and can't forget. The dialogue ("Right then, give me your laptop") makes it concrete and real. He never says "be hands-on"; he shows you two founders closing a laptop's worth of friction in one move.

---

> "I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy."
> — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)

**Demonstrates:** The personal-evidence claim, exactly calibrated. "I have never once seen" is stronger than a statistic and more honest than a study he doesn't have — it's a precise report of his own experience. Note the plain diction ("lured down a blind alley") doing the work a fancier writer would hand to jargon.

## First principles — why is this actually true?

> "I finally realized today why politics and religion yield such uniquely useless discussions."
> — *Keep Your Identity Small* (2009)

**Demonstrates:** The signature opening — drop in at the exact moment a long-standing puzzle resolves. "I finally realized" tells you he's been chewing on this for a while, which makes you want the answer. It's the essay-as-discovery promise made in the first sentence: *I figured something out, come watch.*

---

> "Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off."
> — *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013)

**Demonstrates:** The first-principles correction of a folk-explanation. People say "startups take off" as if it were weather; he reaches in and finds the agency hiding under the passive verb. "Actually" is his favorite first-principles flag — the word that announces *the received answer is wrong, here's what's really going on.*

---

## The contrarian inversion — the obvious approach is backwards

> "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems, preferably problems you have yourself."
> — *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012)

**Demonstrates:** The inversion as thesis engine. The first sentence sounds almost paradoxical; the second resolves it into something obviously right. This shape — *not the obvious X, but the unobvious Y that turns out to be correct* — is the spine of dozens of his essays, and the source of the "surprising but on reflection obvious" feeling.

---

> "Live in the future, then build what's missing."
> — *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012)

**Demonstrates:** The compressed builder's directive as a closing. Six words, an imperative, and a whole method folded inside it. This is how he lands a piece: not a summary, but a thing you can go do tomorrow. Note the absence of any em-dash or flourish — the compression *is* the style.

---

## The honest qualifier, and the unequivocal counterweight

> "To be happy I think you have to be doing something you not only enjoy, but admire."
> — *How to Do What You Love* (2006)

**Demonstrates:** The qualifier ("I think") on a claim about something genuinely uncertain — happiness — paired with a precise distinction (enjoy vs. admire) that *is* the new idea. He's careful exactly where carefulness is honest, and sharp exactly where he's found something. The mix is the voice.

---

> "Prestige is like a powerful magnet that warps even your beliefs about what you enjoy."
> — *How to Do What You Love* (2006)

**Demonstrates:** One homely analogy (a magnet) reorganizing how you see a whole force (prestige) — and a genuinely uncomfortable surprise inside it (it warps not just your choices but your *beliefs about your own preferences*). The model for using a plain physical picture to deliver an idea that would sound preachy stated directly.

---

## Compression — cut everything unnecessary

> "Write a bad version 1 as fast as you can; rewrite it over and over; cut out everything unnecessary…"
> — *Writing, Briefly* (2005)

**Demonstrates:** His entire revision method as a single breathless semicoloned line — and the line itself enacts it, stripped to imperatives with every unnecessary word already cut. The lesson and the demonstration are the same sentence. The bad-fast-first-draft permission is the unlock; the cutting is where the quality comes from.

---

> "You don't have to publish the essay at all, if you can't get it right."
> — *How to Write Usefully* (2020)

**Demonstrates:** The discipline behind the compression — the willingness to throw the whole thing out. It's also pure spoken language (read it aloud; it's exactly how you'd say it) and a quietly high standard stated without any self-importance. The freedom not to publish is what lets the published ones be good.

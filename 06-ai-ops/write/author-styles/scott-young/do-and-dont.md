# Scott Young — Do & Don't

<!-- The fastest reference during drafting + the humanizer's voice-fidelity checklist.
     Each "do" is a move to actively reach for; each "don't" would break the illusion that Scott Young wrote it. -->

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Open on a small personal scene, a flat myth-busting sentence, or a stacked-question hook | Open with a thesis statement, a dictionary definition, or "In today's world…" |
| Run the Expectation Trap: predict what one would expect → break it with a *named* study → explain why | Assert a counterintuitive claim with "studies show" and no named source |
| Anchor every abstraction to a concrete everyday object within a sentence or two (pendulum, ten-dollar bill, a recipe) | Let concepts float in jargon; use a "sciency word" as if it were an explanation |
| Keep sentences short (8–20 words); isolate a 3–6-word line for emphasis; paragraphs 1–3 sentences | Write long, clause-stacked, abstract paragraphs with one idea blurred into the next |
| Bound every strong claim with an honest caveat that names the limit of the evidence | Make an unqualified promise or sell a shortcut as magic |
| Remove the reader's shame ("There is no gene"; "I still get stuck too"); cite named people and studies | Condescend, exhort, or imply the reader is lazy or untalented |
| Land on widened possibility and a quiet, modest note, looping back to the opening scene | End on a motivational crescendo or a grand call-to-greatness |

**The one test:** Could a skeptical reader stop at any claim, ask "*why* is that true, and how do you know?", and find — right there in the next sentence — a named mechanism and a falsifiable, evidence-backed reason rather than a vibe? If not, it isn't Scott Young yet.

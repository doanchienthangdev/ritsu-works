# Scott Young — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A calm, evidence-led experimenter who opens on a concrete scene or a flat myth-buster, sets up what "one would expect," then breaks it with a *named* study and explains *why* mechanically — short plain sentences, every abstraction anchored to a homely object, every strong claim fenced by an honest caveat.

**Rhythm rule:** Mostly 8–20-word sentences. Setup → turn → payoff across two or three of them, then snap to a 3–6-word line on its own ("But that hasn't happened."). Paragraphs 1–3 sentences; one idea each.
**Diction rule:** Plain Anglo-Saxon verbs plus cognitive-science terms *always glossed in plain English* (the testing effect: testing yourself, not re-reading). The concrete instance, never the abstract category. Zero hype, zero guru-speak. One dry italic intensifier at most.
**Reader rule:** A capable peer who is mis-strategized, not untalented. Hand them the *why*, not just the *what*. Use *you*; remove their shame ("There is no gene"); admit your own struggle; name your sources.
**Punctuation note:** He's em-dash-heavy in real life — but the humanizer strips em-dashes, so use a **colon** (definition/expansion), a **period-split** (strong pivot), or **parentheses** (aside) instead. Parentheticals are a genuine tell; keep them.

## Do (top 7)
1. Open on a concrete personal scene, a flat myth-buster ("the truth is X is actually pretty easy"), a stack of 2–3 short questions, or a paradox — never a thesis statement.
2. Run the **Expectation Trap**: "one would expect…" → break it with a *named* study → explain *why* mechanically.
3. Cite real, named researchers + the finding + a specific number ("by as much as 30–50%"). Never "studies show."
4. Anchor every abstraction to a homely physical thing within a sentence (pendulum, ten-dollar bill, France → Eiffel Tower, a recipe).
5. Embed one in-the-moment reader exercise ("try this right now… how many did you remember?").
6. Bound every strong claim with an honest caveat ("the spacing effect does not continue indefinitely").
7. Coin or name the framework (the three phases; the Feynman Technique; a "feel-productive project") and lay it out in parallel.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Open with a definition, a thesis sentence, or "In today's world…".
2. Assert a counterintuitive claim with "studies show" and no named source.
3. Use a "sciency word" as if it were the explanation — bottom out in a mechanism with something falsifiable at stake.
4. Make an unqualified promise or sell a shortcut as magic.
5. Shame, exhort, or imply the reader is lazy or untalented.
6. Let an abstraction float with no concrete anchor; stack clauses into long abstract paragraphs.
7. End on a motivational crescendo or a grand call-to-greatness.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- Self-deprecating scene — e.g. "I don't know anything about cars. Exhibit A:…"
- Flat myth-buster — e.g. "Few skills scare people away like coding. But the truth is that coding is actually pretty easy."
- Rapid-fire question stack — e.g. "How does your memory work? How can you remember more? Prevent forgetting?"
- Blunt negation + reframe — e.g. "There is no gene, however. There's only ineffective strategies and good ones."

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The quiet lift looping back to the opening — e.g. "your world becomes just a little bit bigger… the stuff of happiness itself."
- The recap-then-encourage — restate the claim plainly, name the one real obstacle, open to lifelong value ("anyone can learn to code").
- A bulleted "Summary / we recommend the following:" or a question handed to the reader.

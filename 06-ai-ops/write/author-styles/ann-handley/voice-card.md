# Ann Handley — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A warm, funny writing coach who talks *to* one reader like a friend — short percussive sentences, plain words, a homely metaphor for every idea, and a wink in the parentheses — always in service of teaching you how to do the thing yourself.

**Rhythm rule:** Mostly short sentences (6–14 words); after a run of them, drop a 1–4-word fragment on its own line to land the point ("Harsh, I know." / "It's not naïve." / "Done is better than perfect.").
**Diction rule:** Plain Anglo-Saxon verbs and concrete, homely nouns (a Honda Civic, a budget hostel, peanut butter) — never the corporate Frankenword. If you can't whisper it to your sweetheart in the dark, cut it.
**Reader rule:** Write to one person and greet them by saying hi. Assume they're smart, a little anxious about writing, and want to be respected — so be generous, be funny, and make it about *them* (swap "we" for "you").

## Do (top 7)
1. Open with a small personal confession or a vivid scene, not a thesis statement ("Last Tuesday, for the first time in my life, I did a push-up.").
2. Run a sustained metaphor as the chapter's scaffolding — writing-as-push-ups, content-as-a-polluted-pool, a first draft as "birthing a Honda Civic."
3. Teach with a before/after pair: "This is okay: … This is way better: …" — then a short "Why this works."
4. Use the fragment as percussion — break a triad onto its own beats ("The rewrite. The reshape. The polish.").
5. Ask a quick rhetorical question and answer it immediately ("Feels formulaic, right? It is.").
6. Put the joke in the parentheses (the aside is where the warmth and wit live).
7. Coin a sticky, slightly silly label for the thing you're naming (TUFD, Frankenwords, Weblish, Hot Dog Writing).

## Don't (top 7)
1. Don't open with throat-clearing — "According to…", "I'm excited to announce that…", "The purpose of this post is…".
2. Don't use jargon, buzzwords, or Frankenwords ("leverage," "incentivize," "prioritization," "learnings") — bulked-up words are the weakest words.
3. Don't write to a crowd or in cold broadcast voice — the reader is one person, alone, a little unsure.
4. Don't replace showing with adjectives — dramatize it ("show me the glint of moonlight on broken glass").
5. Don't make the reader work too hard — short sentences, short paragraphs, white space, no maddening meander.
6. Don't be mean while being honest — name the hard truth, then reassure ("Harsh, I know. But no hate. I'm here for you.").
7. Don't end flat — close on momentum, generosity, or a four-word mic-drop, never a generic "in conclusion."

## Signature openings (how they start)
- The small personal confession — e.g. "Last Tuesday, for the first time in my life, I did a push-up."
- The direct hello to the reader — e.g. "You might be picking this up for the first time. (Hi, new friend.)"
- The dropped-in scene or pop-culture cold open — e.g. "Remember the scene in The Lion King…"

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The four-word mic-drop — e.g. "Done is better than perfect." / "But the gratitude is real."
- The generous send-off with a verb — e.g. "consider your project finished, and let it go with love."
- The reassurance that flips to the reader — e.g. "It matters. You matter. You are someone's favorite teacher."

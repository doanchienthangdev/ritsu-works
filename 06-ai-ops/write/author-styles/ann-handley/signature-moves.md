# Ann Handley — Signature Moves

## The Personal-Confession Open
**What:** Begin not with a thesis but with a small, slightly vulnerable confession or scene from her own life — then pivot to the reader's situation. The vulnerability earns trust and disarms the reader's anxiety about writing.
**When:** The first line of almost anything — a chapter, a post, an About page, an email. Any time a "topic sentence" would feel cold or formulaic.
**Example:** "Last Tuesday, for the first time in my life, I did a push-up." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023 (the whole book then runs writing-is-like-building-that-muscle).

## The Sustained Governing Metaphor
**What:** Choose one everyday image and ride it through the entire piece so the abstraction becomes portable. Writing is doing push-ups; content marketing is a swimming pool you can pollute; a rough draft is "birthing a Honda Civic"; a too-long deadline lets your art "sit on its butt on the couch eating chips and salsa."
**When:** Whenever a concept is too dry or too large to land as a plain sentence. Find the homely object that *is* the idea and extend it.
**Example:** "We are a planet of publishers, yet many of us are polluting the pool with content rubbish." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

## The Before/After Pair (+ "Why this works")
**What:** Teach by contrast. Show the flat version, then the better version, labeled plainly — "This is okay: … This is way better: …" or "Tell Me: … Tell Me Without Telling Me: …" — then a one-line explanation of the difference.
**When:** Any craft lesson, especially copy. It's her default teaching unit and the fastest way to make a principle concrete.
**Example:** "This is okay: Outsource your long to-do list… This is way better: You spend evenings and weekends answering emails instead of enjoying your free time." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

## The Fragment as Percussion
**What:** After a run of short declaratives, drop a one-to-four-word fragment on its own line to land the beat — often a triad split across three lines: "The rewrite. The reshape. The polish." / "Inhale. Exhale."
**When:** To close a paragraph, punch a point, or vary a rhythm that's gone flat. The shorter the surrounding sentences, the harder the fragment hits.
**Example:** "Great writing makes us feel. Period." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

## The Rhetorical Question, Answered in Two Words
**What:** Pose the question the reader is already half-thinking, then answer it instantly and curtly. It mimics conversation and keeps the pace brisk.
**When:** Mid-argument, to acknowledge an objection or confirm a suspicion without slowing down.
**Example:** "Feels formulaic, right? It is. Feels boring? That, too." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

## The Parenthetical Wink
**What:** Tuck the joke, the aside, or the burst of warmth inside parentheses — a running second voice commenting on the main line. It's where her personality is loudest.
**When:** Right after a factual or instructional sentence that could otherwise read flat. The aside humanizes the authority.
**Example:** On a misplaced modifier about a sloth: "And definitely not the sloth. (They're famously unathletic.)" — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

## The Coined Sticky Label
**What:** Give the thing you're naming a memorable, slightly silly handle so the reader can carry it out of the room: TUFD (The Ugly First Draft), Frankenwords, Weblish, Hot Dog Writing, "youness," the "It's-me! Minute."
**When:** Introducing a concept, a failure mode, or a method you want the reader to remember and reuse. The label does the mnemonic work.
**Example:** "Avoid Frankenwords—words stitched awkwardly together to create something of an ugly, monstrous mess." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

## The Homely-but-Grotesque Analogy
**What:** Reach for an image that's both household-familiar and a little gross or absurd, so it's vivid *and* funny. The comedy makes the craft point stick.
**When:** Describing bad writing, a messy draft, or a painful process — anywhere a clean simile would be forgettable.
**Example:** A rough draft has "subject-verb disagreements so violent they are practically fistfights." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

## Name-the-Enemy, Then Reassure
**What:** State the hard truth or name the failure mode bluntly, then immediately soften toward the reader so the standard is heard, not resented.
**When:** Setting a high bar or delivering criticism of a common bad habit. The reassurance keeps the reader on your side.
**Example:** "We are all creators, yet many of us are squandering the opportunity… Harsh, I know. But no hate. I'm here for you." — *Everybody Writes*, 2023.

# Barbara Oakley — Do & Don't

<!-- The fastest reference during drafting + the humanizer's voice-fidelity checklist.
     Each "do" is a move to actively reach for; each "don't" would break the illusion that Barbara Oakley wrote it. -->

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Open on a human — your own (or a famous figure's) true story of being stuck ("I flunked my way through… math and science") | Open cold on a definition, a thesis sentence, or an abstract concept |
| Drop one homely physical metaphor onto the brain and walk the reader around it before explaining (pinball machine, Octopus of Attention, brick wall, metabolic vampires) | Explain a brain mechanism abstractly, with no picture to hold it |
| Name the science, then translate it in the very next breath ("the insular cortex activates — that's the icky feeling") | Leave a technical term (hippocampus, basal ganglia) standing alone, untranslated |
| End every section with a concrete technique the reader can use this afternoon (Pomodoro, recall-then-look-away, space it over days, interleave) | Leave the reader with insight but nothing to *do* |
| Reframe a deficit as a gift (slow thinking / weak memory / wandering attention → the hiker who sees more) | Imply a subject needs innate talent ("the math gene"), or shame the slow learner |
| Name the takeaway plainly with the "the bottom line for all of us out of this is this:" pivot | Bury the lesson and make the reader extract it themselves |
| Stay warm, humble, and funny — use *you*/*we*, admit your own struggle, credit the scientists by name | Sound like a guru or a journal; hype a "hack"; pretend certainty; skip attribution |
| Close warm on a small reframe handed back to the reader ("broaden your passions"; "AI shows, but I'm the only one who knows") | Close on a dry "in conclusion" or a motivational send-off ("go crush it!") |

**The one test:** Find the section's single picture and its single thing-to-do. Is there one homely, instantly-visualizable metaphor for the hard idea — and one concrete technique the reader can act on today? If the idea is abstract with no image, or insightful with no action, it isn't Barbara Oakley yet. And if any line shames the struggling learner instead of building them up, delete it: her deepest tell is the belief that you can change your brain.

# Barbara Oakley — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A warm, self-deprecating engineering professor who flunked math as a kid and now teaches the brain by dropping a homely physical metaphor onto a hard idea, naming the science and translating it in the same breath, and ending every point with a technique you can use this afternoon.

**Rhythm rule:** Talk on the page. Conversational sentences broken by short reveals, driven by the question → answer drumbeat ("How can you even get there?" … then answer it). Name the takeaway plainly with "the bottom line for all of us out of this is this:".
**Diction rule:** The homely physical picture and the playful verb — a pinball machine on your brain, metabolic vampires, the Octopus of Attention, "boinking," the "icky feeling." Name the science (hippocampus, insular cortex) only if you translate it in the very next clause. Zero hype, zero shame.
**Reader rule:** Assume they're capable and a little scared of the subject. Use *you* and *we*, admit your own struggle, credit the scientists by name, reframe a deficit as a gift, and hand over one concrete technique.

## Do (top 7)
1. Open on a human, not a concept — your own (or a famous figure's) true story of being stuck ("I flunked my way through… math and science").
2. Drop one homely physical metaphor onto the brain and walk the reader around it before explaining ("look at how far apart those bumpers are").
3. Name the science, then translate it in the next breath ("the insular cortex activates — that's the icky feeling that sends you to your phone").
4. End every section with a concrete technique (Pomodoro, recall-then-look-away, space it over days, interleave, handwrite it).
5. Reframe a deficit as a gift — slow thinking / weak memory / wandering attention as the hiker who sees more, the more creative mind.
6. Use the "bottom line for all of us out of this is this:" pivot to state the takeaway plainly.
7. Coin or use a capitalized mental model (focused/diffuse mode, metabolic vampires) as a handle the reader carries away.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Shame the struggling learner, or imply a subject needs innate talent ("the math gene").
2. Leave a brain structure abstract — never name one without a picture and a plain translation.
3. Recommend the comfortable-but-useless (rereading, highlighting, cramming) as if it worked.
4. Hand over a "hack" with no science, or hype it ("this ONE trick…").
5. Pretend certainty you don't have, or skip crediting the researchers.
6. Tell the reader to just "follow your passion."
7. Close cold on a dry summary — close warm with a small reframe and an expectation of success.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- Reformed-flunky on-ramp — "I grew up moving all over the place. By the time I'd hit 3rd grade, I'd fallen off the math bandwagon."
- Famous-person scene — "According to legend, the great Napoleon was once presented with a Colonel who was put forth for promotion."
- The picture first — "You all know how pinballs work… What we're going to do is take this pinball and put it right on your brain."

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The small reframe handed back — "Don't just follow your passions; broaden your passions. And your lives will be greatly enriched."
- The memorable compression — "AI shows, but I'm the only one who knows."
- The warm send-off with a technique — "start with a challenge that is just manageable, measure your progress, and repeat… and your spirits will be boosted always."

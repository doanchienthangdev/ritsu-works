## 1. The Physical Metaphor Dropped Onto the Brain (her core instrument)

**What:** Take an invisible brain process and make it a homely, instantly-visualizable physical object — then literally superimpose it on the brain and walk the reader around the picture before explaining the mechanism. The metaphor carries the cognitive load so the prose stays plain. This is the single most Oakley thing she does; she calls metaphor itself a learning superpower.

**When:** The moment an idea risks becoming abstract or technical. Reach for the most ordinary physical object available — a pinball machine, an octopus, a brick wall, a key falling from a hand. The power is in the familiarity, not the cleverness.

**Example:** "What we're going to use is a pinball machine analogy… we're going to take this pinball and we're going to put it right on your brain… look at all the rubber bumpers that are in the way. How can you even get there?" — *Learning How to Learn*, TEDxOaklandUniversity (2014)
**Example:** "you can think of it… as something like an octopus, the Octopus of Attention, that reaches through those slots of working memory and makes connections between different ideas." — *Learning How to Learn*, Talks at Google (2015)
**Example:** "you've got these little metabolic vampires that just come and they suck those patterns away before they can get deepened." — *Learning How to Learn*, Talks at Google (2015)

## 2. The Reformed-Flunky Credential (the credibility on-ramp)

**What:** Open with her own true story of failing the very subject she now professes — moving constantly as a kid, falling off the math bandwagon, flunking, joining the Army, then choosing at 26 to change her brain. The biography *is* the argument for her one core claim: you are not stuck with the brain you have.

**When:** At the start of almost any piece about learning, or whenever a reader might believe a subject requires innate talent. The vulnerability earns the authority.

**Example:** "By the time I'd hit 3rd grade, I'd fallen off the math bandwagon. Basically, I flunked my way through elementary, middle, and high school math and science. So it's a little strange looking back now because today I am a professor of engineering and I'm passionate about my job." — *Learning How to Learn*, TEDxOaklandUniversity (2014)
**Example:** "I just thought, 'Oh gosh, I can't seem to catch up… I must not have the math gene.' So I gave up and I flunked my way through… which is a little ironic since I am standing in front of you here today as a distinguished professor of engineering." — *Accelerate Your Learning with ChatGPT*, UFM (2025)

## 3. The Reframe-the-Deficit (your weakness is a hidden strength)

**What:** Take the trait a learner is ashamed of — slow thinking, a poor working memory, a wandering attention — and recast it as a genuine advantage, usually through the race-car-vs-hiker image. The move is always *the thing you think disqualifies you is the thing that makes you see more.*

**When:** Whenever the reader is comparing themselves unfavorably to faster peers. It dissolves the shame and frees them to use their actual cognitive style.

**Example:** "Some students compare themselves to other students and say… 'they are like race car drivers; they go past me so fast.' But think of yourself as a hiker… A hiker can smell the pine air, they can reach out, touch the leaves, they see the rabbit trails. In many ways, your experiences are deeper and more profound." — *Learning How to Learn*, TEDxOaklandUniversity (2014)
**Example:** "if you have a poor working memory… other stuff is always slipping in. That's why you can't hold ideas very well in your mind. But because the other stuff is slipping in, you're actually more creative." — *Learning How to Learn*, Talks at Google (2015)

## 4. Name the Science, Then Translate It (authority without lecture)

**What:** State the real neuroscience term — and then immediately, in the next clause, cash it out into plain English and usually a feeling or a picture. The term gives authority; the translation keeps it readable. She never leaves a structure standing alone.

**When:** Whenever a claim would otherwise read as folk advice. One technical anchor, instantly made human.

**Example:** "when you even just think about something you don't like… it causes an area of the brain the insular cortex to activate… so it's like you get this icky feeling so what does your brain do it skeeters away from that thought." — *Techniques to Deal with Procrastination*, Nelda (2020)
**Example:** "that little bit of relaxation can help your hippocampus, which is part of the memory system, to offload some of this new information… you can almost feel your brain is like shifting things around and putting it away." — *Techniques to Deal with Procrastination*, Nelda (2020)

## 5. The Famous-Person Illustration (the idea wearing a human face)

**What:** Carry an abstract principle on the back of a vivid, named historical figure caught in a small concrete act — Dali falling asleep with a key in his hand, Edison with ball bearings, Napoleon asking whether a colonel is *lucky*, Ramón y Cajal the "not a genius" who out-thought the race cars.

**When:** When a principle (diffuse-mode incubation, intuition, persistence over speed) needs a memorable handle. The figure becomes the permanent hook for the idea.

**Example:** "What Dali used to do when he was kind of stuck… he'd relax in a chair, and he'd have keys in his hands… Just as he'd relax so much that he'd fall asleep, the keys would fall from his hands, the clatter would wake him up, and off you go: he'd take those ideas from the diffuse mode over to the focus mode." — *Learning How to Learn*, TEDxOaklandUniversity (2014)
**Example:** "According to legend, the great Napoleon was once presented with a Colonel who was put forth for promotion… 'I know he's good,' he purportedly said, 'but is he lucky?'" — "Three Powerful Ways To Develop Your Intuition," MinuteHack (2021)

## 6. The "Bottom Line For All Of Us" Pivot (name the takeaway, never bury it)

**What:** Mark the move from picture to lesson with an explicit, stock transitional frame — "The bottom line for all of us out of this is this:" / "So the lesson for us, out of all of this, is this." — then state the takeaway in plain words. She refuses to make the reader extract the principle themselves.

**When:** At the hinge between a story/metaphor and its application. It tells the reader: *here is the part to keep.*

**Example:** "The bottom line for all of us out of this is this: when you're learning, you want to go back and forth between these modes, and if you… get stuck, you want to turn your attention away from that problem and allow the diffuse modes… to do their work in the background." — *Learning How to Learn*, TEDxOaklandUniversity (2014)

## 7. The Internalize-Don't-Offload Argument (her thesis for the AI age)

**What:** Argue that looking it up, or letting AI answer, feels efficient but quietly robs you of the internal knowledge that expertise and even critical thinking are built from. Backed by mechanism (links must be practiced into long-term memory or the synaptic janitors sweep them away) and by a memorable compression: *AI shows, but I'm the only one who knows.*

**When:** Against the seductive ease of offloading — the exact tension of studying in 2025. Deploy the concrete failure case (the nurse who can't feel that a dose is 10× too big) to make the abstract patience feel urgent.

**Example:** "AI shows, but I'm the only one who knows." — *The Memory Paradox*, UFM (2025)
**Example:** "We've gotten so crazily overboard that conceptual understanding is the only golden key to learning that we overlook the fact that if you can't remember what you've learned, you really have not learned it." — *The Memory Paradox*, UFM (2025)
**Example:** "AI makes things easier, but only if you know what you're doing… it can amplify skill if you already have skill, but it will not create your skill for you." — *The Memory Paradox*, UFM (2025)

## 8. The Homely Onomatopoeia (delight as a teaching tool)

**What:** Let the verbs be playful and sound-shaped, so a dry mechanism becomes a little bit fun and therefore memorable — thoughts go "boinking," attention "skeeters away," dopamine gets "squirted around," a feeling "pops to mind," a neuron "tickles the toe" of its neighbor. The delight is not decoration; it lowers the reader's guard and makes the science stick.

**When:** Whenever a mechanism is about to read as clinical. One playful verb resets the tone.

**Example:** "you just pull back on a plunger, and the ball goes boinking out and bounces around on the rubber bumpers." — *Learning How to Learn*, TEDxOaklandUniversity (2014)
**Example:** "your brain… skeeters away from that thought that caused the icky feeling and voila you're on Facebook." — *Techniques to Deal with Procrastination*, Nelda (2020)
**Example:** "What neurons like to do is they like to kind of reach out and tickle the toe of an adjoining neuron." — *The Memory Paradox*, UFM (2025)

---

## 9. The Audience-Participation Beat (make the method the lesson)

**What:** In a talk, stop the exposition and make the reader *do* something — vote on the best study technique, turn to a stranger and explain a concept, say a line aloud together — then reveal that the activity itself just demonstrated the point (e.g. the turn-to-a-neighbor pause *was* a diffuse-mode break).

**When:** To break a long focused stretch and to convert a claim into an experience. On the page this becomes a direct instruction to the reader ("try this right now") and a reveal.

**Example:** "I want you to turn to someone… describe what is the difference between focus and diffuse modes… Did you notice what I was cleverly and sneakily doing here? In the middle of our focused mode lecture, we just took a little diffuse mode break." — *Accelerate Your Learning with ChatGPT*, UFM (2025)
**Example:** "So, let's say it together. AI shows, but I'm the only one who knows." — *The Memory Paradox*, UFM (2025)

# James Clear — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A patient teacher who opens on a vivid true story, moves to a plainly-named principle, and lands on one short quotable line — every sentence concrete, short, and earning its place.

**Rhythm rule:** Short sentences. Build with two or three, then snap to a very short one to land the point ("Not too hard. Not too easy. Just right."). One idea per paragraph; let a single line stand alone.
**Diction rule:** The concrete noun or exact number, never the abstract category. Plain Anglo-Saxon verbs. Zero hype, zero jargon.
**Reader rule:** Assume they're smart and busy; respect both. Use *you* and *we*, admit your own struggle, credit your sources, hand them one clear action.

## Do (top 7)
1. Open cold on a specific scene with a date and a name; reveal the subject after a beat ("His name is Steve Martin.").
2. Move story → principle → application across 2–3 unlike domains (writing / fitness / business).
3. Compress the key idea into one balanced aphorism and set it on its own line.
4. Prove claims with an exact number, a named study, or a real object — not an adjective.
5. Use the antithetical pair: two short sentences, opposite in meaning, parallel in shape.
6. Voice the reader's objection as a question, then answer it.
7. Coin a labeled rule (the Goldilocks Rule) when an idea earns its own name; make it scannable with headers + bold lead-ins.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Open with a definition, a dictionary quote, or "Have you ever wondered…".
2. Use hype, exclamation-selling, or guru certainty.
3. Write long clause-stacked sentences or dense paragraphs with no white space.
4. Make a claim with no name, number, or example behind it.
5. Leave the reader to extract the lesson — state it plainly.
6. End on a generic motivational send-off ("go crush it!").
7. Pad. If a word can be cut, cut it.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- Cold historical scene, date + name — e.g. "In 1666, one of the most influential scientists in history was strolling through a garden…"
- Flat declarative thesis as line one — e.g. "The ultimate productivity hack is saying no."
- Personal, present-tense admission — e.g. "I am struggling today."

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The compressed restatement as a single short line — e.g. "And once you do, stay on the bus."
- The reframe given back to the reader — e.g. "It is the way we approach the goal that matters."
- One clear action — e.g. "start with a challenge that is just manageable, measure your progress, and repeat."

## Micro-samples (≤15 words each, attributed)
- "No is a decision. Yes is a responsibility." — *Saying No*, 2019
- "Anyone can want a gold medal. Few people want to train like an Olympian." — *Giving Up*, 2015
- "Absolute success is luck. Relative success is choices and habits." — *Luck vs. Hard Work*, 2018

# James Clear — Do & Don't

<!-- The fastest reference during drafting + the humanizer's voice-fidelity checklist.
     Each "do" is a move to actively reach for; each "don't" would break the illusion that James Clear wrote it. -->

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Open cold on a specific true scene — a date, a name, a place ("In 1955, Disneyland had just opened…") | Open with a definition, a rhetorical "Have you ever…", or a dictionary quote |
| Keep sentences short (6–16 words); snap to a one-line sentence to land the point | Stack clauses into long winding sentences and dense block paragraphs |
| Move story → named principle → application across 2–3 unlike domains | Stay abstract; explain the idea without a concrete story or example carrying it |
| Compress the lesson into one balanced, quotable aphorism on its own line | Bury the lesson or leave the reader to infer it; trail off without a takeaway |
| Prove with an exact number, named researcher, or real object | Assert with adjectives and hype ("incredibly powerful, game-changing") and no evidence |
| Reframe the reader's default (systems > goals; identity > outcomes; re-work > work) | Repeat conventional advice without the inversion that makes it his |
| Stay warm and honest — use *you*/*we*, admit a struggle, credit every source | Sound salesy or certain like a guru; pretend certainty you don't have; skip attribution |

**The one test:** Pick the single line you'd put on a card. Is it short, concrete, and quotable on its own — and does the piece earn it with a real story and real evidence? If the best line is generic, or the rest is padding around it, it isn't James Clear yet.

# Maria Montessori — Do & Don't

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Build every argument from one precisely-watched learner — an age, an exact act, a counted repetition ("she had repeated the exercise forty-two times") — then extract the law in a short separate sentence | Assert a principle from the armchair, or float it with no child, object, or number attached; never "studies show" without the watched case behind it |
| Reframe by fact: name the received belief, overturn it with one observation, land on a flat verdict ("it is the child who makes the man") | Hedge before the thesis, or bury the claim under qualifiers; the qualification follows the verdict and stays lean |
| Redefine the reader's sure words by reversal — discipline as active self-mastery, reward as the birth of one's own power, "teacher" as the one who "teaches little and observes much" | Accept the conventional sense of the loaded word; never let "discipline" mean immobility or "reward" mean the prize |
| Make the invisible mental fact visible — a biology-parable ("just as the embryo... so the child") or one homely picture (the vase keeping separate from the water) | Leave a grand abstraction un-anchored, or reach for a business or machine metaphor; her images are biological, domestic, sacramental |
| Honour intrinsic motive and the learner's free, concentrated work; "when concentration has begun, act as if the child does not exist" | Praise the prize, the grade, or the badge; extrinsic reward is "the bench of the soul," always condemned |
| Keep reverence as the stance — tender toward the learner's dignity, severe toward the system that wrongs it | Coo, condescend, or moralise the small child as "good" or "bad"; grant grave dignity ("little men") instead of cuteness |
| Treat mind and movement as one — intelligence develops through the hand and through activity ("man builds himself through working") | Separate mind from body, or treat thought as something done sitting still and movement as mere exercise |
| Write long cumulative sentences chained by semicolons and "just as... so," landing each movement on a short sentence that rings | Clip the argument into modern listicle bullets; her meaning lives in the periodic sentence and the recurring motif (and no em-dashes — use commas, colons, parentheses) |
| Close by ascending: lift the single learner up the scale to humanity and the future, on hope or an open charge, ideally sealed on a borrowed line (Dante, Kant) | End merely practical, on a sermon of "we must," or on cynicism and despair; even her sharpest critique resolves upward into hope |

**The one test:** Cover the byline and read one paragraph aloud: is the argument standing on a real watched learner — an age, an act, a count, or a homely object you can see — with the law lifted out of it in a short sentence, and does the movement rise, by its end, from the single learner toward mankind or the future, in long semicolon-chained sentences with reverence as the stance and not one prize praised? If the principle floats free of any child or thing, or the prose stays flat and merely practical, or it cools into cynicism, it isn't Montessori.

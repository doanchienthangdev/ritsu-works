## 1. Coin-and-Capitalize (the proper-noun handle)

**What:** Invent a memorable, capitalized term for a previously fuzzy phenomenon, define it once — often in its own set-off line, term-colon-definition — then use it as the argument's recurring handle. The name does the persuasive work: it turns an abstract claim into something that feels like a discovered law you can hold and recall.

**When:** At the hinge of every piece, after the evidence has made the phenomenon visible but before you generalize. Once the problem is named, route all subsequent reasoning through the term. He is explicit that this is deliberate craft: the more vocabulary he can give the reader, the happier he is.

**Example:** "PSEUDO-PRODUCTIVITY: The use of visible activity as the primary means of approximating actual productive effort." — *Slow Productivity* (2024) · 15 words
**Example:** "**The Principle of Least Resistance**: … we will tend toward behaviors that are easiest in the moment." — *Deep Work* (2016), then referred back to as "our principle" for pages.

## 2. Case Study → Extracted Law (the named human as proof)

**What:** Open on a vividly specific real person — historical, interviewed, or himself — narrate the relevant details, then pivot on a signpost phrase ("he's leveraging the following law of productivity") to the general principle the case "proves." He prefers a named human over an abstraction every time; the person becomes the permanent handle for the idea.

**When:** As the primary unit of argument — almost every sub-section is one of these. The drier or more abstract the principle, the more concrete and sensory the person must be.

**Example:** "Adam Grant produces at an elite level." — *Deep Work* (2016). The flat subject-verb opener; he then anchors Grant with date and institution before extracting the productivity formula.
**Example (spoken):** "I didn't get smarter between my freshman year and the three years that followed. I got better at studying." — *Deep Questions* ("Make Learning As Addictive As Social Media"). His own Dartmouth arc as proof that studying is a *skill*, not a fixed trait.

## 3. The Formula / Equation Framing (qualitative claim as pseudo-math)

**What:** Render a soft, qualitative claim as a pseudo-mathematical equation to lend it the authority of physics — then *reason from* the equation, plugging in numbers to make a trade-off feel like arithmetic rather than opinion. He's a mathematician and leans on it.

**When:** When you want a claim to feel rigorous and quotable, and especially when a trade-off (time vs. intensity) can be dramatized as a calculation. Deploy it once as a centerpiece, not repeatedly.

**Example:** "work accomplished = time spent x intensity of focus" — *How to Become a Straight-A Student* (2006) · 9 words. He then plugs in: ten hours at intensity 3 equals three one-hour bursts at intensity 10, so the rest of the day is free.
**Example:** "High-Quality Work Produced = (Time Spent) x (Intensity of Focus)" — *Deep Work* (2016) · 11 words.

## 4. The Measured Concession (steel-man, then quarantine)

**What:** Pre-empt the obvious objection by stating it fairly — sometimes giving it a whole section and a name — then show why it doesn't undermine the thesis. Steel-manning the opposition earns the reader's trust before the real argument lands.

**When:** Right after establishing a strong claim, before the skeptical reader can object — raise the hand for them. Especially after a case study a reader would dismiss as cherry-picked, privileged, or extreme.

**Example:** "We should be careful, however, not to push the Amish example too far as a case study for meaningful living." — *Digital Minimalism* (2019) · 19 words. He concedes, then runs a second controlled case to isolate the variable he actually cares about.
**Example:** An entire section titled "What About Jack Dorsey?" concedes that a distracted executive thrives, then quarantines him with a named "rule of specificity." — *Deep Work* (2016).

---

## 5. Citation-as-Authority Stacking (researcher, institution, year, quote)

**What:** Anchor a claim to a named researcher, their institution, the year, and often the paper's title — then quote them directly, frequently with a warm editorial aside. It borrows credibility he didn't have to assert, and the aside ("intriguingly,…") signals he actually read it.

**When:** Whenever you move from anecdote to *mechanism* — the "why it works" beat. Immediately follow the citation by translating the jargon into plain English.

**Example:** "In a 2009 paper, titled, intriguingly, 'Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work?,' Leroy introduced an effect she called attention residue." — *Deep Work* (2016) · 23 words.
**Example (spoken, cite-then-translate):** "Okay, I read all those technical details so you could get a lay of the land that this is a different type of motivational system." — *Deep Questions* ("Can You Make Learning As Addictive As Social Media?").

## 6. The Imported Analytic Frame (port a rigorous field onto the problem)

**What:** Lift a framework from an unrelated discipline — economics (supply and demand, diminishing returns), addiction science, operations, neuroscience, Thoreau's "new economics" — and apply it to the human/attention/learning problem. It makes the claim feel *derived* rather than preached, and borrows the field's vocabulary and authority.

**When:** To justify a principle that would sound like mere opinion if asserted directly.

**Example:** "Basic economic theory tells us that if you want something that's both rare and valuable, you need something rare and valuable to offer in return." — *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012) · 24 words. (Supply and Demand → Career Capital.)
**Example:** "The cost of a thing is the amount of … life which is required to be exchanged for it." — Thoreau, quoted in *Digital Minimalism* (2019) as the spine of the "new economics" of attention.

---

## 7. The In-Other-Words Restatement (say it twice, second time clean)

**What:** Immediately re-express a dense analytical sentence in a cleaner, punchier second form. A teacher's instinct: say it once for precision, then again so it sticks. It produces the short hammer-sentence that follows the long build.

**When:** After any dense or qualified sentence; it's his most frequent connective tissue, and it's where the quotable line usually lives.

**Example:** "To summarize these observations more succinctly: If you can't learn, you can't thrive." — *Deep Work* (2016) · 13 words.
**Example:** "In other words, working right trumps finding the right work." — *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012) · 10 words.

---

## 8. Diagnose-Then-Prescribe (story before solution; the three-act machine)

**What:** Refuse to give advice until you've narrated the causal history of the problem — name the wrong mental model the reader already holds, demolish it, install the named replacement — so the prescription feels earned rather than asserted. The macro-shape is always (1) define, (2) prove with a deep case study, (3) hand over a numbered procedure. He pointedly criticizes writers who "just have a bunch of good ideas" without making the pieces click.

**When:** As the whole-piece architecture. The first half is "here's how we got here"; the second half is "here's the system." Never open cold with a tip list.

**Example (spoken):** "It is better to confront the reality than to pretend it doesn't exist. So step one is to prepare to face this productivity dragon." — *Deep Questions* ("How To Stop Wasting Time") — coin a vivid term, then convert it into a numbered step.
**Example (spoken):** "Do the hard work of actually doing hard work. That is the key to differentiating yourself in our current technological moment." — *Deep Questions* ("How Do I Become AI-Proof?") — the closing imperative distillation after a long diagnosis.

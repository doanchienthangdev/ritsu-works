# Cal Newport — Voice Card

**Voice in one sentence:** A calm computer-science professor building a proof — he names the wrong mental model everyone holds, dismantles it with a named researcher and a real person's story, coins a capitalized concept to replace it, then reasons from that concept and hands you a numbered system; long analytical sentences that build, snapped shut by a short declarative.

**Rhythm rule:** Long-build, short-snap. Run a clause-stacked analytical sentence (35–60 words) to lay the case, then cut to a 3–10 word declarative that lands it ("This belief is false." / "If you can't learn, you can't thrive."). Restate dense sentences cleanly: "In other words,…"
**Diction rule:** The coined-and-capitalized concept as the load-bearing noun. Import economics/neuroscience vocabulary. Hedge precisely ("a nontrivial number," "roughly," "I would estimate around 30 percent"). Zero hype, zero exclamation, zero "follow your passion."
**Reader rule:** Professor to a capable adult running on a bad default — not broken, just mistaken in a fixable way. Prove every claim with a named person, study, or number. Concede the best objection out loud. Hand over a system, not a slogan.

## Do (top 7)
1. Open on a real, specific person in a flat declarative ("Adam Grant produces at an elite level."), or a dated scene, or the slogan you're about to demolish.
2. Diagnose before you prescribe — narrate the causal history / the wrong mental model first, so the solution is earned.
3. Coin and capitalize the central idea; define it once in its own line (Term: a one-sentence law); reuse it as the handle.
4. Prove with a deep, sensory case study and a citation (researcher + institution + year + quote), then translate the jargon into plain English.
5. Build the long analytical sentence, then snap it with a short verdict. Use the equation when a trade-off can be made arithmetic.
6. Concede the strongest objection out loud ("What about the person who thrives without depth?"), then quarantine it.
7. End with a numbered, do-this-then-that system; recap it. Earn exactly one lyrical line at the very end, then stop.

## Don't (top 7)
1. Open with hype, an exclamation, or a pep-talk command ("Crush your goals!").
2. Assert "studies show" and stop, or treat one success story as proof.
3. Recommend "follow your passion," "find your purpose," or a "top 10 apps" listicle — the named enemy.
4. Round a messy number up to a clean dramatic one; hedge precisely instead.
5. Pretend change is easy, or flatter the reader as already special.
6. Bury the actionable system in abstraction — no definition box, no numbered procedure = a forgery.
7. Sustain the lyrical or the urgent. Stay calm and dry; let the argument carry the energy.

## Signature openings (how they start)
- Flat biographical cold-open — e.g. "Ric Furrer is a blacksmith." / "Adam Grant produces at an elite level."
- Dated historical scene — e.g. "Near the end of March in 1845, Henry David Thoreau borrowed an ax and walked into the woods near Walden Pond."
- The reader's complaint, then flat refutation — e.g. "Let's get one thing clear: This belief is false."

## Signature closings (how they land)
- The bolded one-line verdict that compresses the section — e.g. "working right trumps finding the right work."
- The two-beat snap of conviction — e.g. "I don't buy it." / "Case closed."
- The single controlled lift to the lyrical, reserved for the last line — e.g. "A deep life is a good life, any way you look at it."

# Cal Newport — Do & Don't

<!-- The fastest reference during drafting + the humanizer's voice-fidelity checklist.
     Each "do" is a move to actively reach for; each "don't" would break the illusion that Cal Newport wrote it. -->

| Emulate (do) | Avoid (don't) |
|---|---|
| Open on a real named person in a flat declarative ("Adam Grant produces at an elite level.") or a dated scene | Open with hype, an exclamation, a rhetorical "Have you ever…", or a pep-talk command |
| Diagnose before you prescribe — narrate the wrong mental model / causal history, *then* give the system | Lead cold with a tip list or a "top 10" listicle; hand over advice that hasn't been earned |
| Coin and capitalize the central idea; define it once in its own line and reuse it as the handle | Leave the idea unnamed and abstract, or name a concept and then never use it again |
| Build the long analytical sentence, then snap it shut with a 3–10 word verdict; restate dense lines "In other words,…" | Write only short punchy aphorisms (that's James Clear, not Newport) — or only long sentences with no snap |
| Prove with a named researcher + institution + year + quote, then translate the jargon into plain English | Say "studies show" and stop; treat a single success story as proof |
| Import a frame from economics/neuroscience; use the equation when a trade-off can be made arithmetic | Assert a productivity claim from vibes, with no mechanism behind it |
| Concede the strongest objection out loud, then quarantine it with a named rule | Strawman the opposing view, or pretend the objection doesn't exist |
| Hedge precisely ("a nontrivial number," "I would estimate around 30 percent," "roughly") | Round a messy number up to a clean dramatic one; overclaim certainty you don't have |
| End with a numbered, do-this-then-that system; earn exactly one lyrical line, then stop | Recommend "follow your passion," manufacture urgency, or sustain the lyrical / motivational register |

**The one test:** Read the draft as if a skeptic wrote it. Is every claim bolted to a named person, study, or number? Is there a coined concept doing the load-bearing work, defined once and reasoned from? Did you raise the best objection yourself before the reader could? And does the piece end with a *system*, not a slogan? If the prose is warm and quotable but undefended — or if the best line is a pep-talk rather than an earned verdict — it isn't Cal Newport yet. He is the measured professor building a proof, not the aphorist landing a snap.

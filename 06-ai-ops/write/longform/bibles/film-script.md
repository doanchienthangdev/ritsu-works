# {{Title}} — film-script bible

> **Purpose:** A single locked reference document every parallel sequence drafter reads before writing a single slug line — freezing character voice, world rules, plot commitments, visual grammar, and tonal register so eight concurrently drafted sequences can be assembled into one coherent shooting script without line-level contradictions or dramatic-logic gaps.

> **Status:** DRAFT → **LOCKED** (no part drafting starts until locked; version-stamped).

## logline_and_premise
_One-sentence logline plus a 2-paragraph premise statement that every drafter tests their sequence against — if the sequence does not advance this, it is cut._

{{fill}}

## character_dossiers
_For each named character: full name, age, physical description (3–5 specific visual details casting can use), defining speech patterns and verbal tics, psychological wound and want vs need, arc endpoint by Act III, and a 'voice test' — 3 sample lines that are definitively that character and 3 lines they would never say._

{{fill}}

## world_rules_and_locations
_Physical world constraints (time period, geography, technology level), social rules (power structures, norms), any genre-specific logic (e.g. time-travel rules, magic system), and a locked locations list with slug-line format (INT./EXT., exact name, DAY/NIGHT) every drafter must use verbatim to avoid continuity collisions in the assembled draft._

{{fill}}

## three_act_plot_map
_Scene-by-scene outline for all three acts: each scene gets a card with sequence number, location slug, dramatic function (inciting, complication, midpoint, etc.), who is present, and the scene's single dramatic question. This is the master spine — drafters cannot invent scenes outside this map without a bible amendment._

{{fill}}

## timeline_and_continuity_log
_Chronological story timeline (story-world days/hours), a props-and-costume continuity log (what each character wears or carries in each scene, so a gun introduced in Seq 3 is still present in Seq 5), and a running list of planted story elements (Chekhov's guns) that must be paid off, with the payoff scene ID noted._

{{fill}}

## theme_and_motif_register
_The film's central theme stated as a dramatic question (not an answer), 2–3 subthemes, and a motif list — recurring images, sounds, lines of dialogue, or visual rhymes (e.g. 'close-up of hands when trust is broken') every drafter must deploy at least once in their sequence and must not contradict._

{{fill}}

## tone_and_visual_grammar
_Tonal register (e.g. 'deadpan absurdist, never winking'), pacing conventions (scene length norms, action-line density, dialogue-to-action ratio), camera-direction policy (whether master script includes any slugged camera direction or leaves it to director), and a 'do-not-use' list of clichés and over-used constructions banned from all sequences._

{{fill}}

## dialogue_style_sheet
_Sentence length norms per character (clipped vs. discursive), subtext policy (how much is said vs. implied), on-the-nose dialogue red-flag list, treatment of silence and action beats within dialogue blocks, and a sample 1-page scene that exemplifies the approved register — all drafters calibrate against this scene._

{{fill}}

## format_and_slug_conventions
_Exact screenplay format rules: font (Courier 12pt), margins, slug-line capitalization rules, parenthetical usage policy (sparingly or never), scene-number convention (only in production draft or locked draft), and how to handle transitions (CUT TO: only at act breaks, SMASH CUT for shock, no others)._

{{fill}}

## What must stay consistent (the non-negotiables)
- Character names and their exact spelling (including aliases and how other characters address them in dialogue)
- Location slug-line format — every reference to the same physical place uses the identical INT./EXT. LOCATION NAME - TIME slug
- Costume and prop state across scenes — a character's injury, their outfit, and any object they are carrying must match the timeline log across sequence boundaries
- Character speech patterns and verbal tics — a character defined as speaking in clipped one-word answers must not deliver a three-sentence speech in a later sequence without a dramatic justification noted in the bible
- The central dramatic question and its answer — the theme must be resolved in the same direction across all sequences; thematic drift (where Seq 6 implies the opposite moral of Seq 2) is the most common parallel-draft failure mode
- Tonal register — a sequence drafted in heightened realism must not introduce slapstick or meta-humor that contradicts the tone register in the style sheet
- Story-world chronology — scene time-of-day, elapsed story time, and cause-effect order must be internally consistent with the timeline log

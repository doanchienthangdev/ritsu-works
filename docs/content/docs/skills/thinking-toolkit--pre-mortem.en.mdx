> Assume the decision already failed. Work backward to *why* — before you commit, while you still can change it.

A pre-mortem is the inverse of a post-mortem. Instead of explaining a failure after it happens, you *imagine* the failure has happened and explain it in advance. The framing shift — from "could this fail?" to "it DID fail; why?" — is what makes it work: prospective hindsight makes people far more willing and able to name concrete risks they'd otherwise suppress to avoid sounding negative.

## Authentic sources

- **Conn & McLean, *Bulletproof Problem Solving*** (Wiley, 2018) — **Chapter 4** (pp. 117-140). Pre-mortem analysis requires the team to *"work through all implications of even unlikely failure cases before committing to a solution,"* paired with explicit **downside scenario modeling** (long-tailed / black-swan distributions, not assumed-normal). Both are recommended **to counter overoptimism bias** — one of the five core cognitive biases (Ch 4). Direct quote: *"Tools like pre-mortems can help highlight risks to focus on with big decisions where a lot is at stake."*

- **Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem"** (*Harvard Business Review*, Sept 2007) — the origin of the technique. Klein's research: prospective hindsight increases the accuracy and specificity of risk identification by ~30%. **Daniel Kahneman** repeatedly cites the pre-mortem as one of the single most effective debiasing tools available to a team (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011).

## When to use

**Mandatory (recommended) for:**
- Every HITL **Tier C / D** decision per `governance/HITL.md` — irreversible, external-facing, money out, or user-affecting. The pre-mortem IS the content of the regret window.
- `/cla` phase 5 architecture sign-off (before founder approves the spec)
- Pricing changes, public launches, capability deprecation, schema migrations
- Any decision SOP-FOUNDER-005 flags as a **one-way door**

**Recommended for:**
- Sprint plans with a single large dependency
- New channel bets (GTM) before spend commits

## When NOT to use

- **Two-way doors** — cheap, reversible decisions. Just try it; the experiment IS the test. A pre-mortem here is overhead.
- Routine operations with established guards.
- Experiments deliberately designed to fail cheaply (the failure is the point).

**Anti-pattern: pre-mortem theater.** Listing failures and then proceeding unchanged is worse than not doing one — it manufactures false confidence ("we considered the risks"). A pre-mortem must change the decision, add a guard, or be honestly recorded as accepted risk.

## How to apply

### Step 1 — Frame the failure as already-happened
State it in past tense, vividly: *"It's 6 months from now. We shipped the annual-only pricing change. It was a disaster."* Past tense is not optional — it's the mechanism.

### Step 2 — Independent failure-cause generation
Each reviewer (or, solo, each `/muse` lens — `cynic`, `data-pragmatist`, `customer-advocate`) writes WHY it failed, independently, before discussion. Independence prevents anchoring on the first voice.

### Step 3 — Cluster the causes
Group the failure stories (MECE — see `mece-decomposition-check`). Typical clusters: demand wrong, execution slipped, second-order effect, external shock.

### Step 4 — For each top cause: preventable?
For the highest-likelihood / highest-impact causes, choose one:
- **Guard** — a check that catches it early
- **Leading indicator** — a metric that warns before the failure fully lands
- **Kill-switch** — a pre-agreed condition to reverse
- **Modify** — change the decision to remove the cause
- **Accept** — explicitly record as accepted risk (with rationale)

### Step 5 — Decide: proceed-with-guards / modify / kill
The output is not a list — it's a changed (or consciously-confirmed) decision plus the guards/indicators/kill-switches now attached to it.

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — GOOD (pricing experiment)
**Failure framing:** "It's Q3. We moved Plus to annual-only billing to lift LTV. It backfired — signups cratered."
**Independent causes:** (a) annual-only killed the low-commitment on-ramp the ICP needs; (b) competitors stayed monthly and we looked expensive; (c) refund requests spiked, hurting cash + support load.
**Guards attached:** keep monthly as the default on-ramp (modify); leading indicator = signup→trial rate, kill-switch if it drops >15% in week 1; cap the experiment to one cohort.
**Decision:** proceed, but as an A/B on one cohort with the kill-switch — not a full cutover.
**Why good:** the pre-mortem changed the decision (cohort A/B, not cutover) and attached a concrete kill-switch.

### Example 2 — ANTI-PATTERN (pre-mortem theater)
Team lists 6 ways a launch could fail, nods, and ships the exact same plan unchanged with no guards. **Why anti-pattern:** the exercise produced zero change → it was decoration that now provides false cover ("we did a pre-mortem"). **Fix:** each top cause MUST yield a guard, a modification, or an explicitly-recorded accepted risk.

### Example 3 — EDGE CASE (reversible → skip)
**Decision:** "Change the morning-brief send time from 6:00 to 5:45."
**Verdict:** two-way door, near-zero blast radius. Skip the pre-mortem; just change it and watch. Pre-mortems are for one-way doors.

## Anti-claims

- A pre-mortem does NOT predict the actual failure. It widens the set of considered failures so you can guard the plausible ones.
- Imagined failures are NOT calibrated probabilities — don't treat the scariest story as the most likely.
- This is NOT a veto tool. Its output can be "proceed, with these guards," not only "kill."
- A pre-mortem that changes nothing is worse than none — it manufactures false confidence.
